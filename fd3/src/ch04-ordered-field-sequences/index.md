# Ordered Fields and Sequences in an Ordered Field

In \( \nQ \) the numbers \( 2, 3/2, 17/12, 577/408, \dots \) crowd together, and their squares crowd toward \( 2 \); yet Chapter 2 proved that no rational squares to \( 2 \) (@thm-no-rational-square-two). Chapter 5 will make the missing number out of sequences like this one. Before it can, we must say what "crowd together" means when the only yardsticks are rationals, and prove the rules for such sequences without presupposing the number they are aiming at. This chapter does that once, for an **arbitrary ordered field** \( F \), with every tolerance \( \varepsilon \) taken from \( F \) itself. It adds to the inequality rules of Chapter 2 what limits need (halving, fractions, the absolute value and the triangle inequality), places a copy of \( \nN \) and of \( \nQ \) inside every ordered field, and names the Archimedean property, which \( \nQ \) has and an ordered field need not have. Then come limits (uniqueness, boundedness, the limit laws, limits and order) and Cauchy sequences (convergent implies Cauchy, Cauchy implies bounded, sums and products, and the sign of a Cauchy sequence that is not null). Chapter 5 applies all of it with \( F = \nQ \).

The chapter ends at the gap it was written for. The sequence above is Cauchy in \( \nQ \) and has no limit in \( \nQ \), so in \( \nQ \) "Cauchy" does not imply "convergent". If you have met limits in a course on \( \nR \), most statements here will look familiar. What is new is that they are proved in any ordered field, from the axioms alone. Watch where the Archimedean property is needed (each use names it), and note that completeness never appears: no supremum, no monotone convergence theorem and no Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem is used, as Section 4.6 checks.

## Roadmap

- **Working in an Ordered Field.** The standing setup of an arbitrary ordered field \( F \), the fraction rules of a field and the absence of zero divisors, halving a tolerance and the midpoint, comparing reciprocals and multiplying non-negative inequalities, and the absolute value with the triangle and reverse triangle inequalities, ending in the form \( \abs{x - a} < \varepsilon \iff a - \varepsilon < x < a + \varepsilon \) that the rest of the chapter uses.
- **The Rationals inside an Ordered Field; the Archimedean Property.** The elements \( n \cdot 1_F \) and powers in \( F \), which add, multiply and compare as in \( \nN \); the unique embedding \( \iota_F \) of \( \nQ \) into every ordered field, strictly increasing and order-reflecting; the Archimedean property as a property of \( F \), with its reciprocal form \( 1/(n \cdot 1_F) < \varepsilon \); the integer part and the density of the rationals in an Archimedean field; and \( \nQ(t) \), an ordered field that is not Archimedean.
- **Sequences and Limits in an Ordered Field.** Sequences indexed from \( 0 \) and "eventually", the limit with tolerances from \( F \) and its negation, first limits in \( \nQ \) and the divergence of \( ((-1)^n) \) in every ordered field, null and bounded sequences, \( 1/(n + 1) \to 0 \) as a restatement of the Archimedean property, changing finitely many terms, uniqueness of limits, and the boundedness of convergent sequences.
- **The Algebra and Order of Limits.** Null times bounded is null, terms near a non-zero limit stay away from \( 0 \), the limit laws for sums, multiples, products and quotients, and weak inequalities passing to the limit, with the squeeze; no result of the section uses the Archimedean property.
- **Cauchy Sequences.** Terms close to one another instead of to a point, convergent sequences Cauchy and Cauchy sequences bounded, sums and products of Cauchy sequences, the sign theorem (a Cauchy sequence that is not null eventually stays at least some \( c > 0 \) above \( 0 \), or at least \( c \) below it), sign stability under null differences, reciprocals, and a sequence of rationals whose steps shrink to \( 0 \) and which is not Cauchy.
- **A Cauchy Sequence with No Rational Limit.** The Newton sequence for \( \sqrt 2 \) is decreasing with overshoot \( e_n \le 1/(4n) \), Cauchy in \( \nQ \), and convergent to no rational, since a limit \( L \) would satisfy \( L^2 = 2 \); the section then lists what the chapter did not prove (suprema, monotone convergence, Bolzano–Weierstrass), each of which fails in \( \nQ \), and the review exercises return to Chapter 2's classes, to induction, and to the negation laws of *Logic and Proof*, Chapter 4.

## Named moves

- **Split \( \varepsilon \).** To make a sum of \( k \) small terms smaller than \( \varepsilon \), give each term the tolerance \( \varepsilon/k \), and combine the finitely many thresholds by a maximum. It proves the sum law in Section 4.4 and the sum of two Cauchy sequences in Section 4.5; Exercise B3 of Section 4.4 and Exercise C1 of Section 4.5 leave part of it to you.
- **Bound, then shrink.** In a product, replace the factor you cannot control by a bound fixed in advance, then make the other factor small enough to absorb the bound. The bound comes from the whole sequence, before \( \varepsilon \) is named. It proves the product law in Section 4.4 and the product of two Cauchy sequences in Section 4.5.
- **Fix the bad \( \varepsilon \).** "Not null" says: some \( \varepsilon \) is missed beyond every threshold. It hands you one \( \varepsilon \), to keep for the rest of the proof; ask the Cauchy condition for a threshold with \( \varepsilon/2 \) first, and only then take a term beyond it that misses \( \varepsilon \). This is the sign theorem of Section 4.5.
- **Locate the missing limit.** Suppose a limit \( L \) exists, and use the limit laws and uniqueness to find an equation that \( L \) must satisfy; if the field has no solution, there is no limit. In Section 4.6 the equation is \( L^2 = 2 \).
