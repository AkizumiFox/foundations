# Countable and Uncountable Sets

Are there more rational numbers than natural numbers? The rationals are dense, with a third between any two, while the naturals are spread out one unit apart; and yet the answer is no, because the rationals can be listed. Are there more real numbers than rationals? Yes: no list of real numbers, however cleverly it is made, contains them all. This chapter makes "more" exact, with bijections and injections in place of counting, and proves both answers. It then shows that there is no largest size at all: by Cantor's theorem, the set of subsets of any set is strictly bigger than the set itself. A second motivating question comes from school: *why does \( 0.999\ldots = 1 \) need a definition at all?* The answer is in Section 3.3, where a decimal expansion is defined with finite sums only, and \( 0.999\ldots = 1 \) becomes true by that definition. Each section teaches one method of proof on the way: list a set, name the choices a proof makes, run a diagonal, and compare sizes by injections both ways.

On the way the chapter meets a principle that the rules of logic and the working rules for sets do not supply: making infinitely many arbitrary choices at once. The obvious proof that a countable union of countable sets is countable needs a list of every set of the family at the same time, and no rule produces one. The chapter states the missing principle as the axiom of choice, marks the line of each proof that uses it, and shows how often a rule (the least element, a formula) makes it unnecessary. It starts from the finite sets of Section 2.5 and the pigeonhole principle.

## Roadmap

- **Countable sets.** Countable as listable with repetitions allowed (empty, or the image of \( \nN \)), the equivalent forms "an injection into \( \nN \)" and "finite or countably infinite", closure under subsets, images, unions of two sets and finite products, and the countability of \( \nN \times \nN \), \( \nZ \) and \( \nQ \); the method: list a set, by a surjection from \( \nN \) or an injection into \( \nN \).
- **Countable unions and the axiom of choice.** The step in the proof for countable unions that no rule justifies, the axiom of choice that licenses it, the constructions that look like choice and are not, right inverses of surjections, countable unions of countable sets, and the countability of the algebraic numbers; the method: name each choice a proof makes.
- **Diagonal arguments: uncountable sets and Cantor's theorem.** The diagonal argument for 0–1 sequences, opening with the five-string game of Section 1.5, Cantor's theorem that no map from a set onto its power set exists, decimal expansions defined with finite sums, the uncountability of \( \nR \), and the existence of transcendental numbers; the method: the diagonal.
- **Comparing sizes: the Schröder–Bernstein theorem.** Comparing sizes by injections, its exchange with surjections at the price of choice, the Schröder–Bernstein theorem proved without choice, and every interval with more than one point equinumerous with \( \nR \); the method: injections both ways; whether any two sets are comparable is left open.
- **Infinite sets.** Every infinite set contains a countably infinite subset (a stronger use of choice), so \( \nN \) is the smallest infinite size, and a set is infinite exactly when it is equinumerous with a proper subset of itself; the review exercises reach back across this volume and *Logic and Proof*.

## Named moves

Each is a method box in the section named at the end of its line; the later sections use the move again.

- **List it** (@exm-method-list-it). To show a set countable, give a surjection from \( \nN \) or an injection into \( \nN \), whichever is cheaper to check; a list may repeat, and an injection only has to keep different elements apart. (Section 3.1)
- **Name the choice** (@exm-method-name-the-choice). When a proof picks one object for each of infinitely many indices and no rule names it, say at that line that the axiom of choice is used; a least element or a formula is a rule, and needs no choice. (Section 3.2)
- **The diagonal** (@exm-method-diagonal). Given any list, build an object that differs from the \( n \)-th entry at the \( n \)-th place; it is not on the list, and since the list was arbitrary, no list contains everything. (Section 3.3)
- **Injections both ways** (@exm-method-injections-both-ways). To show \( \card{A} = \card{B} \), find an injection \( A \to B \) and an injection \( B \to A \) and invoke the Schröder–Bernstein theorem, instead of hunting for the bijection. (Section 3.4)

## Threads

- **The five strings.** Section 1.5 plants a game: write a string of five symbols not on a given list of five. Section 3.3 opens with the rule that always works (@exm-five-strings-diagonal), and the same rule, run on infinitely long strings, proves that the 0–1 sequences cannot be listed (@thm-binary-sequences-uncountable).
- **Russell to Cantor.** Russell's theorem (Section 1.2) built a set that disagrees with each candidate on the candidate itself; Cantor's theorem (Section 3.3, @thm-cantor) is the same construction, as a theorem about sizes.
- **Choice.** Section 1.6 could not build a right inverse of an arbitrary surjection; Section 3.2 adds the axiom of choice that does (@ax-choice, @thm-surjection-right-inverse), Section 3.5 uses it again (@thm-infinite-countable-subset), and Chapter 6 states its strongest everyday form, Zorn's lemma.
- **Comparability.** Section 3.4 asks whether any two sets are comparable and leaves the question open; Section 6.2 answers it.

## Summary

Countable means listable (@def-countable), in three equivalent forms (@thm-countable-characterizations). Countable sets are closed under subsets, images, finite products and, with the axiom of choice, countable unions (@thm-countable-union), so \( \nZ \), \( \nQ \) and the algebraic numbers are countable. The diagonal shows that the 0–1 sequences, \( \nR \) and every power set are too big to be listed (@thm-binary-sequences-uncountable, @thm-reals-uncountable-decimal, @thm-cantor), so transcendental numbers exist. Sizes are compared by injections, and injections both ways give a bijection, without choice (@thm-schroder-bernstein). Every infinite set contains a copy of \( \nN \), by choice (@thm-infinite-countable-subset), and has a proper subset of its own size (@thm-infinite-iff-proper-subset).

What is open: are any two sets comparable? Chapter 6 answers it. Next, Chapter 4 studies relations, and "same size" returns there as an example.

## Terms

- **algebraic number**: a real root of a non-zero polynomial with integer coefficients (Section 3.2, before @thm-algebraic-countable).
- **at most as big as, strictly smaller**: \( \card{A} \le \card{B} \) if there is an injection \( A \to B \); \( \card{A} < \card{B} \) if moreover there is no bijection (@def-card-le).
- **choice function**: a function \( c \) on the index set of a family with \( c(i) \in A_i \) for every \( i \) (@ax-choice).
- **countable**: empty, or the image of a surjection \( \nN \to A \) (@def-countable).
- **countably infinite**: equinumerous with \( \nN \) (@def-countably-infinite).
- **decimal expansion** of \( x \in [0, 1] \): a digit sequence with \( t_n \le x \le t_n + 10^{-n} \) for every \( n \ge 1 \), where \( t_n \) is the finite sum of the first \( n \) digits (@def-decimal-expansion).
- **digit sequence**: a function from \( \nZ_{>0} \) to \( \{0, 1, \dots, 9\} \) (@def-decimal-expansion).
- **transcendental number**: a real number that is not algebraic (Section 3.3, before @cor-transcendental-exist).
- **uncountable**: not countable (@def-countable).
