# Induction and Recursion

*Logic and Proof*, Chapter 4 took one fact about the natural numbers on trust: induction. Everything else we have used about them came from school: that \( 7 - 3 \) is a natural number, that none lies strictly between \( 3 \) and \( 4 \), and that every non-empty set of them has a least member. This chapter derives all of these from induction, extends them to the integers, and proves that the least-member property, **well-ordering**, is induction in another form. It then turns induction from a principle into a craft: which form fits a claim, where to start, when to argue by contrapositive, by contradiction or from a smallest counterexample, when to prove more than was asked, and how a flawed induction fails at exactly one step. It also proves that a starting value and a rule for the next value really do define a sequence, which is what makes \( \sum \), \( \prod \), \( n! \) and powers definitions rather than abbreviations. The first motivating problem comes from *Logic and Proof*, Chapter 2, which reduced a fraction by halving numerator and denominator for as long as both were even: *what stops the halving from going on for ever?* It proves that there are infinitely many primes, and that \( \sqrt[3]{2} \) is irrational.

## Roadmap

- **The natural numbers and well-ordering.** From induction alone, the arithmetic of \( \nN \) and \( \nZ \), the proof that every non-empty set of natural numbers has a least element, and the theorem that induction and well-ordering are equivalent, with a witness for each hypothesis it needs, and the least element as a tool of proof.
- **Induction, strong induction and divisibility.** Induction from any integer, finding the inductive hypothesis inside the step and choosing the base, the division algorithm, lowest terms and prime factors, and a gallery of broken inductions, each located at one step.
- **Definition by recursion.** The theorem that a starting value and a rule define exactly one sequence, its variants from another base and with two previous values, sums, products and powers defined by it, and inductions that follow a definition's clauses or prove more than was asked.
- **Contrapositive and contradiction in practice.** Choosing among a direct proof, the contrapositive and a contradiction; infinitely many primes; an irrational cube root by lowest terms; the smallest counterexample as a method; existence without a witness.

## Named moves

The first four are method boxes; each section that introduces one cites it from then on.

- **The least element** (@exm-method-least-element). Name a set whose least element would help, show it is a non-empty set of natural numbers, take its least element, and use leastness: an element below it is a contradiction, or leastness says what it must be. (Section 3.1)
- **Finding \( P(n) \) inside \( P(n + 1) \)** (@exm-method-induction-step). Write \( P(n + 1) \) out, split off the part that is \( P(n) \), apply the induction hypothesis to it, close the rest by computation, and choose the base as the first \( n \) from which this works. (Section 3.2)
- **Choosing direct, contrapositive or contradiction** (@exm-method-choosing-the-form). Unwind \( P \), \( Q \) and their negations: go direct when \( P \) gives something to compute with, prove the contrapositive when \( \neg Q \) is the richer hypothesis, and assume the positive statement when the claim is a negation. (Section 3.4)
- **Smallest counterexample** (@exm-method-smallest-counterexample). Suppose the claim fails; well-ordering gives a least failure \( m \); below \( m \) the claim holds, and from that produce a failure smaller than \( m \), or the claim at \( m \) itself. (Section 3.4)
- **Prove more (strengthen the hypothesis).** When the inductive step will not go through, ask what the induction hypothesis would need to say, and prove that stronger statement instead.
- **Recursion, then induction.** Define an object by a recursion, and prove facts about it by an induction that follows the same clauses: the base case uses the starting value, and the step uses the rule.

## Summary

Induction and well-ordering are one principle (@thm-induction-iff-well-ordering). Every integer can be divided by a positive integer with a unique quotient and remainder (@thm-division-algorithm). Every integer at least \( 2 \) has a prime factor (@thm-prime-factor), and there are infinitely many primes (@thm-primes-infinite). A starting value and a rule define exactly one sequence (@thm-recursion), which makes sums, products and powers definitions. The number \( \sqrt[3]{2} \) is irrational (@prp-cube-root-two-irrational), by lowest terms and a smallest counterexample.

What is open: what does it mean for two sets to have the same size, and are \( \nQ \) and \( \nR \) the same size as \( \nN \)? Chapter 4.

## Terms

- **factorial**: \( n! = \prod_{k=1}^{n} k \), with \( 0! = 1 \) (@def-sum-product).
- **greatest element**: an element \( m \in S \) with \( s \le m \) for every \( s \in S \) (Section 3.1, before @thm-well-ordering).
- **least element**: an element \( m \in S \) with \( m \le s \) for every \( s \in S \) (Section 3.1, before @thm-well-ordering).
- **maximum, minimum**: the greatest and the least element of a set (Section 3.3, before @prp-finite-max).
- **power**: \( u^0 = 1 \), \( u^{n+1} = u^n u \) (@def-power).
- **prime factor**: a prime that divides the integer (@def-prime, Section 1.1); every integer \( \ge 2 \) has one (@thm-prime-factor).
- **quotient, remainder**: the \( q \) and \( r \) of \( a = qn + r \), \( 0 \le r < n \) (@thm-division-algorithm).
- **smallest counterexample**: the least integer at which a claim fails, given by well-ordering (@exm-method-smallest-counterexample).
- **sum, product**: \( \sum_{k=m}^{n} a_k \) and \( \prod_{k=m}^{n} a_k \), defined by recursion, empty sum \( 0 \), empty product \( 1 \) (@def-sum-product).
