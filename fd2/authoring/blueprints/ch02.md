# Blueprint: Chapter 7 — Equivalence Relations, Orders, and Induction

**Directory:** `src/ch07-relations-and-induction/` (slug `relations-and-induction`). **Part 0**, main line. Written to the full standard of `authoring/STYLE.md`, including §10–§11, at Part 0 density. The book.yaml entry (must-prove list and its note of 2026-09-28) is binding: **induction itself belongs to Chapter 3** (`@ax-induction`, `@thm-strong-induction`, `@thm-structural-induction` are cited, never re-derived). This chapter keeps well-ordering (proved, not assumed) and its equivalence with induction, the arithmetic of ℕ and ℤ that Chapter 11 was promised, definition by recursion on ℕ, the craft and pitfalls of induction, and relations.

**Level reference.** Velleman §4.3–4.5, §6.1–6.4 (scaffolding), Hammack ch. 10 and §11.2–11.5 (problem level), Halmos ch. 7 (depth), for **coverage only**. The recursion theorem's proof by partial solutions is standard; our statement lets the step depend on \( n \), which Chapter 11's nested intervals need.

**Order of sections — a deliberate change from the stub.** The stub puts relations first and induction last. But "ℤ/n has exactly \( n \) classes" needs the division algorithm, which needs well-ordering; and the orders section is the natural bridge into Chapter 11. So: **ℕ and well-ordering (§01) → induction in practice (§02) → recursion (§03) → properties of relations (§04) → equivalence relations (§05) → orders (§06)**. Every stub item is still here, and no proof points forward within the chapter.

**Purpose.**
- Put ℕ on a footing the rest of the book can cite: from `@ax-induction` and two facts about \( 0 \) and \( n + 1 \), prove the arithmetic of ℕ and ℤ that Chapter 11 uses, prove **well-ordering**, and prove that induction and well-ordering are **equivalent**, given \( 0 \in N \), closure under \( n \mapsto n + 1 \) and the predecessor property, with a witness that each of the three is needed.
- Turn induction from a principle into a craft: where to start, when to strengthen, when to use strong induction or a smallest counterexample, and the ways an induction proof goes wrong, each located at an exact step.
- Prove that recursive definitions define something (`thm-recursion`); define \( \sum \), \( \prod \), \( n! \) and powers.
- Relations: their properties; equivalence relations and partitions (the correspondence); ℤ/n; partial and total orders with upper bounds, greatest and maximal elements, and a first look at least upper bounds, **planted** for Chapter 11.
- The reader leaves able to (i) write a correct induction or smallest-counterexample proof, choosing the form; (ii) find the failing step of a flawed induction; (iii) prove that a relation is an equivalence relation and describe its classes; (iv) tell greatest from maximal, with a witness.

**Signature moves** (`index.md`, "Named moves"):
- **Smallest counterexample.** Suppose the claim fails; well-ordering gives a least failure \( m \); produce a smaller one. (The chapter's main move: the faded triple.)
- **Strengthen the hypothesis.** When the inductive step will not go through, prove more.
- **Recursion, then induction.** Define by a recursion; prove by an induction that follows the same clauses.
- **Classes from a relation, a relation from classes.** Pass between an equivalence relation and its partition.

**Scaffolding (STYLE §11.4, Part 0 density).**
- **Proof ordering:** §01 B2 — the \( (\Leftarrow) \) direction of `thm-induction-iff-well-ordering`, seven lines shuffled: (i) let \( S \subseteq N \) contain \( 0 \) and be closed under \( n \mapsto n + 1 \); (ii) suppose \( S \ne N \); (iii) then \( N \setminus S \) is non-empty, so it has a least element \( m \); (iv) \( m \ne 0 \), since \( 0 \in S \); (v) so \( m - 1 \in N \), by the predecessor property; (vi) \( m - 1 < m \), so \( m - 1 \notin N \setminus S \), that is, \( m - 1 \in S \); (vii) hence \( m = (m - 1) + 1 \in S \), a contradiction; so \( S = N \). Admissible orders: only (i)(ii)(iii)(iv)(v)(vi)(vii). (iv) must sit between (iii) and (v): it needs the \( m \) of (iii), and (v) applies the predecessor property, which needs \( m \ne 0 \); (vi) needs the \( m - 1 \in N \) of (v).

- **`lem-naturals-arithmetic`.** For all \( m, n \in \nN \): (a) \( n \ge 0 \), and if \( n \ne 0 \), then \( n \ge 1 \); (b) \( m + n \in \nN \) and \( mn \in \nN \); (c) if \( n \ne 0 \) (equivalently \( n \ge 1 \), by (a)), then \( n - 1 \in \nN \); (d) if \( m \le n \), then \( n - m \in \nN \); (e) there is no \( k \in \nN \) with \( n < k < n + 1 \); equivalently, \( m < n \) implies \( m + 1 \le n \). (a)–(d) are each an induction whose property is **written out** before the base case; (e) is a direct consequence of (a) and (d):
  - (a): one induction on the property "\( n = 0 \) or \( n \ge 1 \)" (the inductive set \( \{0\} \cup [1, \infty) \) that Chapter 11 also uses). Base: \( n = 0 \). Step: either case gives \( n \ge 0 \), so \( n + 1 \ge 1 \). Both clauses of (a) follow. (a) does not depend on (c).
  - (b): fix \( m \) and induct on \( n \). Sums: base \( m + 0 = m \in \nN \); step \( m + (n + 1) = (m + n) + 1 \in \nN \) by (ℕS). Products: base \( m \cdot 0 = 0 \in \nN \) by (ℕ0); step \( m(n + 1) = mn + m \), by the sum half.
  - (c): property "\( n = 0 \) or \( n - 1 \in \nN \)"; the base is the first disjunct, and the step holds because \( (n + 1) - 1 = n \in \nN \), **without** the induction hypothesis. `exm-predecessor-no-hypothesis` makes this a remark that quotes Chapter 3's sentence (02-induction.md:96, inside `@exm-sum-consecutive-products`: "A proof by induction that never uses its induction hypothesis is either not an induction or is hiding a gap") and refines it: when the step ignores the hypothesis, the proof is still an induction, because the axiom is what carries the property from \( 0 \) to every \( n \), and no case analysis can replace it; so the smell Chapter 3 names is a reason to check the proof, not a verdict on it. Here the check passes: the base case is proved, and the step is a valid implication for every \( n \). (Seam item for STATUS: see "Promises this chapter opens".)
  - (d): induct on \( m \) with the property "for every \( n \in \nN \), \( m \le n \Rightarrow n - m \in \nN \)". Base \( m = 0 \): \( n - 0 = n \in \nN \). Step: if \( m + 1 \le n \), then \( m \le n \), so \( n - m \in \nN \) by the hypothesis; also \( n - m \ge 1 > 0 \), so \( n - m \ne 0 \), and \( n - (m + 1) = (n - m) - 1 \in \nN \) by (c).
  - (e): if \( n < k < n + 1 \), then \( k - n \in \nN \) by (d), and \( k - n \ne 0 \), so \( k - n \ge 1 \) by (a); this contradicts \( k - n < 1 \).

- **Opening frame.** Chapter 3 gave the principle, its strong form (`@thm-strong-induction`) and the first worked proofs (`@exm-sum-consecutive-products`, `@exm-power-inequality`, `@exm-threes-and-fives`); Chapter 5 gave its rhetoric (`@exm-rhetoric-induction`, pointed back to). - **`thm-induction`** (the F2.2 ID, applied under **Labels**; its statement is the linear algebra book's Chapter 0 thm-induction, word for word): let \( n_0 \) be an integer and, for each integer \( n \ge n_0 \), let \( P(n) \) be a statement; if \( P(n_0) \) holds and \( P(n) \Rightarrow P(n + 1) \) for every integer \( n \ge n_0 \), then \( P(n) \) holds for every integer \( n \ge n_0 \). Point back to Chapter 3 §02 "Starting from another number", which does this for \( n_0 \in \nN \); here \( n_0 \) may be any integer. The case \( n_0 = 0 \) is `@ax-induction` itself.
- **`thm-strong-induction-from-base`** (a corollary of Chapter 3's `@thm-strong-induction`, per label-triage; not part of `thm-induction`): let \( n_0 \) be an integer and \( P(n) \) a statement for each integer \( n \ge n_0 \); if, for every integer \( n \ge n_0 \), \( P(k) \) holding for every integer \( k \) with \( n_0 \le k < n \) implies \( P(n) \), then \( P(n) \) holds for every integer \( n \ge n_0 \). Proof, one sentence: put \( Q(k) \coloneqq P(n_0 + k) \) for \( k \in \nN \) and apply `@thm-strong-induction` to \( Q \); the hypothesis carries over because, for an integer \( j \), \( n_0 \le n_0 + j < n_0 + k \) exactly when \( j \in \nN \) and \( j < k \) (the \( n \ge 0 \) clause of `prp-integers-closed`). When \( n_0 \in \nN \), it is equivalent to the "if \( n \ge n_0 \), then …" guard of Chapter 3's `@exm-threes-and-fives` (cross-reference).
- **The smallest counterexample** (named move; an `::: {.algorithm}` block): suppose the claim fails; by `thm-well-ordering` there is a least failure \( m \); use the claim for every \( k < m \) to produce a contradiction. One paragraph: it is strong induction with an RAA box drawn around it; the two forms are interchangeable by `thm-well-ordering` and `@thm-strong-induction`.
- **Results.**
  - `def-divides` (\( a \mid b \iff \exists k \in \nZ\ b = ka \)); `def-prime` (an integer \( p \ge 2 \) whose only positive divisors are \( 1 \) and \( p \)).
  - `thm-division-algorithm` (*Try it first*): for \( a \in \nZ \) and \( n \in \nZ_{>0} \) there are **unique** \( q, r \in \nZ \) with \( a = qn + r \) and \( 0 \le r < n \). (For \( n = 0 \) no \( r \) has \( 0 \le r < 0 \); say so in one line.) Existence: \( R \coloneqq \{ a - qn : q \in \nZ \} \cap \{ x \in \nZ : x \ge 0 \} \) is non-empty (\( q = -\abs{a} \) gives \( a + \abs{a} n \ge a + \abs{a} \ge 0 \), using \( n \ge 1 \) and \( \abs{a} \ge 0 \)), so it has a least element \( r \) (`cor-well-ordering-integers`, lower bound \( 0 \)); if \( r \ge n \), then \( r - n \in R \) is smaller. Uniqueness: if \( r \ne r' \), then \( r - r' = kn \) with \( k \) a nonzero integer, so \( \abs{k} \) is a positive integer, hence \( \abs{k} \in \nZ_{>0} \), that is \( \abs{k} \ge 1 \) (`prp-integers-closed`; directly: \( k = \pm m \) with \( m \in \nN \) by the definition of ℤ, so \( \abs{k} = m \), and \( m \ne 0 \) gives \( m \ge 1 \) by `lem-naturals-arithmetic` (a)); then \( \abs{r - r'} = \abs{k} n \ge n \), contradicting \( \abs{r - r'} < n \). So \( r = r' \); then \( qn = q'n \), and since \( n \ne 0 \), \( q = q' \). Aftermath: with \( n = 2 \), every integer is even or odd, and not both — the school fact of Chapters 1–2, now proved.
  - `lem-lowest-terms` (faded triple, full version; **pays (L)**): every rational number can be written \( p/q \) with \( p \in \nZ \), \( q \in \nZ_{>0} \), and \( p, q \) not both even. Proof in smallest-counterexample form, each step named as in the algorithm block. *Suppose it fails:* fix \( r \in \nQ \) and suppose \( r \) has no representation \( p/q \) with \( p \in \nZ \), \( q \in \nZ_{>0} \) and \( p, q \) not both even. Let \( F \coloneqq \{ q \in \nZ_{>0} : r = p/q \text{ for some } p \in \nZ \} \); under the supposition every element of \( F \) is the denominator of a failing representation. *Take the least failure:* \( F \) is non-empty by the definition of ℚ and \( F \subseteq \nZ_{>0} \subseteq \nN \), so \( F \) has a least element \( m \) (`thm-well-ordering`); write \( r = p/m \). *Produce a smaller failure:* that representation fails, so \( p = 2p' \) and \( m = 2m' \) with \( p', m' \in \nZ \); \( m' = m/2 > 0 \), so \( m' \in \nZ_{>0} \) (`prp-integers-closed`), \( r = p'/m' \), and \( m' < m \). So \( m' \in F \) is a smaller failure, contradicting the choice of \( m \). Remark, pointing back: this is the fact (L) that Chapter 2 took on trust in the proof of `@thm-sqrt2-irrational`. Full lowest terms (no common divisor \( d \ge 2 \)) goes the same way; that is C2.
  - `thm-prime-factor` (used in A1(b); compare B2): every integer \( n \ge 2 \) has a prime factor. Strong induction from base \( 2 \) (`thm-strong-induction-from-base`): if \( n \) is not prime, it has a positive divisor \( a \notin \{1, n\} \) (`def-prime`), say \( n = ka \) with \( k \in \nZ \); since \( n > 0 \) and \( a > 0 \), \( k > 0 \), so \( k, a \in \nZ_{>0} = \{ m \in \nN : m \ge 1 \} \) (`prp-integers-closed`), hence \( a \le ka = n \) and \( a \ge 1 \); with \( a \ne 1, n \) this gives \( 1 < a < n \), so \( 2 \le a < n \) by the discreteness clause of `prp-integers-closed` (\( 1 < a \) gives \( 1 + 1 \le a \)); \( a \) has a prime factor by hypothesis, and it divides \( n \) (transitivity of \( \mid \), one line inline).

## 03-recursion.md — Definition by Recursion

- **Hook (a goal theorem first).** "Let \( u^0 = 1 \) and \( u^{n+1} = u^n u \)": such definitions are familiar from school. But a definition must define **something**. Induction proves facts about a function that already exists; here the function is what we want to obtain. Chapter 3 met the same issue for formulas (`@thm-structural-recursion`, pointed back to); this is its version for ℕ.
- **`thm-recursion`** (key proof). Let \( X \) be a set, \( a \in X \), and \( g \colon \nN \times X \to X \). Then there is **exactly one** function \( f \colon \nN \to X \) with \( f(0) = a \) and \( f(n + 1) = g(n, f(n)) \) for every \( n \in \nN \). Existence and uniqueness both depend on the base \( f(0) = a \). Idea with a **Step roadmap**: ① uniqueness, by induction; ② **Claim**: for every \( n \in \nN \) there is exactly one function \( f_n = (G_n, X) \colon \{0, \dots, n\} \to X \) (a *partial solution*) satisfying both equations wherever they make sense (the base at \( 0 \); the step at \( k \) with \( k + 1 \le n \)) — induction on \( n \): \( G_0 = \{(0, a)\} \), \( G_{n+1} = G_n \cup \{ (n + 1, g(n, f_n(n))) \} \) and \( f_{n+1} \coloneqq (G_{n+1}, X) \); uniqueness because the restriction (Chapter 6, Section 6.4, in prose: restriction has no label) of any such function to \( \{0, \dots, n\} \) is \( f_n \); ③ let \( \mathcal{S} \coloneqq \{ G \in \powerset{\nN \times X} : G \text{ is the graph of a partial solution} \} \) (`@def-set-builder`, `@def-power-set`) and \( f \coloneqq (\bigcup \mathcal{S}, X) \) (`@def-family-union-intersection`, the \( \bigcup \mathcal{S} \) form), so no family \( n \mapsto f_n \) has to be formed first; check the clauses of `@def-function` on \( \bigcup \mathcal{S} \): (Fn1) because \( n \) lies in the domain of \( G_n \in \mathcal{S} \), (Fn2) because the graphs agree where they overlap (\( G_m = G_n \cap (\{0, \dots, m\} \times X) \) for \( m \le n \), by the Claim's uniqueness); then check the equations. Chapter 6 supplies functions as a graph with a codomain (`@def-function`), restriction through the graph, unions (`@def-family-union-intersection`) and `@prp-function-equality`. *Before reading on* prompt. Remark: the step may depend on \( n \); Chapter 11 builds nested intervals \( I_{n+1} \) from \( I_n \) and the \( (n{+}1) \)-st listed number in exactly this way, with no choice involved (prose pointer: the promise).
- **`cor-recursion-from-base`:** for an integer \( n_0 \), \( a \in X \) and \( g \) defined on \( \{ n \in \nZ : n \ge n_0 \} \times X \) with values in \( X \), there is exactly one \( f \) on \( \{ n \in \nZ : n \ge n_0 \} \) with \( f(n_0) = a \) and \( f(n + 1) = g(n, f(n)) \) for \( n \ge n_0 \). Proof: shift via \( k = n - n_0 \), the same move as `thm-induction`, and apply `thm-recursion`. It covers sequences indexed from \( 1 \) (Chapter 6's \( (a_n)_{n \ge 1} \), Chapter 11's intervals if listed from \( 1 \)).
- **`cor-two-step-recursion`:** given \( a, b \in X \) and \( h \colon \nN \times X \times X \to X \), there is exactly one \( f \colon \nN \to X \) with \( f(0) = a \), \( f(1) = b \), \( f(n + 2) = h(n, f(n), f(n + 1)) \) for \( n \in \nN \). Proof: `thm-recursion` applied to the pairs \( (f(n), f(n + 1)) \in X \times X \).
- **Definitions by recursion.** `def-sum-product`: for a sequence \( (a_k)_{k \ge 1} \) of reals, \( \sum_{k=1}^{0} a_k \coloneqq 0 \), \( \sum_{k=1}^{n+1} a_k \coloneqq \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k + a_{n+1} \) (`thm-recursion` with \( X = \nR \), base \( 0 \), \( g(n, x) = x + a_{n+1} \)); \( \prod_{k=1}^{0} a_k \coloneqq 1 \), \( \prod_{k=1}^{n+1} a_k \coloneqq (\prod_{k=1}^{n} a_k) \, a_{n+1} \) (base \( 1 \), \( g(n, x) = x a_{n+1} \)); \( n! \coloneqq \prod_{k=1}^{n} k \), so \( 0! = 1 \) is the empty product. General bounds (same label): for integers \( m \le n + 1 \) and reals \( a_k \) given for every integer \( k \ge m \), \( \sum_{k=m}^{n} a_k \coloneqq \sum_{i=1}^{n-m+1} a_{m-1+i} \), and \( \prod_{k=m}^{n} a_k \) in the same way (\( n - m + 1 \in \nN \) by the \( n \ge 0 \) clause of `prp-integers-closed`); when \( m = n + 1 \) the sum is empty, so \( 0 \) (the product \( 1 \)). Defined this way, rather than through `cor-recursion-from-base`, the index shift comes free. Finite lists: \( \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k \) and \( \prod_{k=1}^{n} a_k \) depend only on \( a_1, \dots, a_n \) (induction on \( n \)), so a finite list \( a_1, \dots, a_N \) is summed and multiplied by extending it arbitrarily, for example by \( a_k \coloneqq 0 \) for \( k > N \), and the sums and products up to \( N \) do not depend on the extension; for the general bounds the same holds with \( a_m, \dots, a_n \). Note (shifting the index): \( \sum_{k=m}^{n} a_k = \sum_{k=m+p}^{n+p} a_{k-p} \) for every integer \( p \), because both sides unfold to the same \( \sum_{i=1}^{n-m+1} a_{m-1+i} \); this is what Chapter 11's telescoping uses, with \( p = 1 \) turning \( \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} y^{j+1} \cdots \) into \( \sum_{j=1}^{k} y^{j} \cdots \). Splitting off the last term: \( \sum_{k=m}^{n+1} a_k = \sum_{k=m}^{n} a_k + a_{n+1} \) for \( m \le n + 1 \) (unfold, then the recursive clause). `def-power`: \( u^0 \coloneqq 1 \), \( u^{n+1} \coloneqq u^n u \) for \( n \in \nN \) (a direct instance: \( X = \nR \), \( a = 1 \), \( g(n, x) = xu \)). Examples: \( a_0 = 2 \), \( a_{n+1} = (a_n + 2/a_n)/2 \), approximants to \( \sqrt2 \) (only the definition and the values \( 2, 3/2, 17/12 \); take \( X = (0, \infty) \) and check, **before** the definition, that \( g(n, x) = (x + 2/x)/2 \) maps \( X \) into \( X \) (half a sum of positives), so no induction on \( a_n > 0 \) is needed; convergence is Chapter 12, prose pointer); a two-step example.
- **Results.** `prp-power-laws` ((a) \( u^{m+n} = u^m u^n \), (b) \( (u^m)^n = u^{mn} \), (c) \( (uv)^n = u^n v^n \) for reals \( u, v \) and \( m, n \in \nN \); (d) if \( u \in \nZ \), then \( u^n \in \nZ \), and if \( u \in \nN \), then \( u^n \in \nN \), for every \( n \in \nN \); induction on \( n \) from \( 0 \), the first law in full; (d) by \( u^0 = 1 \) and \( u^{n+1} = u^n u \) with `prp-integers-closed`, or `lem-naturals-arithmetic` (b) for ℕ). `prp-sum-first-n` (\( \sum_{k=1}^n k = n(n+1)/2 \) for \( n \in \nN \); true at \( n = 0 \) by the empty sum). `prp-geometric-sum` (\( \sum_{k=1}^{n} r^{k-1} = (1 - r^n)/(1 - r) \) for \( r \ne 1 \) and \( n \in \nN \)). `thm-bernoulli` (\( (1 + x)^n \ge 1 + nx \) for real \( x \ge -1 \) and \( n \in \nN \); base \( 1 \ge 1 \)): the step multiplies by \( 1 + x \ge 0 \), and **this is where \( x \ge -1 \) is used**; drop it and the statement fails (\( x = -4 \), \( n = 3 \): \( -27 < -11 \)). `prp-finite-max` (**pays, for lists of reals,** Chapter 11's promise, which is for non-empty finite subsets of an ordered field; prose note in the text: the proof uses only that \( \le \) is total, so it works verbatim in any ordered field, and the step from a finite set to a list \( x_1, \dots, x_n \) is Chapter 8's definition of a finite set, a prose pointer): for every integer \( n \ge 1 \) and reals \( x_1, \dots, x_n \), the set \( \{x_1, \dots, x_n\} \) has a maximum and a minimum (the empty set, \( n = 0 \), has neither); induction on \( n \) from base \( 1 \) (`thm-induction`), with the trivial base \( \{x_1\} \) and step \( \max\{M, x_{n+1}\} \).
- **`exm-strengthen-hypothesis`:** for \( n \ge 1 \), \( \sum_{k=1}^n 1/k^2 < 2 \) does not carry its own step; \( \sum_{k=1}^n 1/k^2 \le 2 - 1/n \) does (base \( n = 1 \): \( 1 \le 1 \); the step reduces to \( 1/(n+1)^2 \le 1/(n(n+1)) \)). The move: prove more.
- **Warning.** A recursion needs its starting values: \( a_{n+2} = a_{n+1} + a_n \) alone defines nothing unique. A recursive "definition" whose step leaves \( X \) is not one: \( a_0 = 2 \), \( a_{n+1} = 1/(a_n - 1) \) gives \( a_1 = 1 \) and then division by \( 0 \) (the hypothesis \( g \colon \nN \times X \to X \) fails).

- **Hook (the standard example, generalized).** \( = \), \( \le \), \( \subseteq \), \( \mid \), "same remainder on division by \( 3 \)": what do they share, and where do they differ?

## 05-equivalence-relations.md — Equivalence Relations and Partitions

- **Hook (a naive notion that fails, shown failing).** "On a clock, \( 9 + 5 = 2 \)." Arithmetic on numbers? No: arithmetic on classes of numbers, here the classes of ℤ/12. And we must check that the answer does not depend on which number represents a class.
- **`def-equivalence-relation`** (major for Part 0: the full explain template; slogan: *an equivalence relation declares things the same in a way that behaves like equality*), `def-equivalence-class` (\( [a] = \{ x \in X : x \sim a \} \)), `def-partition` (a set of **non-empty**, pairwise **disjoint** subsets of \( X \) whose union is \( X \)), `def-quotient-set` (\( X/{\sim} \), and the quotient map \( q(a) = [a] \), which is surjective).

## 06-orders.md — Partial Orders, Maximal and Greatest Elements

- **Hook.** "Largest" is clear for numbers. For sets under \( \subseteq \) or numbers under divisibility, two things can be incomparable, and "largest" splits into two notions. This section plants the vocabulary Chapter 11 needs for the supremum.
- **Definitions.** `def-partial-order` (reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive; **total** if any two elements are comparable; the strict part \( \prec \)). `exm-subset-order-not-total`: \( (\powerset{X}, \subseteq) \) is a partial order (pointing back to `exm-relation-table`); it is total iff \( X \) has at most one element: if \( a \ne b \) in \( X \), then \( \{a\} \) and \( \{b\} \) are incomparable (for \( X = \{1, 2\} \): \( \{1\} \), \( \{2\} \)); if \( X \) has at most one element, then \( \powerset{X} \subseteq \{\emptyset, X\} \), a chain. Contrast \( (\nN, \le) \), which is total. `def-order-bounds` (upper and lower bounds of \( A \subseteq X \); bounded above and below). `def-greatest-element` (\( g \in A \) with \( a \preceq g \) for every \( a \in A \)) and `def-maximal-element` (\( m \in A \) with no \( a \in A \) such that \( m \prec a \)), both in symbols: the difference is **where the \( \neg \) sits** — greatest is \( \forall a \in A\ (a \preceq g) \), maximal is \( \neg\exists a \in A\ (m \prec a) \). `def-order-supremum`: a least upper bound of \( A \) in \( X \) (an upper bound that is \( \preceq \) every upper bound), written \( \sup A \); dually an infimum \( \inf A \), a greatest lower bound (a lower bound that every lower bound is \( \preceq \)); each is unique when it exists, by antisymmetry. `def-well-ordered-set`: a total order in which every non-empty subset has a least element; \( (\nN, \le) \) is one by `thm-well-ordering`, and \( (\nZ, \le) \) is not.
- **Results.** `prp-greatest-unique`: a greatest element of \( A \), if one exists, is unique, and dually so is a least element (antisymmetry; so "**the** greatest element" and "**the** least element"). `prp-greatest-maximal`: a greatest element is maximal, and in a **total** order a maximal element is greatest. Unlabelled sentence after it: the proof of `prp-finite-max` (§03) works verbatim in any totally ordered set, since it uses only that any two elements are comparable; in particular in any ordered field (the source for Chapter 11's reading, without a forward reference inside this chapter). The converse fails in general: `exm-maximal-not-greatest`: \( \{2, 3, 4, 5, 6\} \) under \( \mid \) has maximal elements \( 4, 5, 6 \) and no greatest; \( \{\{1\}, \{2\}\} \) under \( \subseteq \).
- **Least upper bounds, a first look.** In \( (\powerset{X}, \subseteq) \) the supremum of \( \{A, B\} \) is \( A \cup B \) (prove both halves: an upper bound, and below every upper bound), and the supremum of any family is its union. `exm-divisibility-lcm`: in \( (\nN, \mid) \), for \( a, b \in \nZ_{>0} \), \( \sup\{a, b\} \) is the least common multiple: the least positive common multiple exists by `thm-well-ordering` (\( ab \) is one), and it divides every integer common multiple \( c \in \nZ \) by `thm-division-algorithm` (the remainder of \( c \) on division by it is a smaller non-negative common multiple, hence \( 0 \); the argument works unchanged for negative \( c \)). If \( a = 0 \) or \( b = 0 \), then \( \sup\{a, b\} = 0 \), the greatest element of \( (\nN, \mid) \) (§04). Explicit instance: \( \sup\{2, 3\} = 6 \), so an integer divisible by \( 2 \) and by \( 3 \) is divisible by \( 6 \) (this pays Chapter 6 §02; §02 B3 gives a direct proof). In \( (\nQ, \le) \): \( \{ x \in \nQ : x^2 < 2 \} \) is bounded above and has **no** least upper bound in ℚ — stated only, and sent to Chapter 11 (prose pointer: Chapter 11 proves it and makes the existence of suprema its axiom). A supremum need not belong to the set (\( A \cup B \notin \{A, B\} \) unless one contains the other).
- **Warning.** "Maximal" is not "greatest" (the witnesses above); and a set may have no maximal element at all (\( \nN \) under \( \le \)) or many. The same set can behave differently under another order: under \( \mid \), ℕ has a greatest element, \( 0 \).
