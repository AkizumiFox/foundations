**Purpose.**
- Make "same size" exact for infinite sets (bijections), and prove the facts about finite sets that "same size" rests on (pigeonhole).
- Countable sets: the definition **Chapter 11 already uses** (listable with repetitions: empty, or the image of ℕ), its equivalent forms, and the closure properties (subsets, images, finite unions, products, countable unions).
- **State the axiom of choice** (`ax-choice`) on the main line, at its first genuine use (countable unions), and show exactly which step needs it and which look-alike steps do not.
- Diagonal arguments: \( \{0, 1\}^{\nN} \), \( \powerset{X} \) (Cantor), ℝ via decimals (the school facts about decimals stated, the \( 0.4999\ldots = 0.5 \) issue handled by the choice of digits).
- Compare sizes with injections; prove Schröder–Bernstein; show every infinite set contains a countably infinite one (choice again) and is equinumerous with a proper subset.
- Close Part 0 with **Gate 0**: a two-hour, closed-book, six-problem examination with a marking scheme per problem, a pass line and a remediation map.
- The reader leaves able to (i) prove a set countable by a surjection from ℕ or an injection into ℕ, choosing the cheaper; (ii) run a diagonal argument and say what makes the diagonal element new; (iii) point to the line of a proof that uses choice, and say when choice is avoidable.

**Signature moves** (`index.md`, "Named moves"):
- **List it.** To show \( A \) countable, give a surjection \( \nN \to A \) (or an injection \( A \to \nN \)); repetitions are free.
- **The diagonal.** Given any list, build an object that differs from the \( n \)-th entry at the \( n \)-th place. (Faded triple.)
- **Injections both ways.** To show \( \card{A} = \card{B} \), find an injection each way and invoke Schröder–Bernstein; do not hunt for the bijection.
- **Name the choice.** When a proof picks one object for each of infinitely many indices with no rule, say that `ax-choice` is used, at that line.

## index.md

- **Title:** Cardinality: Countable and Uncountable Sets.
- **Story.** Are there more rational numbers than natural numbers? The rationals are dense and the naturals are spread out, and yet the answer is no: they can be listed. Are there more reals than rationals? Yes, and no list, however clever, contains them all. This chapter makes "more" exact, proves both answers, and shows that there is no largest size at all (Cantor). On the way it meets a principle that ordinary logic does not supply: making infinitely many arbitrary choices at once. The chapter states it as the axiom of choice and shows the one step that needs it. Motivating questions (curriculum Stage 0 (f)): *are there more rationals or more reals?* and *why does \( 0.999\ldots = 1 \) need a definition at all?* (answered in §04 by `def-decimal-expansion`). ## 01-finite-sets.md — Equinumerous Sets and Finite Sets

- **Hook (an everyday word made exact).** Two piles of coins have the same number when we can pair them off, before we can count. Pairing is a bijection.
- **Definitions.** `def-equinumerous` (major for Part 0: full template; slogan *same size means there is a pairing*): \( \card{A} = \card{B} \) iff there is a bijection \( A \to B \). `prp-equinumerous-properties`: reflexive (\( \id \)), symmetric (`@thm-bijection-iff-inverse`), transitive (`@thm-composition-injective-surjective`). **Say why this is not called an equivalence relation**: there is no set of all sets for it to live on (`@thm-russell`, pointing back); it behaves like one. `def-finite`: for \( n \in \nN \) let \( \{1, \dots, n\} \coloneqq \{ k \in \nN : 1 \le k \le n \} \), so \( \{1, \dots, 0\} = \emptyset \); \( A \) is finite with \( n \) elements if \( \card{A} = \card{\{1, \dots, n\}} \) for some \( n \in \nN \). In particular \( \emptyset \) is finite with \( 0 \) elements (the empty function is a bijection \( \emptyset \to \emptyset \), `@prp-empty-function` (a)). `def-infinite`: not finite.
- **`thm-pigeonhole`** (self-explanation prompt): for \( m, n \in \nN \) with \( m > n \) there is no injection \( \{1, \dots, m\} \to \{1, \dots, n\} \). Induction on \( n \) (`@ax-induction`, pointed back to), with the property **written out** before the base case, \( m \) quantified inside it (the hypothesis is used at \( m - 1 \)): \( P(n) \): "for every \( m \in \nN \) with \( m > n \) there is no injection \( \{1, \dots, m\} \to \{1, \dots, n\} \)". Base \( P(0) \): \( m > 0 \) gives \( 1 \in \{1, \dots, m\} \), so the domain is non-empty while \( \{1, \dots, 0\} = \emptyset \); by `@prp-empty-function` (b) there is no function at all, so no injection. Step: assume \( P(n) \), and let \( f \colon \{1, \dots, m\} \to \{1, \dots, n + 1\} \) be injective with \( m > n + 1 \). Case 1: \( n + 1 \) is not a value; then \( f \) maps into \( \{1, \dots, n\} \) with \( m > n \), contradicting \( P(n) \). Case 2: \( f(j) = n + 1 \) for exactly one \( j \); define \( g \) on \( \{1, \dots, m - 1\} \) by \( g(i) = f(i) \) for \( i \ne j \) and \( g(j) = f(m) \) when \( j \ne m \) (when \( j = m \), \( g \) is the restriction of \( f \)); \( g \) is an injection into \( \{1, \dots, n\} \). Here \( m - 1 \in \nN \) because \( m \ne 0 \) (`@lem-naturals-arithmetic` (c)), and \( m - 1 > n \) follows from \( m > n + 1 \) by order arithmetic (not from the lemma); so \( P(n) \) at \( m - 1 \) gives the contradiction. The construction of \( g \) is the whole proof: write both cases. Illustration only (not the base case): for \( n = 1 \), two points share the value \( 1 \).
- **Corollaries.** `cor-pigeonhole-finite-sets` (**pays** Chapter 6 §04's pointer): let \( A \) have \( m \) elements and \( B \) have \( n \). (a) If \( m > n \), there is no injection \( A \to B \): compose with the bijections of `def-finite` (`@thm-composition-injective-surjective`) and apply `thm-pigeonhole`. (b) If \( m < n \), there is no surjection \( A \to B \): given a surjection \( g \) and a bijection \( \varphi \colon \{1, \dots, m\} \to A \), set \( h(b) \coloneqq \varphi(\min\{ i : g(\varphi(i)) = b \}) \) (`@thm-well-ordering`; the set is non-empty because \( g \) is onto) — a **rule**, so no choice is used (say so; §03's contrast with `thm-surjection-right-inverse`); \( g \circ h = \id_B \), so \( h \) is injective (`@thm-left-inverse-iff-injective` (a)), contradicting (a). `cor-finite-size-unique`: if \( A \) has \( m \) elements and \( n \) elements, then \( m = n \) — so "the number of elements" is well defined (this is the well-definedness step of the explain template). `prp-disjoint-union-count`: disjoint finite sets with \( m \) and \( n \) elements have a union with \( m + n \) elements (shift \( \{1, \dots, n\} \) to \( \{m + 1, \dots, m + n\} \); it uses nothing else from this section, so it comes first). `prp-subset-finite`: a subset of a set with \( n \) elements is finite with at most \( n \) elements, and fewer if it is proper. Induction on \( n \) (`@ax-induction`), the property **written out**: \( P(n) \): \"for every set \( A \) with \( n \) elements and every \( S \subseteq A \), \( S \) is finite with some \( k \le n \) elements, and \( k < n \) if \( S \ne A \)\". Base \( P(0) \): \( A = \emptyset \), so \( S = \emptyset = A \), with \( 0 \) elements. Step: assume \( P(n) \); let \( A \) have \( n + 1 \) elements, \( S \subseteq A \), take a bijection \( \varphi \colon \{1, \dots, n + 1\} \to A \) and put \( A' \coloneqq \varphi(\{1, \dots, n\}) \); \( A' \) has \( n \) elements because \( \varphi \) restricts to a bijection \( \{1, \dots, n\} \to A' \), and \( A = A' \cup \{\varphi(n + 1)\} \) with \( \varphi(n + 1) \notin A' \) (injectivity). Case \( \varphi(n + 1) \notin S \): \( S \subseteq A' \), so \( P(n) \) gives \( k \le n < n + 1 \) elements, and \( S \ne A \) since \( \varphi(n + 1) \in A \setminus S \), as required. Case \( \varphi(n + 1) \in S \): \( S = (S \cap A') \cup \{\varphi(n + 1)\} \) is a disjoint union; \( P(n) \) gives \( S \cap A' \) some \( j \le n \) elements, so \( S \) has \( j + 1 \le n + 1 \) elements (`prp-disjoint-union-count`); if \( S \ne A \), then \( S \cap A' \ne A' \) (otherwise \( S = A' \cup \{\varphi(n + 1)\} = A \)), so \( j < n \) and \( j + 1 < n + 1 \). One sentence in the text: the step deliberately peels off \( \varphi(n + 1) \) by restricting \( \varphi \) rather than citing `cor-remove-point`, because that corollary's proof uses this proposition. `cor-remove-point`: if \( A \) has \( n + 1 \) elements (\( n \in \nN \)) and \( a \in A \), then \( A \setminus \{a\} \) has \( n \) elements. Proof: \( A \setminus \{a\} \) is a proper subset of \( A \), so it is finite with some \( k \) elements, \( k < n + 1 \) (`prp-subset-finite`); \( A = (A \setminus \{a\}) \cup \{a\} \) is a disjoint union and \( \{a\} \) has \( 1 \) element, so \( A \) has \( k + 1 \) elements (`prp-disjoint-union-count`); `cor-finite-size-unique` gives \( k + 1 = n + 1 \), so \( k = n \). (Alternatively, directly: take a bijection \( \varphi \colon \{1, \dots, n + 1\} \to A \), compose with the transposition of \( \varphi^{-1}(a) \) and \( n + 1 \) so that \( \varphi(n + 1) = a \), and restrict to \( \{1, \dots, n\} \).) Every induction on the number of elements below — `prp-power-set-count`, the finite case of `ax-choice` (§03), §03 C3 — starts its step from an **arbitrary** set \( A \) with \( n + 1 \) elements, picks \( a \in A \) (\( A \ne \emptyset \), as \( n + 1 \ge 1 \)) and applies the hypothesis to \( A \setminus \{a\} \) by `cor-remove-point`; it never presents the set as \( X \cup \{a\} \) from the outset. `prp-power-set-count` (**pays** Chapter 6's pointers): a set with \( n \) elements has \( 2^n \) subsets (induction from \( n = 0 \): \( \powerset{\emptyset} = \{\emptyset\} \) has \( 1 = 2^0 \) element; step: let \( A \) have \( n + 1 \) elements, pick \( a \in A \), and put \( X \coloneqq A \setminus \{a\} \), which has \( n \) elements by `cor-remove-point`, so \( \powerset{X} \) has \( 2^n \) elements by the hypothesis; \( \powerset{A} \) is the disjoint union of \( \powerset{X} \) (the subsets without \( a \)) and \( \{ S \cup \{a\} : S \subseteq X \} \) (those with \( a \)), and \( S \mapsto S \cup \{a\} \) is a bijection from \( \powerset{X} \) onto the second set (inverse \( T \mapsto T \setminus \{a\} \)), so `prp-disjoint-union-count` gives \( 2^n + 2^n = 2^{n+1} \) elements, `@def-power`). One sentence after it: via \( A \mapsto \ind_A \) (Chapter 6), the same count is the number of functions \( X \to \{0, 1\} \). `thm-finite-no-proper-copy`: a finite set is not equinumerous with any proper subset (from `prp-subset-finite` and `cor-finite-size-unique`).

## 02-countable-sets.md — Countable Sets

- **Hook.** Can we list the rationals as \( q_0, q_1, q_2, \dots \) so that every rational appears? Density makes it look impossible: between any two there is a third. And yet.
- **`def-countable`** (major for Part 0: full template; slogan *countable means listable, repetitions allowed*): \( A \) is **countable** if \( A = \emptyset \) or there is a surjection \( f \colon \nN \to A \) — that is, \( A = \{ f(0), f(1), f(2), \dots \} \): the elements of \( A \) can be listed as \( x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots \), repetitions allowed. This is the definition Chapter 11 already quoted: match its wording (the promise, as re-quoted in the table above). `def-countably-infinite`: \( \card{A} = \card{\nN} \). **Uncountable** = not countable. Why repetitions are allowed: finite sets are then countable with no special case, and closure proofs are shorter.
- **`lem-subsets-of-naturals`:** every subset \( S \subseteq \nN \) is finite or countably infinite. If \( S \) is infinite, list it in increasing order: \( s_0 \coloneqq \min S \), \( s_{n+1} \coloneqq \min\{ s \in S : s > s_n \} \) — a definition by `@thm-recursion` (base \( 0 \), with \( g(n, x) \coloneqq \min\{ s \in S : s > x \} \) on \( X = \nN \)); the minimum exists by `@thm-well-ordering`, the set being non-empty for every \( x \in \nN \) because \( S \cap \{0, \dots, x\} \) is finite (\( \{0, \dots, x\} \) has \( x + 1 \) elements via \( k \mapsto k + 1 \); `prp-subset-finite`) while \( S \) is not. Then \( n \mapsto s_n \) is strictly increasing, hence injective, and \( s_n \ge n \) by induction from \( s_0 \ge 0 \). It is onto \( S \): for \( t \in S \), take the least \( n \) with \( s_n \ge t \) (it exists since \( n = t \) qualifies); if \( n = 0 \), \( s_0 = \min S \le t \), so \( s_0 = t \); otherwise \( s_{n-1} < t \le s_n \) and \( s_n = \min\{ s \in S : s > s_{n-1} \} \le t \), so \( s_n = t \). So \( n \mapsto s_n \) is a bijection \( \nN \to S \).
- **`thm-countable-characterizations`:** for any set \( A \), TFAE: (i) \( A \) is countable; (ii) there is an injection \( A \to \nN \); (iii) \( A \) is finite or countably infinite. (i) \( \Rightarrow \) (ii): for a surjection \( g \colon \nN \to A \), \( h(a) \coloneqq \min g^{-1}(\{a\}) \) (`@thm-well-ordering`; the preimage is non-empty since \( g \) is onto) — **no choice is needed, because ℕ is well-ordered**: say so, and contrast with §03. (ii) \( \Rightarrow \) (iii): \( A \) is equinumerous with \( h(A) \subseteq \nN \); apply `lem-subsets-of-naturals`. (iii) \( \Rightarrow \) (i): \( A = \emptyset \); or, for \( A \) finite with \( n \ge 1 \) elements, a bijection \( \varphi \colon \{1, \dots, n\} \to A \) extended to ℕ by the value \( \varphi(1) \) at \( 0 \) and at every \( k > n \); or a bijection \( \nN \to A \). The empty set passes all three (the empty function is an injection).
- **Closure.** `prp-countable-subset` (from (ii): restrict the injection). `prp-countable-image` (compose surjections). `prp-union-two-countable` (interleave: \( k(2n) = g(n) \), \( k(2n + 1) = h(n) \) for \( n \in \nN \); \( k \) is well defined and onto because `@thm-division-algorithm` with divisor \( 2 \) writes each element of ℕ uniquely as \( 2q + r \), \( r \in \{0, 1\} \), and for \( a \in \nN \) the quotient \( q \) lies in ℕ: if \( q < 0 \), then \( q \le -1 \) by the discreteness clause of `@prp-integers-closed`, so \( a = 2q + r \le -2 + 1 < 0 \), contradicting \( a \ge 0 \) (`@lem-naturals-arithmetic` (a)); so \( q \ge 0 \), and \( q \in \nN \) (\( q = 0 \), or \( q > 0 \) and \( q \in \nZ_{>0} \subseteq \nN \) by `@prp-integers-closed`); uniqueness inside ℕ follows from uniqueness in ℤ. So every \( a \in \nN \) is \( 2q \) or \( 2q + 1 \) for exactly one \( q \in \nN \), and not both. The empty cases first).
- **`thm-product-countable`** (*Try it first*): \( \nN \times \nN \) is countable. Proof: \( (m, n) \mapsto 2^m 3^n \) is injective (the seven lines of the §02 B2 ordering item: parity — \( 2^j \) is even for \( j \ge 1 \) and \( 3^k \) is odd, by induction, and no integer is both, by `@thm-division-algorithm` — and strict monotonicity of \( 3^k \) by induction; **no unique factorization**), then `thm-countable-characterizations`. Aftermath: the picture of the diagonal-by-diagonal listing, in words, as an alternative that the reader may prefer, with one "Alternatively," paragraph and no formula. `cor-product-countable`: \( A \times B \) is countable if \( A \) and \( B \) are (by `thm-countable-characterizations` (i) \( \Rightarrow \) (ii) take injections \( i \colon A \to \nN \), \( j \colon B \to \nN \); then \( (a, b) \mapsto (i(a), j(b)) \) is an injection \( A \times B \to \nN \times \nN \), and following it with \( (m, n) \mapsto 2^m 3^n \) gives an injection \( A \times B \to \nN \) by `@thm-composition-injective-surjective`; (ii) \( \Rightarrow \) (i) concludes. ## 03-countable-unions-and-choice.md — Countable Unions and the Axiom of Choice

- **Hook (a goal theorem first).** \( A_0, A_1, A_2, \dots \) are countable. Is \( \bigcup_n A_n \) countable? The natural proof: take a list of each \( A_n \) and interleave them as in \( \nN \times \nN \). Where does "take a list of each" come from?
- **The obstacle, in rules.** "\( A_n \) is countable" is an \( \exists \) (a surjection exists); \( \exists \)E names **one** surjection for **one** \( n \) (`@def-rules-existential`, pointed back to). For finitely many \( n \), finitely many \( \exists \)E's; for all \( n \in \nN \) at once there is no derivation rule that does it, and recursion does not help, because the \( n \)-th list is not determined by the earlier ones. So we add a principle.
- **`ax-choice`** (full template for a major notion: situate, hook above, slogan *from any family of non-empty sets one may pick one element from each set at once*, statement, paraphrase, why an axiom, examples, non-example, warning). **Statement:** for every set \( I \) and every family \( (A_i)_{i \in I} \) of **non-empty** sets there is a function \( c \colon I \to \bigcup_{i \in I} A_i \) with \( c(i) \in A_i \) for every \( i \in I \) (a **choice function**). Status, in one paragraph: it is independent of the other rules of set formation (Gödel 1938, Cohen 1963; names and dates only), its equivalents (Zorn's lemma, the well-ordering theorem) are in the optional Chapter 9 (prose pointer), and the main line uses it openly: here; for right inverses; for the Vitali set (Chapter 43), Hahn–Banach (Chapter 67, via Zorn), Tychonoff (Chapter 69) (prose pointers). Remark: forming a sequence \( (a_n)_{n \in \nZ_{>0}} \) from Chapter 11's `cor-sup-approximation` (prose pointer), which Chapter 11 flags in the prose after the proof, is an instance with the countable index set \( I = \nZ_{>0} \); the corollary itself, one \( n \) at a time, is one \( \exists \)E per \( n \) and needs no choice. **When choice is not needed:** \( I \) finite (for one fixed numeral \( n \), \( n \) uses of \( \exists \)E; for a general finite \( I \), induction on the number \( n \) of elements of \( I \) (§01): base \( n = 0 \), the empty function; step: for \( I \) with \( n + 1 \) elements pick \( i_0 \in I \); \( I \setminus \{i_0\} \) has \( n \) elements (`cor-remove-point`), so the hypothesis gives a choice function on it, and that plus one \( \exists \)E for \( A_{i_0} \) gives one on \( I \) — no axiom is needed, and C3 runs the same induction for finite unions); a **rule** that picks (the least element of a set of naturals; the non-negative square root; the first interval of a bisection). This list is the point of the section: a reader must be able to tell the cases apart.
- **`thm-surjection-right-inverse`** (**pays** Chapter 6 §05's promise): every surjection \( f \colon X \to Y \) has a right inverse. Proof: the family \( (f^{-1}(\{y\}))_{y \in Y} \) consists of non-empty sets; a choice function \( c \) satisfies \( f(c(y)) = y \). ## 04-diagonal-arguments.md — Diagonal Arguments: Uncountable Sets and Cantor's Theorem

- **Decimals.** `def-decimal-expansion`: a sequence of digits \( d_1, d_2, \dots \in \{0, \dots, 9\} \) is a **decimal expansion** of \( x \in [0, 1] \), written \( x = 0.d_1 d_2 d_3 \ldots \), if for every \( n \ge 1 \), \( t_n \le x \le t_n + 10^{-n} \), where \( t_n \coloneqq \sum_{k=1}^n d_k 10^{-k} \) (`@def-sum-product`: a finite sum, so no series is needed; digits are indexed by \( \nZ_{>0} \)). A sequence with **no** digit \( 0 \) and no digit \( 9 \) is therefore the **only** expansion of its number, by (D3).

## 05-comparing-cardinalities.md — Comparing Sizes: the Schröder–Bernstein Theorem

- **Hook.** \( (0, 1) \subseteq [0, 1] \subseteq \nR \): are they the same size? Writing down bijections is fiddly; injections are easy.
- **`def-card-le`:** \( \card{A} \le \card{B} \) iff there is an injection \( A \to B \); \( \card{A} < \card{B} \) iff \( \card{A} \le \card{B} \) and not \( \card{A} = \card{B} \). `prp-card-le-preorder`: reflexive and transitive. `prp-card-le-surjection`: for \( A \ne \emptyset \), \( \card{A} \le \card{B} \) iff there is a surjection \( B \to A \) (the hypothesis is needed for \( (\Rightarrow) \) only: for \( A = \emptyset \), \( B = \{b\} \), the empty map is an injection, but there is no surjection \( \{b\} \to \emptyset \)). \( (\Rightarrow) \): an injection \( f \colon A \to B \) has a left inverse \( g \) by `@thm-left-inverse-iff-injective` (b) (uses \( A \ne \emptyset \)), and \( g \) is onto by `@prp-right-inverse-surjective` (\( f \) is a right inverse of \( g \)). \( (\Leftarrow) \): a surjection \( g \colon B \to A \) has a right inverse \( h \) by `thm-surjection-right-inverse`, **using choice** (flag it), and \( h \) is injective by `@thm-left-inverse-iff-injective` (a) (\( g \) is a left inverse of \( h \)). Examples: \( \card{\nN} < \card{\powerset{\nN}} \) (`thm-cantor`); countable means \( \card{A} \le \card{\nN} \) (`thm-countable-characterizations`).
- **`thm-schroder-bernstein`** (key proof): if \( \card{A} \le \card{B} \) and \( \card{B} \le \card{A} \), then \( \card{A} = \card{B} \). Idea with a **Step roadmap** and a picture of chains described in words: points of \( A \) not hit by \( g \) must be sent by \( f \); so must everything reached from them by \( g \circ f \); everything else can be sent back by \( g^{-1} \). ① \( C_0 \coloneqq A \setminus g(B) \), \( C_{n+1} \coloneqq g(f(C_n)) \) (`@thm-recursion`, base \( 0 \), with values in \( \powerset{A} \)), \( C \coloneqq \bigcup_{n \in \nN} C_n \); ② \( h(a) \coloneqq f(a) \) for \( a \in C \), and \( h(a) \coloneqq \) the unique \( b \) with \( g(b) = a \) for \( a \notin C \) (it exists: \( a \notin C_0 \) means \( a \in g(B) \); unique by injectivity of \( g \)); ③ **Claim:** \( h \) injective (the mixed case: \( f(a) = b \), \( g(b) = a' \), \( a \in C_n \) gives \( a' \in C_{n+1} \), a contradiction); ④ **Claim:** \( h \) onto (for \( b \in B \): if \( g(b) \notin C \), \( h(g(b)) = b \); if \( g(b) \in C \), it lies in some \( C_{n+1} \) since \( g(b) \notin C_0 \), so \( g(b) = g(f(a)) \) with \( a \in C_n \), and \( b = f(a) = h(a) \)). *Before reading on* prompt. Remark: no choice is used (every value is given by a rule); the case split "\( a \in C \) or not" is excluded middle (Chapter 5 §07, prose pointer), one clause.
- **Examples.** `exm-closed-open-interval`: \( [0, 1] \) and \( (0, 1) \) are equinumerous, twice: by `thm-schroder-bernstein` (the inclusion and \( x \mapsto x/2 + 1/4 \)), and by the explicit map \( \varphi(0) = \frac12 \), \( \varphi(1/n) = 1/(n + 2) \) for \( n \in \nZ_{>0} \), \( \varphi(x) = x \) otherwise — a Hilbert's hotel inside \( [0, 1] \) (check it is a bijection: the points \( 0, 1, \frac12, \frac13, \dots \) go onto \( \frac12, \frac13, \frac14, \dots \), everything else is fixed). `exm-reals-open-interval`: \( x \mapsto x/(1 + \abs{x}) \) is a bijection \( \nR \to (-1, 1) \) with inverse \( y \mapsto y/(1 - \abs{y}) \) (verified inline; Chapter 6 §05 B3 did the same computation, not cited), then \( y \mapsto (y + 1)/2 \); so every open bounded interval \( (c, d) \) with \( c < d \) is equinumerous with ℝ (an affine map \( t \mapsto c + (d - c)t \) carries \( (0, 1) \) onto it); and every interval \( I \) with more than one point is equinumerous with ℝ: pick \( c < d \) in \( I \); then \( (c, d) \subseteq I \subseteq \nR \) (an interval contains everything between two of its points), the inclusion is an injection \( (c, d) \to I \), and \( I \subseteq \nR \) composed with a bijection \( \nR \to (c, d) \) is an injection \( I \to (c, d) \), so `thm-schroder-bernstein` gives \( \card{I} = \card{(c, d)} = \card{\nR} \) — this covers the closed, half-open and unbounded intervals (rays such as \( (a, \infty) \)) at once.

## 06-infinite-sets.md — Infinite Sets

- **Hook.** ℕ is the smallest infinite set? Every infinite set should contain a copy of ℕ: keep picking new elements. That "keep picking" is choice again, in a stronger form.
- **`thm-infinite-countable-subset`:** every infinite set \( X \) has a countably infinite subset. Proof: by `ax-choice` applied to the family \( (S)_{S \in \powerset{X} \setminus \{\emptyset\}} \), fix a choice function \( c \) (**choice**, flagged); define \( F_0 \coloneqq \emptyset \), \( F_{n+1} \coloneqq F_n \cup \{ c(X \setminus F_n) \} \) by `@thm-recursion` (base \( 0 \); with \( g(n, F) = F \cup \{ c(X \setminus F) \} \) if \( F \ne X \), and \( F \) otherwise), and \( x_n \coloneqq c(X \setminus F_n) \); by induction \( F_n = \{x_0, \dots, x_{n-1}\} \) has exactly \( n \) elements (`prp-disjoint-union-count`), so \( F_n \ne X \) (\( X \) is infinite) and \( x_n \in X \setminus F_n \); for \( m < n \), \( x_m \in F_{m+1} \subseteq F_n \), so \( x_m \ne x_n \): \( n \mapsto x_n \) is an injection \( \nN \to X \), and its image is a countably infinite subset. **Contrast** (a remark): Chapter 11 §07's intervals are chosen by an explicit rule, and needed no choice; here no rule is available for an arbitrary \( X \), so the choice function **is** the rule.
- **`thm-infinite-iff-proper-subset`:** a set is infinite iff it is equinumerous with a proper subset of itself. \( (\Leftarrow) \) is `thm-finite-no-proper-copy`. \( (\Rightarrow) \): take \( \{x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots\} \) from `thm-infinite-countable-subset` (distinct), send \( x_n \mapsto x_{n+1} \) and fix everything else: a bijection \( X \to X \setminus \{x_0\} \) (the hotel of `exm-hilbert-hotel` inside \( X \)). Aftermath: the curriculum's Stage 0 example (d), done.
- **Consequences.** For every infinite \( X \), \( \card{\nN} \le \card{X} \): ℕ is the smallest infinite size. Countable sets are exactly those of size at most \( \card{\nN} \).
- **Warning.** "Infinite" and "uncountable" are different: ℚ is infinite and countable. And "contains a copy of ℕ" is not "is equinumerous with ℕ".
- **Quick check.** Name a proper subset of ℝ equinumerous with ℝ, and a bijection. (\( (-1, 1) \), with \( x \mapsto x/(1 + \abs{x}) \) of `exm-reals-open-interval`; exponentials are not yet available.)

(No.)

(b) \( E = \bigcup_{N \in \nN} E_N \), \( E_N \) = sequences in \( S \) constant from \( N \) on. Each \( E_N \) is countable: \( E_0 \) is the single zero sequence (\( a_0 \le 0 \)); for \( N \ge 1 \), \( f \mapsto (f(0), \dots, f(N)) \) is an injection \( E_N \to \nN^{N+1} \) (a sequence in \( E_N \) is constant from \( N \) on, with value \( f(N) \)), \( \nN^{N+1} \) is countable by `cor-product-countable` (\( N + 1 \ge 2 \) factors), and composing with an injection \( \nN^{N+1} \to \nN \) gives an injection \( E_N \to \nN \) (`thm-countable-characterizations`). So \( E \) is a countable union of countable sets (`thm-countable-union`; choice avoidable: the injections \( E_N \to \nN \) are given by one explicit rule, the injection above followed by the iterated \( 2^m 3^n \) coding of `cor-product-countable`, so each \( E_N \) is listed by least preimages, as in §02). Aside: each \( E_N \) is in fact finite, with \( \prod_{k=1}^{N+1} k = (N + 1)! \) members (\( a_0, a_1, \dots, a_N \) have \( 1, 2, \dots, N + 1 \) possible values; `@def-sum-product`, \( n! \coloneqq \prod_{k=1}^{n} k \)); this rests on §01 C2 and its iteration by induction on the number of factors, and the sidecar checks \( 1, 2, 6, 24, 120 \) for \( N = 0, \dots, 4 \). The diagonal of a list of \( E \) lies in \( S \) and not on the list, hence **not in \( E \)**: no contradiction.
