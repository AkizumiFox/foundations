**Signature moves** (`index.md`, "Named moves"):
- **Element-chasing.** To prove \( A \subseteq B \): "let \( x \in A \)", unwind \( A \)'s definition, rewind into \( B \)'s. To prove \( A = B \): two inclusions.
- **Unwind the image.** "\( y \in f(A) \)" means "there is \( a \in A \) with \( f(a) = y \)": name the witness (\( \exists \)E) before using it; a preimage has no witness to name.
- **Refute with a small witness.** A two- or three-element set, \( x \mapsto x^2 \), the empty set.
- **Build the inverse from the definition.** For a left inverse, send \( f(x) \) back to \( x \) and everything else to one fixed point.

## index.md

- **Title:** Sets, Functions, Images and Preimages.
- **Story.** Every definition of analysis is a sentence about sets and functions: "\( f \) is continuous if the preimage of every open set is open", "\( \sup A \) is the least element of the set of upper bounds". Chapters 1–5 gave the grammar of such sentences; this chapter supplies the nouns. Two surprises motivate it. First, the naive rule "any property defines a set" is contradictory (Russell), so we must say which sets exist. Second, images and preimages behave differently: preimages respect every set operation, images only unions. The motivating problem: *for \( f(x) = x^2 \), is \( f(A \cap B) = f(A) \cap f(B) \)? Is \( f^{-1}(C \cap D) = f^{-1}(C) \cap f^{-1}(D) \)? Why is one always true and the other not?* The answer is one quantifier: an image hides an "there exists", a preimage does not.

## 01-sets-and-subsets.md — Sets, Subsets and Russell's Paradox

- **Hook (a naive notion that fails).** "A set is any collection; any property defines the set of things having it." Run it on the property "\( x \notin x \)". Hold the contradiction until the end of the section; open with what does work.
- **Definitions** (minor notions, steps 4, 7, 8 of the explain template, except `def-subset`, which gets the full template: it is the notion every later proof about sets unwinds).
  - Sets and membership \( x \in A \) are primitive: a set is determined by its elements. `def-set-equality` (extensionality): \( A = B \) means \( \forall x\,(x \in A \leftrightarrow x \in B) \).
  - `def-subset`: \( A \subseteq B \) means \( \forall x\,(x \in A \to x \in B) \); proper subset \( A \subsetneq B \). In symbols with the rules named: proving \( A \subseteq B \) is \( \forall \)I around \( \to \)I (`@def-rules-universal` cited); using it is \( \forall \)E then \( \to \)E. **Slogan:** *to show \( A \subseteq B \), take an arbitrary element of \( A \) and show it lies in \( B \).*
  - `def-empty-set`: a set with no elements. `prp-empty-set-unique` (two empty sets \( E, E' \) are equal: for every \( x \), both \( x \in E \) and \( x \in E' \) are false, so each implies the other vacuously (`@prp-vacuous-implication`), and `def-set-equality` with `@prp-iff-two-implications` gives \( E = E' \); the route goes through `def-set-equality` directly, because `prp-subset-antisymmetric` is printed later in the section). Then \( \emptyset \subseteq A \) for every \( A \): one line, vacuous truth again.
  - `def-set-builder` (specification): for a set \( A \) and a property \( P \), \( \{ x \in A : P(x) \} \) is a set, and \( y \) belongs to it iff \( y \in A \) and \( P(y) \). Also listing, \( \{a, b, c\} \). **The working rules of set formation**, stated as a short list in prose, not as axioms: specification, listing finitely many objects, unions (of two sets, and of a family of sets indexed by a set: §02), power sets, products (§02–§03), and the number sets ℕ, ℤ, ℚ, ℝ as given. One sentence: the optional Chapter 9 lists the axioms behind these rules (prose pointer).
- **Results.**
  - `prp-subset-antisymmetric`: \( A = B \) iff \( A \subseteq B \) and \( B \subseteq A \). Proof: `def-set-equality` and `@prp-iff-two-implications` inside a \( \forall \). This is the licence for the "two inclusions" move.
  - Transitivity of \( \subseteq \), as a worked two-line proof **tagged rule by rule** (the first tagged proof of the chapter).
  - `thm-russell`: for every set \( A \), the set \( R_A \coloneqq \{ x \in A : x \notin x \} \) is **not** an element of \( A \). Hence there is no set of all sets. *Try it first.* Idea: if \( R_A \in A \), ask whether \( R_A \in R_A \); each answer gives the other. Proof: \( \neg \)I on \( R_A \in A \); inside, the two cases of LEM on \( R_A \in R_A \) each reach \( \bot \) (or, without LEM: assuming \( R_A \in R_A \) gives \( R_A \notin R_A \), so \( R_A \notin R_A \) by \( \neg \)I, and then \( R_A \in R_A \) by the definition; state which route is printed: the second, which needs **no RAA** — say so, and why that is worth noticing: the paradox is not an artefact of classical logic). Aftermath: the naive rule "any property defines a set" is exactly specification **without** the "\( x \in A \)"; the paradox is why every set-builder in this book names an ambient set. Forward pointer (prose): Chapter 8's proof that no map \( X \to \powerset{X} \) is onto is the same argument.
- **Examples.** `exm-set-builder-small`: \( \{ x \in \nZ : x^2 < 5 \} = \{-2, -1, 0, 1, 2\} \) proved by two inclusions (the \( \subseteq \) direction needs the school fact that \( \abs{x} \ge 3 \) gives \( x^2 \ge 9 \)); \( \{ x \in \nR : x^2 < 0 \} = \emptyset \); degenerate cases: \( \{\emptyset\} \ne \emptyset \) (one has an element), \( \{1, 1, 2\} = \{1, 2\} \) (extensionality ignores repetition), \( \{1, 2\} = \{2, 1\} \) (and order).
- **Non-example by minimal change.** \( \{1, \{1\}\} \): \( \{1\} \) is an element and also a subset (\( \{1\} \subseteq \{1, \{1\}\} \)), whereas in \( \{1, 2\} \), \( \{1\} \) is a subset and not an element.
- **Warnings.** \( \in \) versus \( \subseteq \): \( \emptyset \subseteq \{1\} \) but \( \emptyset \notin \{1\} \); \( 1 \in \{1\} \) and \( \{1\} \subseteq \{1\} \), but \( \{1\} \notin \{1\} \). A second warning: proving \( A \subseteq B \) by checking some elements is (N1) (`@exm-not-allowed-catalogue` cited).
- **Quick checks.** Is \( \emptyset \subseteq \emptyset \)? Is \( \emptyset \in \emptyset \)? Which of \( \emptyset, \{\emptyset\}, \{\{\emptyset\}\} \) are equal? (Yes; no; none.)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1:** (a) negate "\( A \subseteq B \)" in symbols and in words (`@prp-quantifier-negation` and `@prp-negated-implication` cited: the answer is \( \exists x\,(x \in A \wedge x \notin B) \)); (b) true or false: \( \{1\} \in \{\{1\}, 2\} \), \( \{1\} \subseteq \{\{1\}, 2\} \), \( \emptyset \subseteq \emptyset \), \( \{\emptyset\} \subseteq \{1\} \); (c) list \( \{ n \in \nZ : 0 < n^2 \le 9 \} \); (d) which rule is the first step of every proof of \( A \subseteq B \)?

  - **C1:** (a) Let \( A \) be a set. Show that the set \( A^{+} \coloneqq A \cup \{ R_A \} \), with \( R_A \) as in `thm-russell`, is strictly larger than \( A \): \( A \subsetneq A^{+} \). ## 02-set-operations.md — Unions, Intersections, Complements and Families

- **Hook (the standard example, generalised).** \( \{ x : x \in A \text{ or } x \in B \} \): each connective of Chapter 1 makes a set operation.
- **Definitions.**
  - `def-set-operations`: \( A \cup B \), \( A \cap B \), \( A \setminus B \); disjoint sets. The complement is always relative: \( X \setminus A \) for \( A \subseteq X \), with the ambient \( X \) named.
  - `def-power-set`: \( \powerset{X} = \{ A : A \subseteq X \} \). One clause: the working rules of §01 say this is a set.
  - `def-indexed-family` (a family \( (A_i)_{i \in I} \) is a function \( i \mapsto A_i \); stated informally here since functions are §04, with a forward pointer) and `def-family-union-intersection`: \( \bigcup_{i \in I} A_i = \{ x : \exists i \in I,\ x \in A_i \} \); for \( A_i \subseteq X \), \( \bigcap_{i \in I} A_i = \{ x \in X : \forall i \in I,\ x \in A_i \} \). The union is a set by the §01 working rule "unions", not by specification: the set-builder \( \{ x : \dots \} \) only describes its elements, and when all \( A_i \subseteq X \) it may be written \( \{ x \in X : \exists i \in I,\ x \in A_i \} \), in line with §01's "every set-builder names an ambient set" (say this in one clause, so the reader does not see the rule broken on the next page). ## 04-functions.md — Functions, Composition, Injections and Surjections

- **Hook (an everyday word made exact).** "A rule assigning to each input one output": what is a rule? The answer: forget the rule, keep the input–output pairs.
- **`def-function`** (major for Part 0: full explain template). A **function** from \( X \) to \( Y \) is given by a relation \( f \subseteq X \times Y \) (its graph; see the convention below for the declared codomain) such that **for every** \( x \in X \) there is **exactly one** \( y \in Y \) with \( (x, y) \in f \) (`@def-unique-existence` cited; the \( \exists! \) is the whole definition). \( f \colon X \to Y \), \( f(x) \); domain \( X \), **codomain** \( Y \), range \( f(X) \) (the image of §06, previewed as a set-builder). **Convention, stated as such, and made consistent with "a function is a relation":** a relation is a set, so two functions with the same graph would be **equal** by `def-set-equality` whatever codomain was declared (the graphs of \( x \mapsto x^2 \) as \( [0, \infty) \to \nR \) and as \( [0, \infty) \to [0, \infty) \) are the same set). To make the codomain part of the data, the definition says: a function from \( X \) to \( Y \) is the pair \( (G, Y) \) of its **graph** \( G \subseteq X \times Y \), which has the \( \forall\exists! \) property, and its declared codomain \( Y \); we write \( f \) for both and \( (x, y) \in f \) for \( (x, y) \in G \) (one sentence on the page; the domain needs no slot, since the graph determines it). Then `prp-function-equality`: two functions are equal iff same domain, same codomain and \( f(x) = g(x) \) for every \( x \in X \). Route: the characteristic property of pairs (`def-ordered-pair`) splits \( (G, Y) = (G', Y') \) into \( G = G' \) and \( Y = Y' \); \( G = G' \) by `def-set-equality` unwinds to same domain and same values (the \( \exists! \) turns "\( (x, y) \in G \iff (x, y) \in G' \)" into "\( f(x) = g(x) \)"). (S2a recheck: without the pair, `prp-function-equality` contradicts `def-set-equality`.) Why this convention: surjectivity depends on the codomain (`exm-square-three-ways`). Slogan: *a function is its graph, with a declared target.* Families \( (A_i)_{i \in I} \) of §02 are now exactly functions on \( I \) (a family of subsets of \( X \) is a function \( I \to \powerset{X} \): the codomain the definition asks for); sequences \( (a_n) \) are functions on \( \nN \) (NOTATION row exists); \( Y^X \) the set of all functions \( X \to Y \); indicator functions \( \ind_A \colon X \to \{0, 1\} \).
- **Examples.** \( \id_X \); constants; \( \ind_A \); `prp-empty-function`: for every \( Y \) there is exactly one function \( \emptyset \to Y \) (the empty relation; the condition is vacuous), and there is **no** function \( X \to \emptyset \) when \( X \ne \emptyset \) (degenerate cases, both proved in two lines). Restriction \( f|_A \).
- **Non-examples.** `exm-not-a-function`: the circle relation (two \( y \) for \( x = 0 \), none for \( x = 2 \): each clause of \( \exists! \) fails separately); the "rule" \( p/q \mapsto p \) on ℚ (not well defined: \( 1/2 = 2/4 \) has two outputs) — a function must not depend on how the input is written, which is Chapter 7's theme for classes (prose pointer).

- **`def-injective`, `def-surjective`, `def-bijective`**, each also in symbols: injective \( \forall x_1, x_2 \in X\,(f(x_1) = f(x_2) \to x_1 = x_2) \) (and its contrapositive form, `@prp-contrapositive` cited, as the working form); surjective \( \forall y \in Y\ \exists x \in X\ f(x) = y \). ## 05-inverses.md — Left, Right and Two-Sided Inverses

- **Hook (a goal theorem first).** Undoing a function: when can we get \( x \) back from \( f(x) \)? When can we reach every \( y \)? Injectivity and surjectivity are exactly the answers, one for each side.
- **Definitions.** `def-left-right-inverse`: \( g \colon Y \to X \) is a **left inverse** of \( f \) if \( g \circ f = \id_X \), a **right inverse** if \( f \circ g = \id_Y \). `def-inverse-function`: a **two-sided** inverse, written \( f^{-1} \) (with a warning, §06, about the preimage reading). Examples first: \( n \mapsto 2n \) on ℕ has many left inverses (\( m \mapsto m/2 \) on evens, anything on odds) and no right inverse.
- **Results.**
  - `thm-left-inverse-iff-injective` (key proof), stated in two parts so that the hypothesis sits only where it is used: let \( f \colon X \to Y \). (a) If \( f \) has a left inverse, \( f \) is injective (no hypothesis). (b) If \( X \ne \emptyset \) and \( f \) is injective, \( f \) has a left inverse. Hence for \( X \ne \emptyset \): a left inverse exists iff \( f \) is injective. (S2a recheck: with \( X \ne \emptyset \) as a global hypothesis, `thm-bijection-iff-inverse` (\( \Leftarrow \)) could not cite (a) when \( X = \emptyset \).) \( (\Rightarrow) \): \( f(x_1) = f(x_2) \) gives \( x_1 = g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)) = x_2 \). \( (\Leftarrow) \): fix \( x_0 \in X \) (the one \( \exists \)E the hypothesis \( X \ne \emptyset \) licenses); for \( y \in f(X) \) let \( g(y) \) be the **unique** \( x \) with \( f(x) = y \) (uniqueness is injectivity; `@prp-unique-existence-use` cited); for \( y \notin f(X) \) let \( g(y) = x_0 \).   - `prp-right-inverse-surjective`: if \( f \) has a right inverse, \( f \) is surjective (\( y = f(g(y)) \)). **The converse question, stated and left open:** given a surjection, a right inverse must pick **one** preimage for **every** \( y \) at once. For one \( y \), \( \exists \)E names a preimage; for finitely many, finitely many \( \exists \)E's do; for infinitely many \( y \), no rule of Chapter 4 does it. When a rule is available (\( x \mapsto x^2 \) on \( \nR \to [0, \infty) \): take the non-negative root) we can write \( g \) down. In general a new principle is needed: Chapter 8 states it (prose pointer; the promise). Keep this to one short subsection, **no** proof and no axiom here.
  - `thm-bijection-iff-inverse`: \( f \) is bijective iff it has a two-sided inverse; `prp-inverse-unique`: a left inverse and a right inverse of the same \( f \) are equal (\( g = g \circ (f \circ h) = (g \circ f) \circ h = h \)), so a two-sided inverse is unique, and \( f^{-1} \) is well named. Route for \( (\Leftarrow) \): a two-sided inverse is a left inverse and a right inverse, so `thm-left-inverse-iff-injective` (a) and `prp-right-inverse-surjective` (valid also for \( X = \emptyset \)). Route for \( (\Rightarrow) \) **without choice**: for a bijection the preimage of each \( y \) has exactly one element, so \( g(y) \coloneqq \) that element is defined by `@prp-unique-existence-use` — say explicitly that this is why no new principle is needed here, unlike the converse question above.
- **Warnings.** A left inverse need not be a right inverse (\( n \mapsto 2n \)). \( f^{-1} \) written for a non-bijective \( f \) as a function is meaningless; as a preimage it is always meaningful (§06).

## 06-images-and-preimages.md — Images and Preimages

- **Hook (the motivating problem of the index).** For \( f(x) = x^2 \), \( A = [-1, 0] \), \( B = [0, 1] \): \( f(A \cap B) = \{0\} \) but \( f(A) \cap f(B) = [0, 1] \). For preimages no such thing happens. Why?
- **Definitions.** `def-image`: \( f(A) = \{ f(a) : a \in A \} = \{ y \in Y : \exists a \in A,\ f(a) = y \} \) for \( A \subseteq X \). `def-preimage`: \( f^{-1}(B) = \{ x \in X : f(x) \in B \} \) for \( B \subseteq Y \), defined for **every** function. **Slogan:** *an image hides a "there exists"; a preimage hides nothing.* Paraphrase: membership in \( f(A) \) must be unwound by \( \exists \)E (name the witness), membership in \( f^{-1}(B) \) is unwound by one substitution.
- **Examples.** `exm-images-of-square`: for \( f(x) = x^2 \): \( f([-1, 2]) = [0, 4] \), \( f^{-1}([1, 4]) = [-2, -1] \cup [1, 2] \), \( f^{-1}(\{-1\}) = \emptyset \), \( f^{-1}(f([0, 1])) = [-1, 1] \) (square roots: school fact, ledgered). `exm-sets-of-numbers-as-images` (**pays the Chapter 11 promise**): \( -A \) is \( f(A) \) for \( f(x) = -x \); \( cA \) is the image under \( x \mapsto cx \); \( A + B \) is the image of \( A \times B \) under \( (a, b) \mapsto a + b \). Say that Chapter 11 uses these sets (prose pointer; Chapter 11 defines them by set-builder, which is the same set by `def-image`).
- **Results.**
  - `thm-preimage-operations` (key proof): for any \( f \colon X \to Y \) and any family \( (B_i)_{i \in I} \) of subsets of \( Y \): \( f^{-1}(\bigcup B_i) = \bigcup f^{-1}(B_i) \); \( f^{-1}(\bigcap B_i) = \bigcap f^{-1}(B_i) \) (intersections relative to \( Y \) and \( X \), consistent at \( I = \emptyset \): both sides are \( X \)); \( f^{-1}(Y \setminus B) = X \setminus f^{-1}(B) \); \( f^{-1}(B \setminus C) = f^{-1}(B) \setminus f^{-1}(C) \). Proof: one chain of "iff"s per law, each line naming the definition unwound; **no hypothesis on \( f \)**, and the Idea says why: there is no witness to name.
  - `prp-preimage-composition`: \( (g \circ f)^{-1}(C) = f^{-1}(g^{-1}(C)) \) (for Part II's continuity of composites; prose pointer).
  - `thm-image-union`: \( f(\bigcup A_i) = \bigcup f(A_i) \). Proof: element-chasing with one \( \exists \)E and one \( \exists \)I in each direction, tagged; the quantifiers \( \exists i\,\exists a \) are exchanged inline (two \( \exists \)E, then two \( \exists \)I in the other order): no Chapter 4 label states the \( \exists\exists \) swap, so the proof does it on the page in two sentences.
  - `thm-image-intersection`: \( f(A \cap B) \subseteq f(A) \cap f(B) \) always, and **equality holds for all \( A, B \subseteq X \) iff \( f \) is injective**. \( (\Leftarrow) \): *Try it first*; the two witnesses \( a \in A \), \( b \in B \) of \( y \) are equal by injectivity. \( (\Rightarrow) \): contrapositive — if \( f(x_1) = f(x_2) \) with \( x_1 \ne x_2 \), take \( A = \{x_1\} \), \( B = \{x_2\} \). *Before reading on* prompt. The motivating example is the witness for "equality can fail".
  - `prp-image-preimage`: \( A \subseteq f^{-1}(f(A)) \), with equality for all \( A \) iff \( f \) injective; \( f(f^{-1}(B)) = B \cap f(X) \subseteq B \), with equality for all \( B \) iff \( f \) surjective. Prove the first pair in full; the second pair's "iff" as an exercise-sized paragraph in the text (not an exercise: nothing later may cite an exercise).
