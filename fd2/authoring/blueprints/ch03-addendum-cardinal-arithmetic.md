### 4.1 Section 3.6: Cardinal Arithmetic (`06-cardinal-arithmetic.md`)

**Budget:** main line ≤ 5,000 (expected ~4,800). One fold, F3.6-a (~350 words, the routine parts of `thm-cardinal-laws`). Six Quick checks, two warnings, one remark (the notation of other books).

**Opening and `## The plane and the line` (hook, ~330).**
1. Opening paragraph (~90): Section 2.5 counted finite sets, and Section 2.3 proved the laws of powers \( m^{n+k} = m^n m^k \) and \( (m^n)^k = m^{nk} \) by induction (@prp-power-laws). For finite sets these count functions: \( Y^X \) has \( m^n \) elements when \( Y \) has \( m \) and \( X \) has \( n \) (Section 1.5, after @exm-function-set-small). This section asks what is left of that arithmetic when the sets are infinite, where there are no numbers to compute with, only bijections.
2. The hook (~240). "A square has two dimensions and a segment has one. Surely the plane \( \nR \times \nR \) has more points than the line \( \nR \)?" Take one line in it: \( x \mapsto (x, 0) \) is an injection \( \nR \to \nR \times \nR \), so \( \card{\nR} \le \card{\nR \times \nR} \). The plane is at least as big. A second question: is the set \( \nN^{\nN} \) of all sequences of natural numbers bigger than \( \nR \)? Each real number in \( [0, 1) \) is a sequence of digits, a sequence of natural numbers that stay below \( 10 \), so \( \nN^{\nN} \) looks like the bigger set. "Before reading on, guess both answers." Then: "Both questions turn into arithmetic. The plane is a product, \( \nN^{\nN} \) is a power, and the line turns out to be a power too: of \( 2 \)." No answer is given here.

**`## Adding sizes: disjoint unions` (~700).**
1. **Naive attempt** (~110): for finite disjoint sets, Section 2.5 added sizes by taking a union (@prp-disjoint-union-count). So define the sum of two sets as their union? Try \( A = B = \{0\} \): we want a set with two elements, and \( A \cup B = \{0\} \) has one. The union counts the overlap once. The cure is to make two copies that cannot overlap, by tagging every element with the name of the set it came from.
2. *One-line summary:* "*A disjoint union keeps a labelled copy of each set, so nothing is counted twice.*"
3. **`def-disjoint-union`** `[Disjoint Union]`: "Let \( A \) and \( B \) be sets. Their **disjoint union** is
   \[ A \sqcup B \coloneqq (A \times \{0\}) \cup (B \times \{1\}) . \]
   The maps \( \iota_A \colon A \to A \sqcup B \), \( \iota_A(a) \coloneqq (a, 0) \), and \( \iota_B \colon B \to A \sqcup B \), \( \iota_B(b) \coloneqq (b, 1) \), are the **inclusions**."
4. Unwinding (~150), stated and proved inline: every element of \( A \sqcup B \) is \( (a, 0) \) with \( a \in A \) or \( (b, 1) \) with \( b \in B \), and not both, since \( (a, 0) = (b, 1) \) would give \( 0 = 1 \) (@def-ordered-pair). Both inclusions are injective by the same property of pairs, and their images \( A \times \{0\} \) and \( B \times \{1\} \) are disjoint and cover \( A \sqcup B \). So a function on \( A \sqcup B \) may be defined by giving its values on the two tagged parts separately. This is the "two cases that cannot overlap" pattern of `exm-closed-open-interval` and of the proof of `@prp-union-two-countable`.
5. **`exm-two-copies-of-naturals`** `[Two copies of ℕ]`: "Show that \( \card{\nN \sqcup \nN} = \card{\nN} \)." *Solution:* \( e(n, 0) \coloneqq 2n \) and \( e(n, 1) \coloneqq 2n + 1 \) define \( e \colon \nN \sqcup \nN \to \nN \). The decoder: each \( m \in \nN \) is \( 2q + r \) for exactly one pair with \( q \in \nN \), \( r \in \{0, 1\} \) (@thm-division-algorithm with divisor 2, the quotient in \( \nN \) as in the proof of @prp-union-two-countable), and \( d(m) \coloneqq (q, r) \). Then \( d(e(n, r)) = (n, r) \) by the uniqueness, and \( e(d(m)) = 2q + r = m \). By @thm-bijection-iff-inverse, \( e \) is a bijection. Aftermath (one sentence): this is the first encoding of the section; the box comes after the next example.
6. **Quick check 1** (weird examples, after the example): "(a) List the elements of \( \{a, b\} \sqcup \{a\} \), and of \( \{a, b\} \cup \{a\} \). (b) What is \( \emptyset \sqcup B \)? (c) If \( A \cap B = \emptyset \), give a bijection \( A \cup B \to A \sqcup B \)." *Solution:* (a) \( (a, 0), (b, 0), (a, 1) \): three elements, against the two of \( \{a, b\} \). (b) \( B \times \{1\} \), and \( b \mapsto (b, 1) \) is a bijection \( B \to \emptyset \sqcup B \). (c) Send \( x \) to \( (x, 0) \) if \( x \in A \) and to \( (x, 1) \) if \( x \in B \). The cases do not overlap because \( A \cap B = \emptyset \), so this is a function; the inverse is \( (a, 0) \mapsto a \), \( (b, 1) \mapsto b \).
7. **Warning** `**No subtraction.**` (~90): "For numbers, \( m + k = n + k \) gives \( m = n \). For sets it fails: \( \card{\{0\} \sqcup \nN} = \card{\nN} = \card{\emptyset \sqcup \nN} \), and \( \card{\{0\}} \ne \card{\emptyset} \). (The first equality is Hilbert's hotel, @exm-hilbert-hotel: \( (0, 0) \mapsto 0 \) and \( (n, 1) \mapsto n + 1 \).) Arithmetic of infinite sizes has no cancellation, and every law below is proved, never borrowed from numbers." B4 (a) returns to this.

**`## Multiplying, raising to powers, and comparing` (~650).**
1. Lead (~80): products \( A \times B \) (@def-cartesian-product) and sets of functions \( Y^X \) (Section 1.5) are the other two operations. For finite sets they have \( mn \) and \( m^n \) elements (Section 2.5); this is prose, not a citation of `@exr-finite-sets-c2` in a proof. The base of the power is written on the line, \( Y \), and the exponent is the domain, \( X \).
2. **`prp-card-operations-well-defined`** `[Sums, Products and Powers Respect Size]`: "Let \( A, A', B, B' \) be sets with \( \card{A} = \card{A'} \) and \( \card{B} = \card{B'} \). Then
   (a) \( \card{A \sqcup B} = \card{A' \sqcup B'} \); (b) \( \card{A \times B} = \card{A' \times B'} \); (c) \( \card{A^B} = \card{A'^{B'}} \)."
   *Proof:* let \( \alpha \colon A \to A' \) and \( \beta \colon B \to B' \) be bijections, with inverses \( \alpha^{-1} \), \( \beta^{-1} \) (@thm-bijection-iff-inverse). (a) \( (a, 0) \mapsto (\alpha(a), 0) \) and \( (b, 1) \mapsto (\beta(b), 1) \); the same with \( \alpha^{-1}, \beta^{-1} \) is a two-sided inverse. (b) \( (a, b) \mapsto (\alpha(a), \beta(b)) \), with inverse \( (a', b') \mapsto (\alpha^{-1}(a'), \beta^{-1}(b')) \). (c) \( f \mapsto \alpha \circ f \circ \beta^{-1} \), a function \( B' \to A' \); the inverse is \( g \mapsto \alpha^{-1} \circ g \circ \beta \). The round trips follow from @prp-composition-associative and \( \alpha^{-1} \circ \alpha = \id_A \), and so on. Each map is defined by the two-part rule of the unwinding in item 4 of the previous subsection.
3. **Remark** (the plant; ~80, prose, the section's one forward pointer): "The proposition is what allows us to speak of 'the' sum of two sizes: the answer does not depend on which sets of those sizes we use. Computing with representatives and then checking that the answer is independent of them is a pattern that comes back with the clock of Section 4.2, where it is called well-definedness."
4. **`prp-card-operations-monotone`** `[Monotonicity]`: "Let \( A, A', B, B' \) be sets with \( \card{A} \le \card{A'} \) and \( \card{B} \le \card{B'} \). Then
   (a) \( \card{A \sqcup B} \le \card{A' \sqcup B'} \); (b) \( \card{A \times B} \le \card{A' \times B'} \); (c) if \( A' \ne \emptyset \), then \( \card{A^B} \le \card{A'^{B'}} \)."
   *Idea* (two lines): carry the injections across, as in the previous proof; for powers, a function on \( B \) must be extended to all of \( B' \), and the extension needs a value to put on the points that \( B \) does not reach.
   *Proof:* let \( u \colon A \to A' \) and \( v \colon B \to B' \) be injections. (a), (b): the maps of the previous proof with \( u, v \) in place of \( \alpha, \beta \) are injective, by @def-ordered-pair. (c) Fix \( a_0 \in A' \). For \( f \in A^B \), define \( F(f) \colon B' \to A' \) by \( F(f)(b') \coloneqq u(f(b)) \) if \( b' = v(b) \) for some \( b \in B \), and \( F(f)(b') \coloneqq a_0 \) if \( b' \notin v(B) \). In the first case \( b \) is unique, since \( v \) is injective, so \( F(f) \) is a function. If \( F(f) = F(g) \), then for every \( b \in B \), \( u(f(b)) = F(f)(v(b)) = F(g)(v(b)) = u(g(b)) \), so \( f(b) = g(b) \), since \( u \) is injective. Hence \( f = g \) (@prp-function-equality).
5. **Non-example by minimal change** (~90): drop "\( A' \ne \emptyset \)" from (c). Take \( A = A' = \emptyset \), \( B = \emptyset \) and \( B' = \{0\} \). Then \( \card{B} \le \card{B'} \), but \( \emptyset^{\emptyset} \) has one element (the empty function, @prp-empty-function) and \( \emptyset^{\{0\}} = \emptyset \), so there is no injection from the first into the second. "The proof used \( a_0 \) exactly once, and here there is no \( a_0 \)." (If \( A' = \emptyset \), then \( A = \emptyset \), since an injection into \( \emptyset \) has empty domain.)
6. **Quick check 2:** "Where does the proof of (c) use that \( v \) is injective? Where does it use that \( u \) is injective?" *Solution:* \( v \) is used to make \( F(f) \) a function: the point \( b \) with \( v(b) = b' \) must be unique. \( u \) is used to make \( F \) injective: \( u(f(b)) = u(g(b)) \) must give \( f(b) = g(b) \).

**`## Encoding: ℕ × ℕ once more` (~800; method section 1).**
1. Lead (~70): 3.1 showed \( \nN \times \nN \) countable by an injection, and 3.4's Quick check obtained \( \card{\nN \times \nN} = \card{\nN} \) from Schröder–Bernstein without ever seeing a bijection. Here is one, written down.
2. **`exm-pairing-bijection`** `[A pairing of ℕ × ℕ with ℕ]`: "Show that \( \pi(m, n) \coloneqq 2^m(2n + 1) - 1 \) is a bijection \( \nN \times \nN \to \nN \)."
   - **`[Scratch work]`** (≤ 20 lines). What must the code record? Two numbers, inside one, so that both can be read back. Powers of 2 and odd numbers do not interfere: the power of 2 in \( 2^m(2n+1) \) records \( m \), and the odd part records \( n \). The \( -1 \) is there because \( 2^m(2n+1) \ge 1 \) never takes the value 0. The decoder: given \( k \), factor \( k + 1 \) as a power of 2 times an odd number. We need this to be always possible, and in only one way.

     | Givens | Goal |
     |---|---|
     | \( (m, n) \in \nN \times \nN \) | \( \pi(m, n) \in \nN \) |
     | \( k \in \nN \) | exactly one \( (m, n) \) with \( 2^m(2n + 1) = k + 1 \) |
     | gap | every integer \( N \ge 1 \) is \( 2^m q \) with \( m \in \nN \) and \( q \) odd, in exactly one way |

     "The gap is a claim about integers. Existence: if \( N \) is odd, \( m = 0 \); if \( N \) is even, halve it and use the claim for \( N/2 \), which is smaller, so strong induction. Uniqueness: two factorizations with different powers would make an odd number even."
   - *Solution.* *Claim.* Every integer \( N \ge 1 \) equals \( 2^m q \) for some \( m \in \nN \) and some odd \( q \ge 1 \), and \( m \) and \( q \) are unique. *Existence*, by @thm-strong-induction-from-base with base 1. Let \( N \ge 1 \), and suppose the claim holds for every \( k \) with \( 1 \le k < N \). By @thm-even-or-odd, \( N \) is odd or even. If \( N \) is odd, take \( m = 0 \), \( q = N \). If \( N = 2N' \), then \( 1 \le N' < N \) (since \( N \ge 1 \) forces \( N' \ge 1 \), by @lem-naturals-arithmetic (a) applied to \( N' \in \nN \)), so \( N' = 2^{m'} q \), and \( N = 2^{m' + 1} q \). *Uniqueness.* Let \( 2^m q = 2^{m'} q' \) with \( q, q' \) odd. The statement is symmetric, so without loss of generality \( m \le m' \). If \( m < m' \), dividing by \( 2^m \) gives \( q = 2 \cdot 2^{m' - m - 1} q' \), which is even, against @thm-even-or-odd ("not both"). So \( m = m' \), and then \( q = q' \). *The bijection.* \( \pi(m, n) \in \nN \), since \( 2^m(2n + 1) \ge 1 \). For \( k \in \nN \), write \( k + 1 = 2^m q \) by the claim, with \( q = 2n + 1 \) and \( n = (q - 1)/2 \in \nN \) (as \( q \ge 1 \)), and set \( \delta(k) \coloneqq (m, n) \). Then \( \pi(\delta(k)) = 2^m q - 1 = k \), and \( \delta(\pi(m, n)) = (m, n) \) by the uniqueness half of the claim, applied to \( N = 2^m(2n + 1) \). By @thm-bijection-iff-inverse, \( \pi \) is a bijection. A table of values: \( \pi(0, 0) = 0 \), \( \pi(1, 0) = 1 \), \( \pi(0, 1) = 2 \), \( \pi(2, 0) = 3 \), \( \pi(0, 2) = 4 \), \( \pi(1, 1) = 5 \); and \( \delta(11) = (2, 1) \), since \( 12 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \).
   - Ledger: cancelling \( 2^m \ne 0 \), \( 2^{a+b} = 2^a 2^b \) (@prp-power-laws), and "an integer \( q \ge 1 \) has \( (q - 1)/2 \in \nN \) when \( q \) is odd" are school arithmetic.
3. **`exm-method-encode`** `[Method: build a bijection by encoding and decoding]`.
   - **Template.** (1) Say what an element of the source must record for nothing to be lost. (2) Choose a code: a rule sending each element to an element of the target, and check that it lands there. (3) Write the decoder: a rule reading an element of the source back from each element of the target, and check that it is a function, with one answer for each input. (4) Check both round trips and cite @thm-bijection-iff-inverse.
   - **When to use it.** The two sets carry the same information arranged differently: two numbers in one, a pair of sequences in one sequence, a function of two variables as a function to functions.
   - **The usual failure.** Checking only one round trip: \( \delta \circ \pi = \id \) alone shows only that \( \pi \) is injective (@thm-left-inverse-iff-injective). And a decoder that is not a function, with two readings of one code or none (`exm-interleaving-decimals`). If the decoder works only on part of the target, keep the injection and use @exm-method-injections-both-ways.
4. **Quick check 3:** "Compute \( \pi(3, 2) \) and \( \delta(47) \)." *Solution:* \( \pi(3, 2) = 8 \cdot 5 - 1 = 39 \). \( 48 = 16 \cdot 3 = 2^4(2 \cdot 1 + 1) \), so \( \delta(47) = (4, 1) \); check: \( \pi(4, 1) = 47 \).

**`## The laws of exponents, for all sets` (~900; method section 2).**
1. Lead (~70): for numbers, \( m^{n+k} = m^n m^k \) and \( (m^n)^k = m^{nk} \). Read through the sets that these numbers count, each law says that two sets of functions are the same size. For sets we cannot compute; we must find the bijection.
2. **`thm-cardinal-laws`** `[Laws of Cardinal Arithmetic]`: "For all sets \( A, B, C, X, Y, Z \):
   (a) \( \card{A \sqcup B} = \card{B \sqcup A} \), \( \card{(A \sqcup B) \sqcup C} = \card{A \sqcup (B \sqcup C)} \), \( \card{A \times B} = \card{B \times A} \) and \( \card{(A \times B) \times C} = \card{A \times (B \times C)} \);
   (b) \( \card{A \times (B \sqcup C)} = \card{(A \times B) \sqcup (A \times C)} \);
   (c) \( X^{\emptyset} \) has exactly one element, and \( \card{X^{\{0\}}} = \card{X} \);
   (d) \( \card{X^{Y \sqcup Z}} = \card{X^Y \times X^Z} \);
   (e) \( \card{(X \times Y)^Z} = \card{X^Z \times Y^Z} \);
   (f) \( \card{(X^Y)^Z} = \card{X^{Y \times Z}} \)."
3. **`[Scratch work]`** for (d) (≤ 20 lines). What is an element of each side? On the left, a function \( h \) defined on the tagged copies of \( Y \) and \( Z \). On the right, a pair \( (f, g) \) with \( f \colon Y \to X \) and \( g \colon Z \to X \). A function on two disjoint pieces is the same as a function on each piece. So cut \( h \) into its two pieces by composing with the inclusions, and glue a pair back together piece by piece.

   | Givens | Goal |
   |---|---|
   | \( h \in X^{Y \sqcup Z} \) | an element of \( X^Y \times X^Z \): \( (h \circ \iota_Y, h \circ \iota_Z) \) |
   | \( (f, g) \in X^Y \times X^Z \) | one \( h \) with \( h \circ \iota_Y = f \), \( h \circ \iota_Z = g \) |
   | gap | \( h(y, 0) \coloneqq f(y) \), \( h(z, 1) \coloneqq g(z) \) is a function: the parts do not overlap (`def-disjoint-union`, unwinding) |

4. **Proof** of (d) and (f) on the main line, (a)–(c) and (e) in the fold.
   - (d) \( \Phi(h) \coloneqq (h \circ \iota_Y, h \circ \iota_Z) \). \( \Psi(f, g) \coloneqq h \), where \( h(y, 0) = f(y) \) and \( h(z, 1) = g(z) \). This is a function on \( Y \sqcup Z \) because every element has exactly one of the two forms. \( \Phi(\Psi(f, g)) = (f, g) \): \( h(\iota_Y(y)) = h(y, 0) = f(y) \), and likewise for \( g \), by @prp-function-equality and @def-ordered-pair. \( \Psi(\Phi(h)) = h \): both agree at every \( (y, 0) \) and every \( (z, 1) \), which are all the points. By @thm-bijection-iff-inverse, \( \Phi \) is a bijection.
   - (f) For \( g \in (X^Y)^Z \), each \( g(z) \) is a function \( Y \to X \); let \( \Phi(g)(y, z) \coloneqq g(z)(y) \), a function \( Y \times Z \to X \). For \( h \in X^{Y \times Z} \) let \( \Psi(h)(z) \coloneqq (y \mapsto h(y, z)) \), an element of \( X^Y \) for each \( z \), so \( \Psi(h) \in (X^Y)^Z \). \( \Phi(\Psi(h))(y, z) = \Psi(h)(z)(y) = h(y, z) \). And \( \Psi(\Phi(g))(z) \) is the function \( y \mapsto g(z)(y) \), which is \( g(z) \) (@prp-function-equality, used twice: first in \( Y \), then in \( Z \)). By @thm-bijection-iff-inverse, \( \Phi \) is a bijection.
   - **Fold F3.6-a** (`::: {.second-reading title="The other laws"}`, ~350; holds no cited statement): the maps, with the inverse of each. (a) \( (a, 0) \mapsto (a, 1) \), \( (b, 1) \mapsto (b, 0) \); \( ((a, 0), 0) \mapsto (a, 0) \), \( ((b, 1), 0) \mapsto ((b, 0), 1) \), \( (c, 1) \mapsto ((c, 1), 1) \); \( (a, b) \mapsto (b, a) \); \( ((a, b), c) \mapsto (a, (b, c)) \). (b) \( (a, (b, 0)) \mapsto ((a, b), 0) \), \( (a, (c, 1)) \mapsto ((a, c), 1) \). (c) \( X^{\emptyset} = \{\text{the empty function}\} \) by @prp-empty-function; \( f \mapsto f(0) \) is a bijection \( X^{\{0\}} \to X \), with inverse \( x \mapsto \) the constant function \( 0 \mapsto x \). (e) \( h \mapsto (p_1 \circ h, p_2 \circ h) \), with \( p_1(x, y) = x \), \( p_2(x, y) = y \); inverse \( (f, g) \mapsto (z \mapsto (f(z), g(z))) \). Each round trip is a line of @prp-function-equality and @def-ordered-pair. These are also exercises in B2.
5. Aftermath (~90): (f) is called **currying**; it turns a function of two variables into a function whose values are functions. For finite sets with \( m, n, k \) elements the theorem gives back \( m^{n+k} = m^n m^k \) and \( (m^n)^k = m^{nk} \), and this time without induction. The induction of 2.3 has moved into the counts of Section 2.5. The point of the theorem is that nothing in its proof used finiteness.
6. **`exm-method-laws-by-bijection`** `[Method: prove a law of sizes by a bijection of sets]`.
   - **Template.** (1) Write out what an element of each side is: a tagged element, a pair, a function, a function with function values. (2) Find the map that only regroups: compose with the inclusions, pair the coordinates, or curry. (3) Write the reverse regrouping. (4) Check both round trips with @prp-function-equality and @def-ordered-pair, and cite @thm-bijection-iff-inverse.
   - **When to use it.** A law familiar from numbers, such as \( m^{n+k} = m^n m^k \), is claimed for sets of any size; or a size is to be computed by rewriting the set step by step.
   - **The usual failure.** Computing with the symbols, as in "\( \card{X}^{\card{Y} + \card{Z}} = \card{X}^{\card{Y}} \card{X}^{\card{Z}} \)", when no number \( \card{X} \) exists and the step is the law itself (N9). Also dropping the tags: with \( \cup \) in place of \( \sqcup \) the law fails (B4 (b)).
7. **Quick check 4:** "Let \( X = \{0, 1\} \), \( Y = \{a\} \), \( Z = \{b, c\} \). How many elements has each side of (d)? Let \( h(a, 0) = 1 \), \( h(b, 1) = 0 \), \( h(c, 1) = 1 \). What is \( \Phi(h) \)?" *Solution:* \( Y \sqcup Z \) has 3 elements, so \( 2^3 = 8 \); \( X^Y \times X^Z \) has \( 2 \cdot 4 = 8 \). \( \Phi(h) = (f, g) \) with \( f(a) = 1 \), \( g(b) = 0 \), \( g(c) = 1 \).

**`## Subsets are 0–1 functions` (~450).**
1. Lead (~60): Section 1.5 matched the subsets of \( X \) with the functions \( X \to \{0, 1\} \) and promised the match works for every set. Here it is proved, and it makes \( 2 \) a base like any other.
2. **`thm-power-set-two-power`** `[Subsets and 0–1 Functions]`: "For every set \( X \), the map \( A \mapsto \ind_A \) is a bijection \( \powerset{X} \to \{0, 1\}^X \), with inverse \( f \mapsto \{ x \in X : f(x) = 1 \} \). In particular \( \card{\powerset{X}} = \card{\{0, 1\}^X} \)."
   *Idea* (one line): by the method of @exm-method-encode; a subset records, for each \( x \), one yes-or-no answer.
   *Proof:* \( \ind_A \in \{0, 1\}^X \) (05:61). For \( A \subseteq X \), \( \{ x : \ind_A(x) = 1 \} = A \) by the definition of \( \ind_A \). For \( f \in \{0, 1\}^X \), with \( S \coloneqq \{ x : f(x) = 1 \} \), we get \( \ind_S(x) = 1 = f(x) \) for \( x \in S \), and for \( x \notin S \), \( f(x) \ne 1 \), so \( f(x) = 0 = \ind_S(x) \). So \( \ind_S = f \) (@prp-function-equality). Cite @thm-bijection-iff-inverse.
   Aftermath, one sentence: the earlier uses in 2.5 (after @prp-power-set-count), 3.3 (after @cor-powerset-naturals-uncountable) and 3.4 (Quick check after @prp-card-le-surjection) quoted this correspondence. It is now a theorem to cite.
3. **`cor-cantor-two-power`** `[No Set Is the Size of Its 0–1 Functions]`: "For every set \( X \), \( \card{X} < \card{\{0, 1\}^X} \)." *Proof:* \( x \mapsto \ind_{\{x\}} \) is injective, since \( \ind_{\{x\}} \) takes the value 1 at \( x \) only. A bijection \( X \to \{0, 1\}^X \), composed with the inverse of the bijection of @thm-power-set-two-power, would be a bijection \( X \to \powerset{X} \), and in particular a surjection, against @thm-cantor.
4. Aftermath (one sentence): so \( \nN \), \( \{0,1\}^{\nN} \), \( \{0,1\}^{\{0,1\}^{\nN}} \), … grow strictly at each step, and there is no largest size.

**`## Back to Russell` (~330).**
1. Russell's theorem (Section 1.2) ends: there is no set of which every set is an element. Cantor's theorem proves it again. Suppose \( U \) were such a set. Every subset of \( U \) is a set, hence an element of \( U \): \( \powerset{U} \subseteq U \). Define \( g \colon U \to \powerset{U} \) by \( g(u) \coloneqq u \) if \( u \in \powerset{U} \), and \( g(u) \coloneqq \emptyset \) otherwise. It is onto, since each \( S \in \powerset{U} \) is an element of \( U \) with \( g(S) = S \). This contradicts @thm-cantor.

So \( D \) is, up to the elements that are not subsets of \( U \), Russell's set \( \{ x : x \notin x \} \). "The two arguments of Section 1.2 and Section 3.3 are one argument, seen once as a statement about membership and once as a statement about size." (S2a checks the claim "up to": the elements \( u \notin \powerset{U} \) all lie in \( D \), since \( u \notin \emptyset \), so \( D = \{ u \in U : u \notin \powerset{U} \} \cup \{ u \in \powerset{U} : u \notin u \} \).)

**`## The size of the line` (~800).**
1. Lead (~60): the hook asked about \( \nR \times \nR \). First \( \nR \) itself. A real number in \( [0, 1) \) is a digit sequence, and a digit sequence is a function \( \nZ_{>0} \to \{0, \dots, 9\} \). So \( \nR \) should be a power: \( \{0,\dots,9\}^{\nN} \), and that should be a power of 2.
2. **`thm-reals-power-set-naturals`** `[The Line Is a Power of Two]`: "\( \card{\nR} = \card{\{0, 1\}^{\nN}} = \card{\powerset{\nN}} \)."
3. *Idea* (three lines): injections both ways (@exm-method-injections-both-ways). Into the 0–1 sequences, the digits are encoded and the bookkeeping is left to the laws. Out of them, each 0–1 sequence becomes digits 1 and 2, which avoid the double expansions.
4. *Proof.* Let \( T \coloneqq \{0, 1, \dots, 9\} \) and \( D \coloneqq T^{\nZ_{>0}} \), the digit sequences (@def-decimal-expansion).
   - *\( \card{[0, 1)} \le \card{D} \).* Send \( x \) to its expansion not ending in all 9s. It exists by (D1), and is unique by (D3), as in the proof of @thm-reals-uncountable-decimal, so this is a function (no choice). It is injective by the uniqueness in (D2): a digit sequence is an expansion of only one number.
   - *\( \card{D} \le \card{\{0, 1\}^{\nN}} \).* \( n \mapsto n + 1 \) is a bijection \( \nN \to \nZ_{>0} \), so \( \card{D} = \card{T^{\nN}} \) (@prp-card-operations-well-defined (c)). The map \( t \mapsto \ind_{\{t\}} \) is an injection \( T \to \{0, 1\}^T \), so \( \card{T^{\nN}} \le \card{(\{0, 1\}^T)^{\nN}} \) (@prp-card-operations-monotone (c); \( \{0, 1\}^T \ne \emptyset \)). Then \( \card{(\{0, 1\}^T)^{\nN}} = \card{\{0, 1\}^{T \times \nN}} \) (@thm-cardinal-laws (f)). Finally \( (t, n) \mapsto 10n + t \) is a bijection \( T \times \nN \to \nN \): its inverse is quotient and remainder by 10 (@thm-division-algorithm, the quotient in \( \nN \) as in `exm-two-copies-of-naturals`). So \( \card{\{0, 1\}^{T \times \nN}} = \card{\{0, 1\}^{\nN}} \) (@prp-card-operations-well-defined (c)). Composing the bijections and the injection gives an injection \( D \to \{0, 1\}^{\nN} \) (@thm-composition-injective-surjective (a)).
   - *\( \card{\{0, 1\}^{\nN}} \le \card{[0, 1)} \).* For \( b \in \{0, 1\}^{\nN} \), let \( e_{k+1} \coloneqq 1 + b(k) \) for \( k \in \nN \). This is a digit sequence with digits 1 and 2 only. By (D2) it is the expansion of exactly one \( y_b \in [0, 1] \), and \( y_b \le e_1/10 + 1/10 \le 3/10 \), so \( y_b \in [0, 1) \). If \( b \ne b' \), the two digit sequences differ, and neither contains 0 or 9. By the consequence of (D3) stated in Section 3.3 (a digit sequence with no 0 and no 9 is the only expansion of its number), \( y_b \ne y_{b'} \).
   - *Conclusion.* By @prp-card-le-preorder (b) and @thm-schroder-bernstein, \( \card{[0, 1)} = \card{\{0, 1\}^{\nN}} \). By @exm-reals-open-interval, \( \card{[0, 1)} = \card{\nR} \). By @thm-power-set-two-power, \( \card{\{0, 1\}^{\nN}} = \card{\powerset{\nN}} \). Chain with @prp-equinumerous-properties.
5. Aftermath (~60): Exercise C1 of Section 3.4 reached \( \card{\nR} = \card{\powerset{\nN}} \) with a hand-built injection into \( \powerset{\nN} \). Here the laws did the bookkeeping, and each step is one line. Neither proof needed binary expansions of real numbers.
6. **Remark** `[Notation in other books]` (~110): "Many books treat sizes as objects, called **cardinal numbers**, and write \( \aleph_0 \) for the size of \( \nN \) and \( 2^{\aleph_0} \), or \( \mathfrak c \), for the size of \( \nR \). Making a size into an object needs more set theory than this book uses (Section 2.5, after @prp-equinumerous-properties). Here '\( \card{A} = 2^{\aleph_0} \)' can be read only as shorthand for \( \card{A} = \card{\{0, 1\}^{\nN}} \). Whether some subset of \( \nR \) is strictly bigger than \( \nN \) and strictly smaller than \( \nR \) is the **continuum hypothesis**; it is known to be neither provable nor refutable from the usual axioms of set theory." (S2a: check the exact place where 2.5 says that no object \( \card{A} \) is defined, NOTATION row `\card{S}`; cite it by label or section.)
7. **Quick check 5:** "Which step of the proof of @thm-reals-power-set-naturals uses the axiom of choice?" *Solution:* None. The expansion of \( x \) is chosen by a rule ("not ending in all 9s"), every other map is a formula, and @thm-schroder-bernstein was proved without choice (Section 3.4).

**`## Back to the plane` (~840).**
1. **Lead and the almost-correct proof** (`exm-interleaving-decimals` `[Interleaving digits]`, ~300). "The natural bijection \( [0, 1) \times [0, 1) \to [0, 1) \) interleaves digits: if \( x = 0.a_1 a_2 \ldots \) and \( y = 0.b_1 b_2 \ldots \), with the expansions not ending in all 9s, let \( F(x, y) \coloneqq 0.a_1 b_1 a_2 b_2 \ldots \). The inverse splits the digits of \( z \) into odd and even places." Is it a bijection? *Solution:* \( F \) is a function into \( [0, 1) \): the interleaved sequence has infinitely many places where it is not 9, those of \( a \), so it does not end in all 9s, and by (D2) it names one number. That number is less than 1, since the only expansion of 1 is all 9s (S2a: check this follows from @def-decimal-expansion with \( n = 1 \) and induction, or cite the 3.3 text). \( F \) is injective: if \( F(x, y) = F(x', y') = z \), both interleaved sequences are expansions of \( z \) and neither ends in all 9s, so by (D3) they are equal; then \( a = a' \) and \( b = b' \), and \( x = x' \), \( y = y' \) by (D2). But \( F \) is **not** onto. \( z = \frac{1}{11} = 0.090909\ldots \) has digits with no 0 tail and no 9 tail, so this is its only expansion. A preimage \( (x, y) \) would need \( a = 000\ldots \) and \( b = 999\ldots \). But \( b \) was the expansion of \( y \) not ending in all 9s. The splitting "inverse" named "the \( y \in [0, 1) \) whose expansion is \( 999\ldots \)" without checking that it exists, which is (N14) of @fd1:exm-not-allowed-catalogue. (The digits 9, 9, … are an expansion of 1, which is not in \( [0, 1) \).) "So the direct route gives an injection and no bijection. The injection is enough (@exm-method-injections-both-ways), and the laws give the bijection with no digit-fiddling at all." S2a verifies \( \frac{1}{11} = 0.\overline{09} \) via (D2) and @prp-geometric-sum-style truncations.
2. **`thm-plane-equinumerous-line`** `[The Plane Is the Size of the Line]`: "\( \card{\nR \times \nR} = \card{\nR} \)." *Idea:* by the method of @exm-method-laws-by-bijection, rewrite the plane step by step. *Proof:* By @thm-reals-power-set-naturals and @prp-card-operations-well-defined (b), \( \card{\nR \times \nR} = \card{\{0,1\}^{\nN} \times \{0,1\}^{\nN}} \). By @thm-cardinal-laws (d) this is \( \card{\{0,1\}^{\nN \sqcup \nN}} \). By @exm-two-copies-of-naturals and @prp-card-operations-well-defined (c) this is \( \card{\{0,1\}^{\nN}} \), which is \( \card{\nR} \). Chain with @prp-equinumerous-properties. Aftermath (~60): the composite bijection, read through the maps, is the interleaving of two 0–1 sequences of Exercise B3 of Section 3.4. The difficulty with decimals has gone because 0–1 sequences, unlike numbers, have no double expansions; it is paid for once, in @thm-reals-power-set-naturals.
3. **`thm-baire-space-reals`** `[Sequences of Natural Numbers]`: "\( \card{\nN^{\nN}} = \card{\nR} \)." *Proof:* \( \{0, 1\}^{\nN} \to \nN^{\nN} \), reading a 0–1 sequence as a sequence of natural numbers, is injective. \( n \mapsto \ind_{\{n\}} \) is an injection \( \nN \to \{0, 1\}^{\nN} \), so \( \card{\nN^{\nN}} \le \card{(\{0, 1\}^{\nN})^{\nN}} \) (@prp-card-operations-monotone (c)). This is \( \card{\{0, 1\}^{\nN \times \nN}} \) (@thm-cardinal-laws (f)), and that is \( \card{\{0, 1\}^{\nN}} \) by @exm-pairing-bijection and @prp-card-operations-well-defined (c). By @thm-schroder-bernstein, \( \card{\nN^{\nN}} = \card{\{0, 1\}^{\nN}} \), and this is \( \card{\nR} \) (@thm-reals-power-set-naturals). Aftermath (one sentence): B4 (d) of Section 3.4 reached the same equality through graphs and \( \powerset{\nN \times \nN} \).
4. **`cor-real-sequences`** `[Sequences of Real Numbers]`: "\( \card{\nR^{\nN}} = \card{\nR} \)." *Proof:* \( \card{\nR^{\nN}} = \card{(\{0,1\}^{\nN})^{\nN}} = \card{\{0,1\}^{\nN \times \nN}} = \card{\{0,1\}^{\nN}} = \card{\nR} \), by @prp-card-operations-well-defined (c), @thm-cardinal-laws (f), @exm-pairing-bijection with @prp-card-operations-well-defined (c), and @thm-reals-power-set-naturals. No Schröder–Bernstein is needed.
5. Closing (~90): the answers to the hook. The plane is no bigger than the line, and \( \nN^{\nN} \) is no bigger either. One sentence on what is not proved here: for every infinite \( X \), \( \card{X \times X} = \card{X} \) and \( \card{X \sqcup X} = \card{X} \); in general these need Zorn's lemma, and this book does not prove them. (No section pointer, so this is not a forward pointer.)
6. **Quick check 6:** "Is \( \card{\nQ \times \nR} = \card{\nR} \)?" *Solution:* Yes. \( x \mapsto (0, x) \) is an injection \( \nR \to \nQ \times \nR \). The inclusion \( \nQ \times \nR \subseteq \nR \times \nR \) is an injection, and \( \card{\nR \times \nR} = \card{\nR} \) (@thm-plane-equinumerous-line). Apply @thm-schroder-bernstein.

- **A1** (four parts).
  - (a) True or false, for every set \( A \): \( \card{\emptyset \sqcup A} = \card{A} \); \( \emptyset \times A = \emptyset \); \( A^{\emptyset} \) has exactly one element; \( \emptyset^A = \emptyset \).
  - (b) Under the bijection of @thm-cardinal-laws (f) with \( X = Y = \{0, 1\} \) and \( Z = \{0\} \), which element of \( (X^Y)^Z \) corresponds to \( h(y, z) \coloneqq y \)?
  - (c) Give an injection \( \nR \sqcup \nN \to \nR \).
  - (d) In the proof of @thm-reals-power-set-naturals, why must the digits of \( y_b \) avoid 9? Would the digits 0 and 1 have worked as well as 1 and 2?
  - *Solution:* (a) True (\( (a, 1) \mapsto a \)); true (no pair has a first coordinate in \( \emptyset \)); true (@prp-empty-function); false: for \( A = \emptyset \), \( \emptyset^{\emptyset} \) has one element (and the claim holds for every non-empty \( A \)). (b) The \( g \) with \( g(0) = \id_{\{0,1\}} \), since \( g(0)(y) = h(y, 0) = y \). (c) \( (x, 0) \mapsto x/(1 + \abs{x}) \in (-1, 1) \) (@exm-reals-open-interval) and \( (n, 1) \mapsto n + 1 \ge 1 \). Each part is injective, and their values are disjoint. (d) If 9 were allowed, two different digit sequences could name one number, as \( 0.4999\ldots \) and \( 0.5000\ldots \) do, and injectivity would fail. Digits 0 and 1 would also have worked: if two different 0–1 digit sequences named one number, then by (D3) one of them would end in all 9s, and a 0–1 sequence has no 9s. Digits 1 and 2 let the proof quote the consequence of (D3) stated in Section 3.3 word for word.
- **B1** `[B1: Encodings]`. Prove by an explicit bijection and its inverse: (a) \( \card{\nN \times \{0, 1, 2\}} = \card{\nN} \); (b) \( \card{\{0, 1\}^{\nN}} = \card{\{0, 1, 2, 3\}^{\nN}} \); (c) \( \card{\nZ} = \card{\nN} \).
  - *Solution:* (a) \( (n, r) \mapsto 3n + r \), with inverse quotient and remainder by 3 (@thm-division-algorithm, the quotient in \( \nN \)). Round trips by the uniqueness in the division algorithm. (b) \( \Phi(c)(n) \coloneqq 2c(2n) + c(2n + 1) \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\} \). Inverse: for \( d \), write \( d(n) = 2q_n + r_n \) with \( q_n, r_n \in \{0, 1\} \) (division by 2, unique), and set \( c(2n) \coloneqq q_n \), \( c(2n + 1) \coloneqq r_n \). This defines \( c \) on all of \( \nN \), since each \( m \) is \( 2n \) or \( 2n + 1 \) for exactly one \( n \). Round trips by uniqueness and @prp-function-equality. (c) \( e(n) \coloneqq 2n \) for \( n \ge 0 \), \( e(n) \coloneqq -2n - 1 \) for \( n < 0 \). Inverse: \( m = 2q \mapsto q \), \( m = 2q + 1 \mapsto -q - 1 \). Check both round trips, the signs included.
- **B2** `[B2: The other laws]`. Prove @thm-cardinal-laws (b) and (e) by the method of @exm-method-laws-by-bijection, and show \( \card{X^{\{0, 1\}}} = \card{X \times X} \) for every set \( X \).
  - *Solution:* (b), (e) with the maps of F3.6-a, both round trips written out. For the last: \( f \mapsto (f(0), f(1)) \), inverse \( (x, x') \mapsto \) the function \( 0 \mapsto x \), \( 1 \mapsto x' \); or from (c) and (d) of the theorem, since \( \{0, 1\} \) is the same size as \( \{0\} \sqcup \{0\} \) (by \( 0 \mapsto (0, 0) \), \( 1 \mapsto (0, 1) \)).
- **B3** `[B3: Sets the size of the line]`. Prove: (a) \( \card{\nN \times \nR} = \card{\nR} \); (b) \( \card{\nR \sqcup \nR} = \card{\nR} \); (c) \( \card{\nR^k} = \card{\nR} \) for every integer \( k \ge 1 \).
  - *Solution:* (a) \( x \mapsto (0, x) \) injects \( \nR \) into \( \nN \times \nR \subseteq \nR \times \nR \); apply @thm-plane-equinumerous-line and @thm-schroder-bernstein. (b) \( \nR \sqcup \nR = \nR \times \{0, 1\} \) (unwind `def-disjoint-union`), a subset of \( \nR \times \nR \); and \( x \mapsto (x, 0) \) injects the other way. (c) Induction on \( k \) (@thm-induction from 1). \( k = 1 \) is trivial. \( \nR^{k+1} \) is the same size as \( \nR^k \times \nR \) by \( (x_1, \dots, x_{k+1}) \mapsto ((x_1, \dots, x_k), x_{k+1}) \), so it is the same size as \( \nR \times \nR \) by the hypothesis and @prp-card-operations-well-defined (b), and that is the size of \( \nR \). (S2a: match the k-fold product to Section 3.1's definition in @cor-product-countable (b).)
- **B4** `[B4: Prove or disprove]`. "Prove each true statement and refute each false one with a counterexample. Here \( A, B, C, X, Y, Y', Z \) are sets."
  - (a) If \( \card{A \sqcup C} = \card{B \sqcup C} \), then \( \card{A} = \card{B} \).
  - (b) \( \card{X^{Y \cup Z}} = \card{X^Y \times X^Z} \).
  - (c) If \( \card{Y} \le \card{Y'} \), then \( \card{X^Y} \le \card{X^{Y'}} \).
  - (d) \( \card{\{0, 1\}^{\nN}} = \card{\{0, 1, 2\}^{\nN}} \).
  - (e) If \( \card{A} < \card{B} \) and \( C \ne \emptyset \), then \( \card{A \times C} < \card{B \times C} \).
  - (f) There is a set \( X \) with \( \card{\{0, 1\}^X} \le \card{X} \).
  - *Solution:* (a) False: \( A = \{0\} \), \( B = \emptyset \), \( C = \nN \) (the warning "No subtraction"). (b) False: \( X = \{0, 1\} \), \( Y = Z = \{0\} \); \( X^{Y \cup Z} \) has 2 elements and \( X^Y \times X^Z \) has 4. Finite sets of different sizes are not equinumerous (@cor-finite-size-unique). True when \( Y \cap Z = \emptyset \), by Quick check 1 (c) and (d) of the theorem. (c) False: \( X = \emptyset \), \( Y = \emptyset \), \( Y' = \{0\} \) (the non-example after @prp-card-operations-monotone). True when \( X \ne \emptyset \). (d) True. Inclusion gives \( \le \). Back: \( 0 \mapsto (0, 0) \), \( 1 \mapsto (0, 1) \), \( 2 \mapsto (1, 0) \) injects \( \{0, 1, 2\} \) into \( \{0, 1\}^{\{0, 1\}} \), written as pairs of values. Monotonicity (c) gives \( \card{\{0,1,2\}^{\nN}} \le \card{(\{0,1\}^{\{0,1\}})^{\nN}} \), which is \( \card{\{0,1\}^{\{0,1\} \times \nN}} \) by (f), and that is \( \card{\{0,1\}^{\nN}} \) by \( (r, n) \mapsto 2n + r \). Then apply @thm-schroder-bernstein. (e) False: \( A = \{0\} \), \( B = \{0, 1\} \), \( C = \nN \). \( \card{\{0\} \times \nN} = \card{\nN} = \card{\{0, 1\} \times \nN} \), the latter by @exm-two-copies-of-naturals, since \( \{0, 1\} \times \nN \) and \( \nN \sqcup \nN \) are the same size by \( (r, n) \mapsto (n, r) \). (f) False: \( x \mapsto \ind_{\{x\}} \) gives \( \card{X} \le \card{\{0, 1\}^X} \), so \( \card{\{0, 1\}^X} \le \card{X} \) would give equality by @thm-schroder-bernstein, against @cor-cantor-two-power.
- **C1** `[C1: Functions from the line to the line]`. "Prove that \( \card{\nR^{\nR}} = \card{\powerset{\nR}} \). Deduce that there is no surjection \( \nR \to \nR^{\nR} \)."
  - *Hints:* 1. "Replace the base \( \nR \) by \( \{0, 1\}^{\nN} \), then curry." 2. "You will need \( \card{\nN \times \nR} = \card{\nR} \)."
  - *Solution:* Let \( \beta \colon \nR \to \{0, 1\}^{\nN} \) be a bijection (@thm-reals-power-set-naturals). Then \( \card{\nR^{\nR}} = \card{(\{0,1\}^{\nN})^{\nR}} \) (@prp-card-operations-well-defined (c), with \( \beta \) and \( \id_{\nR} \)), which is \( \card{\{0,1\}^{\nN \times \nR}} \) (@thm-cardinal-laws (f)), which is \( \card{\{0,1\}^{\nR}} \) (@exr-cardinal-arithmetic-b3 (a), with @prp-card-operations-well-defined (c)), which is \( \card{\powerset{\nR}} \) (@thm-power-set-two-power). For the second part, let \( \Theta \colon \nR^{\nR} \to \powerset{\nR} \) be a bijection. If \( s \colon \nR \to \nR^{\nR} \) were onto, \( \Theta \circ s \) would be onto (@thm-composition-injective-surjective (b)), against @thm-cantor. So there are more functions \( \nR \to \nR \) than real numbers.
  - *Marking scheme* (10): base replaced, with the citation 2; currying named and applied in the right order (\( (X^Y)^Z \), \( Y = \nN \), \( Z = \nR \)) 3; \( \card{\nN \times \nR} = \card{\nR} \) used with the well-definedness of powers in the exponent 2; power set 1; no surjection, by composing with Cantor 2. Fatal: computing with symbols as if sizes were numbers ("\( \mathfrak c^{\mathfrak c} = 2^{\aleph_0 \mathfrak c} \)") with no bijection behind each step.
- **C2** `[C2: Find the error]` (false conclusion). "Claim: \( \{0, 1\}^{\nN} \) is countable. 'Proof': Send \( b \) to the natural number \( N(b) \coloneqq \sum_k b(k) 2^k \). Different sequences have different binary digits, so \( N \) is injective, and an injection into \( \nN \) makes a set countable (@thm-countable-characterizations)." Is the conclusion true? Where does the argument fail? What does it prove?
  - *Solution:* The conclusion is false (@thm-binary-sequences-uncountable). The error is in the first line: \( N(b) \) is named before it is shown to exist, (N14). The sum is infinite whenever \( b \) has infinitely many 1s, and Section 2.3 defines only finite sums (@def-sum-product); no natural number is "\( 1 + 2 + 4 + \cdots \)". Restricted to the sequences that are eventually 0, where the sum is finite (\( \sum_{k < K} b(k) 2^k \) for any \( K \) past the last 1), the map is defined and injective. So that set is countable, which is review exercise R4, now in this section. The "uniqueness of binary digits" also needs proof, and R1 gives only existence. A complete answer names (N14), gives a sequence with infinitely many 1s, and states what the argument does prove.
  - *Marking scheme* (6): false, with the citation 1; the step named, with (N14) and the infinite sum 2; a witness such as the all-1 sequence 1; what survives (eventually zero sequences) 1; uniqueness of binary digits flagged as unproved 1.
- **C3** `[C3: Rearrangements of ℕ]`. "Let \( B \) be the set of bijections \( \nN \to \nN \). Prove that \( \card{B} = \card{\nR} \)."
  - *Solution:* \( B \subseteq \nN^{\nN} \), so \( \card{B} \le \card{\nR} \) (@thm-baire-space-reals). Back: for \( b \in \{0, 1\}^{\nN} \), \( \pi_b \) swaps \( 2n \) and \( 2n + 1 \) when \( b(n) = 1 \) and fixes them when \( b(n) = 0 \). It is its own inverse, hence in \( B \), and \( b \mapsto \pi_b \) is injective, since \( b(n) = 1 \) exactly when \( \pi_b(2n) = 2n + 1 \) (the construction of @exr-diagonal-arguments-c2, which an exercise may cite). By @thm-schroder-bernstein and @thm-reals-power-set-naturals, \( \card{B} = \card{\nR} \).
