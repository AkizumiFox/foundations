# Lines, Hyperplanes, and Convex Sets

Chapter 9 gave \( \nR^n \) a distance. This chapter asks how much of the familiar geometry that distance already decides. A great deal: lines and segments, spheres and balls are all sets cut out by distances, and "\( x \) lies between \( p \) and \( q \)" turns out to be a statement about three distances and nothing else. One linear equation \( \inner{a}{x} = c \) cuts out a hyperplane, and the distance from a point to it has a formula. A set is convex when it contains the segment between any two of its points, and the smallest convex set containing a given set is the set of its convex combinations.

Analysis uses all of this from its first chapters on: the mean value theorem moves along a segment, a ball is the set of points where an estimate holds, and in a convex set an argument made along one segment works between any two points.

## Roadmap

- **Lines and Segments.** Lines \( p + \nR v \) and when two parametrizations give the same line; two points determine a line; meeting points and skew lines, found by giving each line its own parameter; segments \( [p, q] \); and the theorem that \( x \) lies on \( [p, q] \) exactly when \( \norm{p - x} + \norm{x - q} = \norm{p - q} \) (@thm-betweenness), proved from the equality case of the triangle inequality, so that a point of a segment is pinned by its distances to the two ends.
- **Hyperplanes and Nearest Points.** Hyperplanes \( H_{a,c} = \{x : \inner{a}{x} = c\} \) and their half-spaces, with a normal vector fixed only up to a non-zero factor; the lines of \( \nR^2 \) as its hyperplanes; the points equidistant from \( p \) and \( q \) as a hyperplane, with the two sides read off; nearest points on a line and on a hyperplane, with the distance \( \abs{\inner{a}{x} - c}/\norm{a} \) (@prp-nearest-point-on-hyperplane); spheres, and how a line meets one in none, one or two points; and a segment crossing a hyperplane exactly once.
- **Convex Sets and Hulls.** Convex sets and the open and closed balls, which are convex (@prp-balls-convex); lines, segments, hyperplanes and half-spaces as convex sets, and intersections of convex sets; the warning that containing every midpoint is not enough; convex combinations, which a convex set contains; the convex hull of a set as the set of convex combinations of its points (@thm-convex-hull-combinations-rn); star-shaped sets; and review exercises on a cube between two balls, on quadratics that are non-negative on \( [0, 1] \), and on discs in \( \nC \).

## Named moves

- **Parametrize and solve** (@exm-method-parametrize). Give each line or segment its own parameter, write one equation per coordinate, solve, and check every equation, with the range \( [0, 1] \) for a segment. Named in Section 10.1, where it decides whether two lines in space meet; it shortens to an Idea in Section 10.2, where a line meets a sphere at the parameters solving one quadratic equation.
- **Use the equality case** (@exm-method-equality-case). When a known inequality holds with equality, write its equality condition with a named multiplier and solve. Named in Section 10.1, where it proves that a point with \( \norm{p - x} + \norm{x - q} = \norm{p - q} \) lies on \( [p, q] \); Exercise B3 of Section 10.3 uses it again, at Idea level, to show that the points strictly between two points of a sphere lie inside it.
- **The two-point test** (@exm-method-two-point-test). To prove a set convex, take two of its points and \( t \in [0, 1] \), split \( (1 - t)p + tq \) into \( (1 - t)(\dots) + t(\dots) \), and finish by the triangle inequality or by linearity. Named in Section 10.3, where it shows that balls are convex; Exercise B2 there runs it for sums of convex sets, and review exercise R2 offers it as a second route.
- **Peel off the last point.** Convexity speaks about two points at a time, so a statement about \( k + 1 \) points is reduced to one about \( k \) points and a segment. It proves in Section 10.3 that a convex set contains every convex combination of its points (@thm-convex-contains-combinations).

## Threads

- **Betweenness by distances.** Section 10.1 says "\( x \) lies on \( [p, q] \)" using distances alone (@thm-betweenness). The equality case behind it belongs to the Euclidean distance: after Exercise B3 of Section 10.3, the square \( \{\max(\abs{x_1}, \abs{x_2}) \le 1\} \) has a whole segment in its edge, so the analogue of that exercise fails for the distance that measures by the largest coordinate. *Real Numbers, Metric Spaces, and Derivatives* takes the distance-only form to sets with any distance, and *Affine and Projective Geometry* uses it to show that distance-preserving maps keep segments.
- **Convex and star-shaped sets.** Balls are convex (Section 10.3), and convex sets are star-shaped about each of their points. *Real Numbers, Metric Spaces, and Derivatives* joins points of a ball by segments when it studies connectedness, *Complex Analysis* proves Cauchy's theorem first on discs and convex sets, where a primitive is built by integrating along segments, and *Analysis on Manifolds* proves the Poincaré lemma on star-shaped sets.

## Summary

A line in \( \nR^n \) is \( p + \nR v \) with \( v \ne 0 \), and two distinct points lie on exactly one line (@prp-line-through-two-points). A point lies on the segment \( [p, q] \) exactly when its distances to the ends add up to \( \norm{p - q} \), and then those two distances fix it (@thm-betweenness, @cor-point-pinned-on-segment). A hyperplane \( H_{a,c} \) determines its normal vector up to a non-zero factor (@prp-hyperplane-normals); the points equidistant from \( p \ne q \) form a hyperplane (@prp-perpendicular-bisector); and every point has exactly one nearest point on a line and on a hyperplane, with explicit formulas (@prp-nearest-point-on-line, @prp-nearest-point-on-hyperplane). A line meets a sphere in zero, one or two points according to its distance from the center (@prp-line-meets-sphere). Balls, lines, segments, hyperplanes and half-spaces are convex, and so is any intersection of convex sets (@prp-balls-convex, @prp-convex-examples, @prp-convex-operations). The convex hull of a set is the set of convex combinations of its points (@thm-convex-hull-combinations-rn).

What is open: the words "open ball" and "closed ball" are only names here. *Real Numbers, Metric Spaces, and Derivatives* explains them, defines balls for any distance, and starts the analysis that uses this chapter's geometry.

## Terms

- **closed ball**, **open ball**: \( \overline{B}(c, r) = \{x : \norm{x - c} \le r\} \) and \( B(c, r) = \{x : \norm{x - c} < r\} \), for \( r > 0 \) (@def-ball-rn).
- **collinear points**: points that all lie on one line (@def-collinear).
- **convex combination**: a sum \( \lambda_1 p_1 + \dots + \lambda_k p_k \) with every \( \lambda_i \ge 0 \) and \( \lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_k = 1 \) (@def-convex-combination-rn).
- **convex hull**: the intersection of all convex sets containing a set \( S \), written \( \operatorname{conv} S \) (@def-convex-hull-rn).
- **convex set**: a set that contains the segment between any two of its points (@def-convex-set-rn).
- **half-space**: one of the sets \( \{\inner{a}{x} \le c\} \), \( \{\inner{a}{x} \ge c\} \) (closed) or \( \{\inner{a}{x} < c\} \), \( \{\inner{a}{x} > c\} \) (open), for \( a \ne 0 \) (@def-hyperplane-rn).
- **hyperplane**: a set \( H_{a,c} = \{x \in \nR^n : \inner{a}{x} = c\} \) with \( a \ne 0 \) (@def-hyperplane-rn).
- **line**: a set \( p + \nR v = \{p + tv : t \in \nR\} \) with \( v \ne 0 \); \( t \mapsto p + tv \) is a parametrization of it (@def-line-rn).
- **normal vector**: the vector \( a \) in \( H_{a,c} \), fixed by the hyperplane only up to a non-zero factor (@def-hyperplane-rn).
- **segment**: \( [p, q] = \{(1 - t)p + tq : t \in [0, 1]\} \), with midpoint \( (p + q)/2 \) (@def-segment-rn).
- **sphere**: \( S(c, r) = \{x : \norm{x - c} = r\} \), for \( r > 0 \) (@def-sphere-rn).
- **star-shaped set**: a set \( S \) with a point \( c \in S \) such that \( [c, x] \subseteq S \) for every \( x \in S \) (@def-star-shaped).
