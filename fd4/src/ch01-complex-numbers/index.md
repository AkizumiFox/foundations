# Complex Numbers

No real number squares to \( -1 \): in an ordered field a non-zero square is positive (@fd3:prp-ordered-ring-rules (e)), so \( x^2 + 1 = 0 \) has no solution in \( \nR \). The usual remedy, "invent a symbol \( i \) with \( i^2 = -1 \)", leaves open why such a symbol is safe when "a number \( z \) with \( 0 \cdot z = 1 \)" is not. This chapter does not invent anything. It takes pairs of real numbers, objects the reader already trusts, adds them coordinate by coordinate, and multiplies them by the one formula that \( i^2 = -1 \) would force. Then it checks, law by law, that nothing breaks: the complex numbers \( \nC \) form a field in the sense of *Number Systems: From \(\nN\) to \(\nR\)*, Chapter 2, and they contain \( \nR \) as the pairs \( (a, 0) \). Every later volume that computes with complex numbers, from inner products in linear algebra to roots of unity in algebra, starts from this field.

Two habits of \( \nR \) do not survive the passage. No order on \( \nC \) is compatible with its arithmetic, and no choice of "the" square root of each complex number keeps the rule \( \sqrt{x}\sqrt{y} = \sqrt{xy} \). One new habit takes the place of order: measure a complex number by its modulus, a non-negative real, and compute with \( \abs{u}^2 = u\conj{u} \), which is algebra. The first four sections need nothing but the real numbers, and they reach far: division, the triangle inequality, and square roots and the quadratic formula without a single angle. The picture of multiplication as a turn and a stretch does need angles, and angles need cosine and sine, which belong to analysis. The chapter borrows exactly five facts about them, says so in one place, and derives everything else from those five: the familiar special values, De Moivre's formula, the \( n \) different \( n \)-th roots of every non-zero complex number, and the roots of unity. It ends with a look ahead at the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, which *Algebraic Preliminaries*, Chapter 4 proves without the borrowed facts.

## Roadmap

- **The Complex Numbers as Pairs of Reals.** \( \nC \) defined as \( \nR \times \nR \) with the product that \( i^2 = -1 \) would force; the real numbers inside \( \nC \) as the pairs \( (a, 0) \), which makes \( a + bi \) honest notation and \( i^2 = -1 \) a computation; a product with one sign changed, which has zero divisors; the laws of arithmetic, which make \( \nC \) a commutative ring with \( 1 \ne 0 \); powers and their laws in any commutative ring; and the factorization of \( z^n - w^n \).
- **Conjugate, Modulus, and Division.** The conjugate and the modulus, a non-negative real square root and the place where the completeness of \( \nR \) enters; the modulus as an extension of the absolute value of \( \nR \); the rules for conjugate and modulus, with \( \abs{zw} = \abs{z}\abs{w} \) proved by squaring; the inverse \( \conj{z}/\abs{z}^2 \) of a non-zero \( z \); the theorem that \( \nC \) is a field in which \( \nR \) is closed under the field operations; and division by the conjugate of the denominator.
- **The Triangle Inequality.** Why the trapping proof for ordered fields cannot run in \( \nC \); comparing squares of non-negative reals; real and imaginary parts at most the modulus; the triangle inequality, by expanding \( \abs{z + w}^2 \) and recognizing the cross term as a real part; and three variants: the reverse inequality, the inequality for \( n \) terms, and a lower bound for the modulus of a sum.
- **Square Roots, Quadratic Equations, and the Missing Order.** An explicit formula for a square root of a complex number, built from two real square roots, and the count of exactly two square roots of every non-zero number; the quadratic formula with complex coefficients; the proof that no order makes \( \nC \) an ordered field; and why no choice of square roots keeps the product rule \( \sqrt{z}\sqrt{w} = \sqrt{zw} \).
- **The Unit Circle and Polar Form.** The unit circle \( \nT \), closed under products and inverses, and every non-zero number as a length times a direction in exactly one way, all without angles; the five borrowed facts about cosine and sine; \( e^{i\theta} \) as an abbreviation for \( \cos\theta + i\sin\theta \), multiplication as "multiply moduli, add angles", and polar form, unique up to a multiple of \( 2\pi \) in the argument; De Moivre's formula; and the values of cosine and sine at \( \pi/6 \), \( \pi/4 \), \( \pi/3 \), \( \pi/2 \) and \( \pi \), derived from the five facts.
- **Higher Roots, Roots of Unity, and a Look Ahead.** The unique non-negative \( n \)-th root of a non-negative real, constructed as a supremum; exactly \( n \) solutions of \( z^n = c \) for every non-zero \( c \), listed by polar form; the roots of unity, the set \( \mu_n \), and the sums of their powers; a look ahead at the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra; the conjugate of a solution of an equation with real coefficients as a second solution; and review exercises on the maps of \( \nC \) to itself that respect sums, products and \( 1 \) and send \( \nR \) into \( \nR \), on \( \nZ/n\nZ \) and \( \mu_n \), on the triangle inequality of an ordered field, and on the equation \( \conj{z} = z^2 \).

## Named moves

- **Compare real and imaginary parts.** One equation between complex numbers is two equations between real numbers: for real \( a, b, c, d \), \( a + bi = c + di \) if and only if \( a = c \) and \( b = d \). It is named in Section 1.1, where it proves the laws of arithmetic; Section 1.4 uses it to turn \( w^2 = z \) into two real equations, and Section 1.5 to turn \( e^{i\theta} = 1 \) into \( \cos\theta = 1 \) and \( \sin\theta = 0 \). The warning of Section 1.1 marks the hypothesis it needs: the four numbers must be real. Exercise C1 of Section 1.1 proves the same uniqueness of parts in any field that contains \( \nR \) and a square root of \( -1 \).
- **Square the modulus.** The modulus is a square root, and square roots do not expand; its square \( \abs{u}^2 = u\conj{u} \) is a product, and products do. So square, expand with conjugates, bound or compare the real numbers that appear, and take the non-negative square root back, which is legitimate because non-negative reals compare as their squares do. Section 1.2 uses it first, for \( \abs{zw} = \abs{z}\abs{w} \); Section 1.3 names it and runs it in full for the triangle inequality. Exercise B2 of Section 1.3 leaves the last step to you, and Exercise C2 of Section 1.3 the last half.
- **Multiply moduli, add angles.** In polar form a product is the product of the moduli and the sum of the arguments, so an equation \( z^n = c \) splits into a real equation \( r^n = \rho \) for the length and an angle condition \( n\theta \in \alpha + 2\pi\nZ \). It is named in Section 1.5, after the multiplication theorem, and gives De Moivre's formula there; Section 1.6 uses it to solve \( z^n = c \) and to count the solutions. The warning of Section 1.6 shows what goes wrong when the angle condition is read as an equation \( n\theta = \alpha \): all roots but one are lost.
