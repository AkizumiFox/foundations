# Preface

*Number Systems: From \(\nN\) to \(\nR\)* ended with the real numbers as a complete ordered field. This volume supplies what both analysis and algebra use on their first pages without stopping to prove it. Its first three chapters are common ground: finite sums, the binomial theorem, inequalities and real powers; the arithmetic of the integers, divisibility and congruences; and the complex numbers, built on those real numbers. From there two branches go their own ways. Chapters 4–7 build the algebra that linear algebra and the algebra volumes assume: fields as objects of their own, groups through permutations, polynomials as formal objects, and the arithmetic of polynomials over a field, ending with the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra. Chapters 8–10 build the tools of analysis: real functions and their graphs, coordinates and vectors in \( \nR^n \), and lines, hyperplanes and convex sets.

**Who it is for.** The reader we have in mind has worked through *Logic and Proof*, *Sets and Functions* and the main line of *Number Systems: From \(\nN\) to \(\nR\)*, or has their habits from elsewhere. That means running an induction, checking that an operation on classes is well defined, and proving a statement about every element of a ring from the ring axioms alone. The chapters point back to those volumes wherever they use a definition or a result, with a link, so a reader who is unsure of a step can look it up. Much of the material is familiar in outline from school or from a first linear algebra course; what is new is that every rule is proved, and that nothing is used before it is proved.

**Two ways through.** Read Chapters 1–3 first; each uses the ones before it. A reader heading for linear algebra and algebra then reads Chapters 4–7 in order; a reader heading for analysis reads Chapters 8–10, which use the algebra chapters only for the facts about polynomials of Chapters 6 and 7. Every section has Quick checks with folded answers; answer each one before you unfold it. The exercises of a section come in three groups, A to check understanding, B for practice and C to go deeper, and the last section of each chapter adds R items that reach back to earlier chapters. A chapter's opening page lists what it needs, with links, and the time it takes. Second-reading folds hold edge cases and longer justifications. They can stay closed on a first reading, but they are not optional, since later proofs may use what they contain.

**One borrowed fact.** This volume does not define cosine, sine or \( \pi \): defining them needs limits and power series, which belong to analysis. Section 3.5 states five facts about them, numbered (T1)–(T5), as an assumption, and the analysis volumes prove them. They are used only for polar form and for the roots of complex numbers in Sections 3.5 and 3.6, and each proof that uses one names it. Chapters 4–7 use them only in examples and remarks, and Chapter 7 proves the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra from the square roots of Section 3.4 and the completeness of \( \nR \) alone.

**Time.** The written chapters, 2–7, add up to about 155 hours on the core track and about 220 hours for everything; with Chapters 1 and 8–10 the volume is planned at about 255 and 325 hours.

**What comes next.** *Analysis on the Real Line* uses Chapters 1, 3 and 8–10. The linear algebra volumes, beginning with *Spaces and Maps*, take their scalars from a field and use the complex numbers, polynomials and their roots throughout: Chapter 4's fields and subfields, Chapter 6's evaluation of a polynomial, Chapter 7's division with remainder and the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, and Chapter 5's permutations, which *Determinants and the Structure of Operators* needs for the determinant. The algebra volumes start where Chapter 7 stops. *Groups* continues Chapter 5 with cyclic groups, the sign of a permutation, cosets and Lagrange's theorem; *Rings and Fields* continues Chapters 4, 6 and 7 with fraction fields, polynomials over the integers, Gauss's lemma and field extensions. Many of their first definitions are the ones proved here, under the same names.
