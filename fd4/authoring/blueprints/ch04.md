**Purpose.**
- The linear algebra book (§0.8) defined polynomials over a **field** and proved the ring laws by triangular double sums. fd4 needs more and less: more, because F3.5 left ℤ/nℤ as a commutative ring that is not a field, F3.9 used ℚ(t) on school credit, and AL2.6 needs ℤ[x] and reduction mod p; less, because nothing in this chapter needs division. So: polynomials over an arbitrary **commutative ring** R (fd3's `@fd3:def-commutative-ring-and-field`), and every result that is really about fields says which hypothesis it uses (no zero divisors; a unit leading coefficient).
- Build R[x] as a commutative ring; attach degree and leading coefficient; show that R[x] inherits "no zero divisors" from R and that its units are R's; evaluate at an element and, more generally, **substitute** along a ring homomorphism (the universal property, which gives evaluation, reduction of coefficients and composition at once); separate polynomials from polynomial functions; define the formal derivative and prove its rules in every characteristic.
- Keep every label of la §0.8 with its name and its meaning (hypotheses widened from "field F" to "commutative ring R" where the proof allows; every la citation stays valid). Prepare F4.5 (division, gcd, roots over a field), LA1 (F[x] and F[x]_{≤n} as vector spaces, the derivative as a linear map, polynomials of a matrix) and AL2 (polynomial rings over a ring).

**Signature moves** (`index.md`, "Named moves").
- **Compare coefficients.** Two polynomials are equal iff all coefficients agree; to prove an identity in R[x], compute the k-th coefficient of both sides (F3.2's "reduce to the level below", now with sequences).
- **Check on monomials, extend by additivity.** A map that is additive in each argument is determined by its values on monomials a·x^n; so two such maps that agree on monomials agree everywhere (`lem-check-on-monomials`). (Faded triple.)
- **Look at the top coefficient.** The coefficient of x^{deg p + deg q} in pq is lc(p)·lc(q); everything about degrees of products follows from asking whether that one element can be 0.

## index.md

- **Story.** At school a polynomial is "an expression in x", and x is a number not yet chosen. Over 𝔽_2 this breaks at once: x² + x is 0 at both elements, yet we want it to have degree 2. So a polynomial is its list of coefficients, and x is a name for a position. From that one decision the chapter builds a ring R[x] over any commutative ring R, proves its laws with one device (check on monomials, extend by additivity), attaches the degree, and recovers "plugging in" as a homomorphism. Which school rules survive depends on R: over ℤ/6ℤ the degree of a product can drop, over ℤ/4ℤ the polynomial 1 + 2x has an inverse, over 𝔽_p the derivative of x^p is 0. The chapter ends where F4.5 begins: R = F a field, ready for division.
- **What you need:** fd3 F3.2 (commutative rings, multiples n·a), F3.5 (ℤ/nℤ, units, ideals and quotient rings, the field ℤ/pℤ), F4.2 (fields, 𝔽_p, characteristic); fd2 Chapter 1 (functions and their equality), §2.3 (recursion; finite sums and powers, as templates); fd1 Chapter 3 (induction). ## 01-polynomials-as-sequences.md — Polynomials as Coefficient Sequences

- **Hook.** Over 𝔽_2, the expressions x² + x and 0 give the same value at both points (0² + 0 = 0, 1² + 1 = 0, by `@exm-finite-fields`). If polynomials were their functions, x² + x would be 0 and have no degree. Decide instead that a polynomial **is** its coefficients.
- **`lem-finite-sums-and-powers`** (bookkeeping lemma; proofs in a second-reading fold, statements on the main line). Let R be a commutative ring (`@fd3:def-commutative-ring-and-field`).
  - (a) For a sequence (u_i)_{i ∈ ℕ} in R, the sums \( \sum_{i=0}^{k} u_i \) are defined by the recursion of `@fd2:def-sum-product` (used as a pattern: `@fd2:thm-recursion` with X = R, indices from 0, empty sum 0), and depend only on u_0, …, u_k (the "Finite lists" remark there, with its proof, which uses only + on the target set).
  - (b) (Σ1) \( \sum_{i=0}^{k} (u_i + v_i) = \sum u_i + \sum v_i \); (Σ2) \( c \sum_{i=0}^{k} u_i = \sum c u_i \); (Σ3) if u_i = 0 for every i ≠ j, then \( \sum_{i=0}^{k} u_i \) is u_j when j ≤ k and 0 when j > k; (Σ4) −\( \sum u_i = \sum (−u_i) \). These are F4.2's `@lem-ring-finite-sums` (a), (b), (d), (e), cited, not reproved (reconciliation 2026-10-12; F4.4 → F4.2 is an `s` edge).
  - (c) Powers s^0 ≔ 1, s^{n+1} ≔ s^n s (`@fd2:thm-recursion`, the template `@fd2:def-power`) satisfy s^{m+n} = s^m s^n and (st)^n = s^n t^n (template `@fd2:prp-power-laws`).
  - (d) Multiples (F3.2 §03, "Multiples" paragraph; cited by position): (m + n)·a = m·a + n·a, m·(a + b) = m·a + m·b, (m·a)b = m·(ab), (m·1)a = m·a, (mn)·a = m·(n·a). All five are F4.2's `@lem-integer-multiples` (c), (d), stated there for any commutative ring and m, n ∈ ℤ; cited, not reproved (reconciliation 2026-10-12). The purely additive items of (a), (b) and (d) are F4.3's list products and powers in the abelian group (R, +) (F4.3 blueprint: def-group-list-product, prp-group-list-product-laws, prp-group-power-laws); F4.4 has no plan edge to F4.3, and F4.2 already proves them for commutative rings (open question 3).
  - Route: (b) and (d) by citation of F4.2 as above; (a) and (c) (the recursions and the power laws, the same fd2 template ledger line as F4.2 §06; F4.1's prp-power-laws-commutative-ring states the same laws, but F4.4 has no `s` edge to F4.1) each by one induction on one index (`@fd1:ax-induction` through `@fd3:cor-omega-identification`), with (F1)–(F9) at the step. No reordering or regrouping of a double sum is ever needed in this chapter (the design point of §02), so no general associativity/commutativity theorem for finite sums is stated.
- **`def-polynomial`** (la label; widened). Let R be a commutative ring. A **polynomial over R** (in the indeterminate x) is a function p : ℕ → R, written (p_0, p_1, p_2, …), such that **only finitely many** p_k are non-zero: there is N ∈ ℕ with p_k = 0 for every k > N. p_k is the **coefficient of x^k**. For a ∈ R and n ∈ ℕ, the **monomial** a x^n is the polynomial with a in position n and 0 elsewhere; x ≔ 1x^1, and the **constant** a is a x^0. Two polynomials are equal iff p_k = q_k for every k (`@fd2:prp-function-equality`: same domain ℕ, same codomain R). Display convention: \( p = \sum_{k=0}^{N} p_k x^k \) for any admissible N; for now this is notation only (it becomes an honest sum in §02, `prp-polynomial-sum-of-monomials`); zero terms are dropped, a x^1 is written a x, a x^0 is written a.
  - Main-line sentences: why "only finitely many" (evaluation needs finite sums, §04; every non-zero polynomial gets a highest position, §03). The **zero polynomial** 0 = (0, 0, …). If R is the zero ring, the zero polynomial is the only polynomial.
  - **Warning (notation).** p_2 is a coefficient; p(2) will be a value (§04). Over ℤ both are integers, so the book never writes p(k) for a coefficient.
- **`exm-elements-of-rx`** (la label; content changed to rings fd4 has built). Read off the coefficient sequences: 3x² − x + 2 ∈ ℚ[x] → (2, −1, 3, 0, …); x² + x ∈ 𝔽_2[x] → (0, 1, 1, 0, …), **not** the zero sequence; [3]x + [4] ∈ (ℤ/6ℤ)[x] → ([4], [3], [0], …); the constant 7 over ℤ, over ℤ/5ℤ ([2] = [7]) and over 𝔽_2 (1); and 0. Non-example by minimal change: (1, 1, 1, …) ("1 + x + x² + ⋯") fails only the finiteness clause (a formal power series, §02 C2); x^{−1} + 1 is not a sequence indexed by ℕ at all. Prose aside (no claim used): ℝ[x] and ℂ[x] (polynomials with coefficients in Chapter 1's fields) are the linear algebra book's main examples; this chapter's proofs never need them.
- **Main-line remark (where R matters).** Nothing in the definition mentions inverses, order or size: the definition makes sense, word for word, over ℤ/6ℤ, and the chapter keeps track of what each later result needs.
- **Check.** Is 6x² equal to 0 in (ℤ/6ℤ)[x]? (Yes: [6] = [0] in every position.) In ℤ[x]? (No.)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1:** (a) coefficient sequences of x³ − 2x ∈ ℤ[x] and [2]x² + [4]x ∈ (ℤ/4ℤ)[x] ((0, −2, 0, 1, 0, …); ([0], [4] = [0], [2], …), i.e. [2]x², since [4] = [0]); (b) is "1 + x + x² + ⋯" a polynomial over ℚ? ## 02-polynomial-ring.md — The Polynomial Ring

- **Hook.** (3x² − x + 2)(x + 1) at school: expand every product a_i x^i · b_j x^j = a_i b_j x^{i+j} and collect. Turn that into a definition, then prove the school laws without expanding three-fold products coefficient by coefficient.
- **`def-polynomial-ring`** (la label; widened). R[x] is the set of all polynomials over R. For p, q ∈ R[x]: (p + q)_k ≔ p_k + q_k; −p by (−p)_k ≔ −p_k; (pq)_k ≔ \( \sum_{i=0}^{k} p_i q_{k-i} \) (`lem-finite-sums-and-powers` (a); k − i ∈ ℕ for i ≤ k, read in ℤ ⊇ ℕ, `@fd3:prp-naturals-embed-integers`). Closure (main line, a paragraph): if p_i = 0 for i > m and q_j = 0 for j > n, then (p + q)_k = 0 for k > max(m, n), and for k > m + n every term p_i q_{k−i} has i > m or k − i > n, so (pq)_k = 0 by (Σ3)-style reasoning (all terms 0; `@fd3:prp-ring-algebra` (b)). So + and · are functions R[x] × R[x] → R[x].
- **`exm-polynomial-product`** (la label). (3x² − x + 2)(x + 1) in ℚ[x] from the formula, k = 0, …, 3: 2, 1, 2, 3, and 0 for k ≥ 4; = 3x³ + 2x² + x + 2. The same product in 𝔽_3[x]: 2x² + x + 2 (3 = 0); in (ℤ/6ℤ)[x]: [3]x³ + [2]x² + x + [2]. Sidecar.
- **`prp-polynomial-sum-of-monomials`.** Let p ∈ R[x] with p_k = 0 for k > N. Then p = \( \sum_{k=0}^{N} p_k x^k \), the sum of the monomials p_k x^k taken in (R[x], +) (`lem-finite-sums-and-powers` (a) with + of R[x]). Route: induction on N with the statement "for every p with p_k = 0 for k > N"; compare coefficients, position by position. Uses + only: no product yet. ## 03-degree.md — Degree, Leading Coefficients, and Integral Domains

- **Hook.** In ℤ[x], deg((2x + 1)(3x² + x)) = 3 = 1 + 2. In (ℤ/6ℤ)[x] the same product is [5]x² + x: degree 2. What made the difference is one product of two leading coefficients, 2·3 = 6.
- **`def-degree`** (la label; widened). For p ∈ R[x], p ≠ 0: deg p is the **largest** n with p_n ≠ 0 (exists by finiteness; a largest element of a non-empty subset of ℕ bounded above, `@fd2:prp-finite-max` with the subset of {0, …, N}, or by `@fd3:thm-omega-order`); lc(p) ≔ p_{deg p} is the **leading coefficient**; p is **monic** if lc(p) = 1. deg 0 ≔ −∞, with −∞ < n, −∞ + n = n + (−∞) = −∞ + (−∞) = −∞. Why −∞ (la's paragraph kept: the only value making `thm-degree-of-product` true with p = 0). Over the zero ring every polynomial is 0.
- **`thm-degree-of-sum`** (la label; R arbitrary). deg(p + q) ≤ max(deg p, deg q), with equality when deg p ≠ deg q. Proof as la (coefficients beyond the maximum vanish; at the larger degree, a_M + 0). Remark: strict inequality iff deg p = deg q and the leading coefficients cancel.
- **`thm-degree-of-product`** (la label; key proof; widened and split; Before reading on: "which line needs R to have no zero divisors, and which works in every ring?"). Let R be a commutative ring and p, q ∈ R[x] non-zero, m = deg p, n = deg q.
  - (a) (pq)_k = 0 for every k > m + n, and (pq)_{m+n} = lc(p) lc(q). Hence deg(pq) ≤ deg p + deg q for **all** p, q ∈ R[x] (with the −∞ conventions when p or q is 0).
  - (b) deg(pq) = m + n iff lc(p) lc(q) ≠ 0, and then lc(pq) = lc(p) lc(q). This holds in particular when R has no zero divisors (ab = 0 ⇒ a = 0 or b = 0), e.g. when R is a field (`@thm-field-basic-properties` (f)), and when lc(p) is a unit of R (`@fd3:def-ring-unit`: if u is a unit and ub = 0, then b = u^{-1}ub = 0), in particular when p is monic and 1 ≠ 0.
  - Proof. (a) for k > m + n, each term p_i q_{k−i} has i > m (so p_i = 0) or i ≤ m and then k − i > n (so q_{k−i} = 0); for k = m + n, every term with i ≠ m is 0 for the same reason, and (Σ3) leaves p_m q_n. The inequality: if p = 0 or q = 0, then pq = 0 (every coefficient is a sum of terms 0, `@fd3:prp-ring-algebra` (b)) and both sides are −∞; if pq = 0 with p, q ≠ 0 the left side is −∞; otherwise it follows from the vanishing above m + n. (b) is (a) read at k = m + n. The unit case: one line.
  - *Idea:* the top of a product comes from the tops of the factors alone; the only question is whether the product of the tops is 0, and that is a question about R, not about polynomials.

## 04-evaluation-and-substitution.md — Evaluation, Substitution, and Polynomial Functions

- **Hook.** A polynomial is not a function, but every polynomial **defines** one: put c in place of x. And more: put a matrix, another polynomial, or a class modulo n in place of x. One theorem covers all of these.
- **`def-polynomial-evaluation`** (la label; widened). For p ∈ R[x] and c ∈ R: p(c) ≔ \( \sum_{k=0}^{N} p_k c^k \) for any N with p_k = 0 for k > N (`lem-finite-sums-and-powers` (a), (c), c^0 = 1). Well defined: enlarging N adds terms 0·c^k = 0 (`@fd3:prp-ring-algebra` (b)). c is a **root** of p if p(c) = 0; the **polynomial function** of p is c ↦ p(c), a function R → R. Examples (la's): p = x² − 3x + 2 ∈ ℚ[x]: p(0) = 2, p(1) = 0; over 𝔽_3, x³ + 2 has the root 1 only (values 2, 0, 1). Warning repeated from §01: p(2) versus p_2.
- **`thm-evaluation-respects-operations`** (la label; R arbitrary). For p, q ∈ R[x] and c ∈ R: (p + q)(c) = p(c) + q(c), (pq)(c) = p(c) q(c), a(c) = a for a constant a, and x(c) = c. Proof: sums by (Σ1) with a common N; products by **check on monomials** (second use of the move, written in full but short): Φ(p, q) ≔ (pq)(c), Ψ(p, q) ≔ p(c)q(c) are additive in each argument (the sum rule and (F9)); on monomials, ((ab)x^{m+n})(c) = (ab)c^{m+n} = (a c^m)(b c^n) by `lem-monomial-product` (b), `lem-finite-sums-and-powers` (c), (F5), (F6); `lem-check-on-monomials` with T = R. Remark (kept from la, rewritten): the monomial line moves b past c^m; **that** is where commutativity is used. The linear algebra companion's polynomials of a matrix (prose pointer to its Chapter 0, now LA1.0) use the same proof, because there b is a scalar and scalars commute with matrices.
- Consequence paragraph (la's): a root of a factor is a root of the product; and if R has no zero divisors, (pq)(c) = 0 ⇒ p(c) = 0 or q(c) = 0 (how one solves (x − 1)(x − 2) = 0).
- **`def-ring-homomorphism`.** For commutative rings R, S, a **ring homomorphism** φ : R → S is a function with φ(a + b) = φ(a) + φ(b), φ(ab) = φ(a)φ(b) and φ(1) = 1. Consequences (one paragraph): φ(0) = 0 and φ(−a) = −φ(a) (`@fd3:prp-ring-algebra` (a), uniqueness of 0 and of negatives); φ(s^n) = φ(s)^n (induction); a composite of ring homomorphisms is one.
- **`exm-ring-homomorphisms`.** (i) The quotient map ℤ → ℤ/nℤ, a ↦ [a] (`@fd3:thm-quotient-ring-by-ideal` (c)); more generally R → R/I. (ii) The constants R → R[x] (`thm-polynomial-ring-laws` (b)). (iii) The map of `@fd3:thm-chinese-remainder-two` (b). (iv) Prose, F4.1: complex conjugation ℂ → ℂ (its rules are Chapter 1's; used only in R2). Non-examples: a ↦ 2a on ℤ (additive, not multiplicative: 2(1·1) = 2 ≠ 4 = (2·1)(2·1)); the zero map ℤ → ℤ (multiplicative and additive, but φ(1) = 0 ≠ 1).
- **`thm-polynomial-substitution`** (key proof; Try it first: "take R = ℤ, S = ℤ/5ℤ, φ the quotient map, s = [2]. Which value must a homomorphism Φ with Φ(x) = s give to 3x² + 1? ## 05-formal-derivative.md — The Formal Derivative

- **Hook.** At a double root, as for (x − 1)² at 1, the graph touches the axis: slope 0. Over 𝔽_p there are no slopes. Keep the formula of the derivative and drop the limit. (Throughout §05, polynomials are f, g, h; p is a prime.)
- **`def-formal-derivative`** (la label, moved from la §6.4; widened). For f ∈ R[x], f' is the polynomial with (f')_k ≔ (k + 1)·f_{k+1} (F3.2's multiples); that is, f' = \( \sum_{k \ge 1} (k \cdot f_k) x^{k-1} \). Higher derivatives f^{(0)} ≔ f, f^{(k+1)} ≔ (f^{(k)})' (`@fd2:thm-recursion`). f' is a polynomial (if f_k = 0 for k > N then (f')_k = 0 for k ≥ N). By `lem-finite-sums-and-powers` (d), k·f_k = (k·1) f_k: **the element k·1 of R can be 0**, and that is the whole difference between characteristics.
- **`exm-formal-derivatives`.** Over ℚ: (x⁴ − 2x³ + 2x − 1)' = 4x³ − 6x² + 2. Over any R: a' = 0, x' = 1, (x − c)' = 1. Over 𝔽_3: (x³ + x)' = 1. Over 𝔽_2: (x²)' = 0. Over ℤ/6ℤ: (2x³ + x)' = [6]x² + 1 = 1 (degree drops by 3). Over ℤ: (x^n)' = n x^{n−1}.
- **`thm-formal-derivative-rules`** (la label; R arbitrary; la's (c) restated with m·1). For f, g ∈ R[x], a, c ∈ R and m ≥ 1: (a) (f + g)' = f' + g', (af)' = a f' for a constant a, a' = 0; (b) (fg)' = f'g + fg' (product rule); (c) (g^m)' = (m·1) g^{m−1} g'; in particular ((x − c)^m)' = (m·1)(x − c)^{m−1}.
  - Proof. (a) coefficientwise, `lem-finite-sums-and-powers` (d) (m·(a + b), (m·a)b). (b) **check on monomials** (third use, written in full): both sides are additive in f and in g (by (a) and distributivity); for monomials a x^i, b x^j: if i, j ≥ 1, ((ab)x^{i+j})' = ((i + j)·(ab)) x^{i+j−1} = (i·a)x^{i−1}·b x^j + a x^i·(j·b)x^{j−1} (`lem-monomial-product` (b), `lem-finite-sums-and-powers` (d)); if i = 0 the first summand is 0 and both sides are (j·(ab))x^{j−1} (or 0 when j = 0 too); j = 0 symmetric; `lem-check-on-monomials`, m = 2, T = R[x]. (c) induction on m with (b): (g^{m+1})' = (m·1)g^{m−1}g'·g + g^m g' = ((m + 1)·1)g^m g'.
  - Remark (la's, kept): over ℚ this agrees with the calculus derivative of the polynomial function (prose; an1's derivative is never cited).
- **`prp-formal-derivative-zero`.** Let F be a field (`@def-field`) and f ∈ F[x]. (a) If F has characteristic 0 (`@def-characteristic`), then f' = 0 iff f is constant; and deg f' = deg f − 1 when deg f ≥ 1. (b) If F has characteristic p > 0 (then p is prime: F4.2's `@thm-characteristic-prime`), then f' = 0 iff f_k = 0 for every k not divisible by p, iff f = g ∘ x^p for some g ∈ F[x] (`def-polynomial-composition`). Proof: (f')_{k−1} = (k·1) f_k; in characteristic 0, k·1 ≠ 0 for k ≥ 1, and F has no zero divisors (`@thm-field-basic-properties` (f)); in characteristic p, k·1 = 0 iff p | k (divide k by p, `@fd3:thm-integers-division-remainder`: k·1 = q·(p·1) + r·1 = r·1 by `lem-finite-sums-and-powers` (d), and r·1 ≠ 0 for 0 < r < p by minimality in `@def-characteristic`); g ≔ \( \sum_j f_{pj} x^j \), and g ∘ x^p = \( \sum_j f_{pj} x^{pj} \) by `thm-polynomial-ring-laws` (c).
- **Warning.** (b) is why repeated-root tests in characteristic p need care: x^p − x has derivative p·x^{p−1} − 1 = −1 over 𝔽_p (prose pointer: F4.5 concludes that x^p − x has no repeated roots).
