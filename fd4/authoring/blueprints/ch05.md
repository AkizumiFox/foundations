**Signature moves** (`index.md`, "Named moves").
- **Least degree, then divide.** In a set of polynomials closed under the right operations, take a non-zero element d of least degree; divide any element by d; the remainder lies in the set and has smaller degree, so it is 0. (F3.5's "least positive element, then divide", with degree in place of size. **Faded triple.**)
- **Evaluate at a root.** In f = qg + r, plug in a root of g: the term qg vanishes and the value of r is read off (remainder theorem, interpolation, CRT with linear moduli).
- **Compare degrees.** Uniqueness of quotients, of interpolants and of factorizations comes from a degree count: a non-zero polynomial of degree < n cannot be a multiple of one of degree n.
- **Keep the quarter that keeps the infimum.** To find a point where a bounded-below quantity attains its infimum, halve a square, keep the first quarter (in a fixed order) whose infimum is the same, and take the point common to all the squares (§06; F3.10's bisection, now in the plane).
- From F3.5, reused by name: **multiply the Bézout identity**; **compute on representatives; the ideal absorbs the change**.

## index.md

- **Story.** F3.5 did arithmetic in ℤ: divide with a remainder, take the least positive element of an ideal, and gcds, Bézout, Euclid's lemma and unique factorization followed. F[x] over a field has the same arithmetic, with the degree playing the part of the size — and it has something ℤ lacks: polynomials can be **evaluated**, so they have roots. Roots are what the rest of the chapter is about: a root c is a factor x − c; distinct roots can be no more than the degree; a repeated root is visible in the formal derivative; values at enough points determine a polynomial, which is interpolation, and interpolation is the Chinese remainder theorem for the moduli x − c. The quotient ring F[x]/⟨m⟩ — F3.5's construction once more — is a field exactly when m is irreducible. Last, the question every algebra course leaves to analysis: does every complex polynomial have a root? It does, and the only analytic fact the proof needs is that bounded sets of reals have suprema.

## 01-polynomial-division.md — Division with Remainder

- **Hook.** F3.5 divided −17 by 5 and kept the remainder 3, smaller than 5. For polynomials "smaller" means "of smaller degree": x⁴ + 2x³ − x + 3 = (x² + x)(x² + x − 1) + 3. Three questions: why can one always continue until the degree drops below that of the divisor; why is the answer unique; and where does the field enter (dividing leading coefficients). (LA §6.01 hook, shortened.)
- **`def-polynomial-divides`.** Let F be a field and f, g ∈ F[x]. g **divides f over F**, g | f, if f = gh for some h ∈ F[x]; divisor, factor, multiple. f and g are **associates** if f = cg for some non-zero c ∈ F. Read on the four-way example: x − 1 | x² − 1 and 2x − 2 | x² − 1 in ℚ[x] (associates); x + 1 | x² + 1 in 𝔽₂[x] ((x + 1)² = x² + 1); x + 1 ∤ x² + 1 in ℚ[x] (evaluate at −1: 2 = 0·h(−1), `@thm-evaluation-respects-operations`). Minimal-change non-example: same symbols, field changed from 𝔽₂ to ℚ.
- **`prp-polynomial-divisibility-rules`.** For f, g, h ∈ F[x]: (a) g | 0; 0 | f iff f = 0; every non-zero constant divides every f; (b) f | g and g | h ⇒ f | h; (c) h | f and h | g ⇒ h | af + bg for all a, b ∈ F[x]; (d) g | f and f ≠ 0 ⇒ deg g ≤ deg f; (e) the units of F[x] (`@fd3:def-ring-unit`) are exactly the non-zero constants (`@prp-polynomial-units`, F a domain); (f) f | g and g | f iff f, g are associates or both 0; every non-zero f has **exactly one** monic associate (a⁻¹f, a the leading coefficient); two monic polynomials that divide each other are equal. Proofs: (a)–(c) from `@thm-polynomial-ring-laws`; (d) f = gu with u ≠ 0, `@thm-degree-of-product`; (e) is F4.4's `@prp-polynomial-units` with F^× = F ∖ {0}; (f) g = fu, f = gv, f ≠ 0 ⇒ f(1 − uv) = 0 ⇒ uv = 1 (`@cor-polynomial-no-zero-divisors`) ⇒ u a unit, (e). One sentence: (e)–(f) are F3.5's "±1 are invisible to divisibility" (`@fd3:prp-divisibility-rules-integers` (f), (g)), with "positive" replaced by "monic".
- **`thm-polynomial-division-remainder`.** Let F be a field and f, g ∈ F[x] with g ≠ 0. (a) There are **unique** q, r ∈ F[x] with f = qg + r and deg r < deg g (deg 0 = −∞ allows r = 0). (b) g | f iff r = 0.
  - Proof of (a), existence (the least-degree route, F3.5's `@fd3:thm-integers-division-remainder` with degree for size). m ≔ deg g ∈ ℕ, b its leading coefficient. S ≔ {f − hg : h ∈ F[x]}. If 0 ∈ S, take r = 0. Otherwise {deg s : s ∈ S} ⊆ ℕ is non-empty, so it has a least element (`@fd2:thm-well-ordering`, read on ω via `@fd3:cor-omega-identification`); pick r = f − qg ∈ S of that degree n. If n ≥ m, let a be the leading coefficient of r and r₁ ≔ r − ab⁻¹x^{n−m}g = f − (q + ab⁻¹x^{n−m})g ∈ S. By `@thm-degree-of-product`, ab⁻¹x^{n−m}g has degree n and leading coefficient a, so r₁ = 0 or deg r₁ < n (`@thm-degree-of-sum`): either contradicts the choice of r (0 ∉ S; n least). So deg r < m.
  - Uniqueness (compare degrees): qg + r = q′g + r′ gives (q − q′)g = r′ − r of degree < m; if q ≠ q′ the left side has degree ≥ m (`@thm-degree-of-product`). So q = q′, then r = r′.
  - (b) ⇐ f = qg; ⇒ f = hg + 0 is a division with remainder, so r = 0 by uniqueness.
  - Remarks: where the field is used (b⁻¹; the degree of a product, no zero divisors); the proof is an algorithm (each pass removes the leading term: long division, at most deg f − deg g + 1 passes). F4.4 stated (a) for orientation; this pays it. The integer twin is `@fd3:thm-integers-division-remainder` (compared with).
- **`exm-polynomial-long-division`** (LA, two parts kept, one new): (i) in ℚ[x], 2x⁴ − 3x³ + x² + 4x − 1 = (x² − x − 3/2)(2x² − x + 3) + (11/2)x + 7/2; (ii) in 𝔽₃[x], x⁴ + x + 2 = (2x² + 2)(2x² + 1) + x (2⁻¹ = 2 in 𝔽₃). Sidecars check both.

The leading coefficient of the divisor must be invertible (§01 C1 does monic divisors over ℤ).
- **`thm-polynomial-remainder-factor`** (remainder and factor theorem). Let f ∈ F[x], c ∈ F. (a) The remainder of f on division by x − c is the constant f(c). (b) c is a root of f iff x − c | f. (c) f has a factor of degree 1 in F[x] iff f has a root in F. Proof: (a) divide: f = q(x − c) + r with deg r ≤ 0, so r is a constant; **evaluate at the root c** of x − c (`@thm-evaluation-respects-operations`): f(c) = q(c)·0 + r. (b) by (a) and `thm-polynomial-division-remainder` (b). (c) ax + b with a ≠ 0 is an associate of x − (−a⁻¹b); (b).
- **`exm-polynomial-remainders-without-dividing`** (LA): (a) remainder of x⁵ − 3x³ + 2x + 7 on division by x − 2 in ℚ[x]: 19; (b) remainder of x⁵ on division by x² − x: write x⁵ = q(x² − x) + ax + b, evaluate at the roots 0 and 1: b = 0, a + b = 1, so x.

## 02-polynomial-gcd.md — Ideals, the Greatest Common Divisor, and the Euclidean Algorithm

- **Hook.** Which polynomials can be written as a(x)(x² − 1) + b(x)(x² + x − 2)? All multiples of x − 1, and nothing else. Why x − 1, and why "all multiples"? (F3.5's coin hook, for polynomials.)
- **F[x] is a commutative ring** (one sentence, pointing back to F4.4's `@thm-polynomial-ring-laws` (a) and its remark): so F3.5's ideals (`@fd3:def-ideal-commutative-ring`), quotient rings (`@fd3:thm-quotient-ring-by-ideal`) and units (`@fd3:def-ring-unit`) apply to it.
- **`def-polynomial-combinations`.** For f₁, …, f_k ∈ F[x] (k ≥ 1): ⟨f₁, …, f_k⟩ ≔ {a₁f₁ + … + a_kf_k : a_i ∈ F[x]}; ⟨f⟩ = fF[x], the multiples of f. It is an ideal of F[x] containing every f_i, and it lies inside every ideal containing every f_i (one line each, `@thm-polynomial-ring-laws`). Examples: ⟨0⟩ = {0}; ⟨c⟩ = F[x] for a non-zero constant c (f = c·c⁻¹f); I_c ≔ {f : f(c) = 0} is an ideal (`@thm-evaluation-respects-operations`), and I_c = ⟨x − c⟩ by `thm-polynomial-remainder-factor` (b). Non-example: {f : deg f ≥ 3} ∪ {0} is not closed under + (x³ + (−x³ + x) = x has degree 1), so it is not an ideal.
- **`thm-polynomial-ideals-principal`** (key proof; Before reading on: "where is 'least degree' used, and why is d made monic?"). Let I be an ideal of F[x]. If I = {0}, then I = ⟨0⟩. If I ≠ {0}, there is **exactly one monic** d ∈ F[x] with I = ⟨d⟩, and d is the unique monic element of least degree in I.
  - Proof (the move **least degree, then divide**, in full; named in bold here). I ≠ {0}: the degrees of non-zero elements form a non-empty subset of ℕ; least element (`@fd2:thm-well-ordering`); an element e of that degree; d ≔ a⁻¹e with a its leading coefficient, monic and in I ((I3)). ⟨d⟩ ⊆ I by (I3). For f ∈ I, f = qd + r with deg r < deg d (`thm-polynomial-division-remainder`); r = f − qd ∈ I ((I2), (I3) with −q); a non-zero r would have smaller degree than the least: so r = 0, f ∈ ⟨d⟩. Uniqueness: if ⟨d⟩ = ⟨d′⟩ with d, d′ monic, each divides the other, so d = d′ (`prp-polynomial-divisibility-rules` (f)); and every monic element of least degree generates I by the same argument, hence equals d.
  - *Idea:* a smallest non-zero element divides everything in the ideal because dividing by it leaves a remainder that is both in the ideal and smaller.
  - Remark: the twin is `@fd3:thm-integer-ideals-principal` (compared with); in two variables the theorem fails (§02 A1 (d), prose, no proof).
- **`def-polynomial-gcd`.** Let f, g ∈ F[x], not both 0. A **greatest common divisor** of f and g is a **monic** d with d | f, d | g, and h | d for every common divisor h. There is at most one (`prp-polynomial-divisibility-rules` (f)); it is written gcd(f, g). Convention gcd(0, 0) ≔ 0 (every polynomial divides 0; the convention makes (b), (e) below hold for (0, 0)). Existence is the next theorem.
- **`thm-polynomial-bezout`.** Let f, g ∈ F[x] be not both 0, and let d be the monic generator of the ideal ⟨f, g⟩ (`thm-polynomial-ideals-principal`). Then (a) d | f and d | g; (b) every common divisor of f and g divides d; (c) d = gcd(f, g), so gcd(f, g) exists and ⟨f, g⟩ = ⟨gcd(f, g)⟩; (d) gcd(f, g) = af + bg for some a, b ∈ F[x]; (e) gcd(f, g) has the largest degree among the common divisors, and a common divisor of that degree is an associate of it. Proof: (a) f, g ∈ ⟨d⟩; (b) d = a₀f + b₀g and `prp-polynomial-divisibility-rules` (c); (c) by (a), (b) and `def-polynomial-gcd`; (d) d ∈ ⟨f, g⟩; (e) h | d gives deg h ≤ deg d (`prp-polynomial-divisibility-rules` (d)); if equal, d = hu with deg u = 0.
  - Remark: F3.5 defined the integer gcd as the **largest** common divisor and then proved Bézout; for polynomials "largest" would only fix the degree (associates have equal degree), which is why the definition asks for "divided by every common divisor" and "monic" (part (e) recovers "largest").
- **`def-coprime-polynomials`:** f, g are **coprime over F** if gcd(f, g) = 1.
- **`cor-polynomial-coprime-iff-combination`:** f, g coprime iff af + bg = 1 for some a, b ∈ F[x]. (⇒ (d); ⇐ 1 ∈ ⟨f, g⟩, so ⟨f, g⟩ = ⟨1⟩ and the monic generator is 1, by uniqueness in `thm-polynomial-ideals-principal`.)
- **`lem-polynomial-gcd-remainder-step`:** if f = qg + r, then ⟨f, g⟩ = ⟨g, r⟩, hence gcd(f, g) = gcd(g, r). (Each generator of one side is a combination of the other's; the minimality clause of `def-polynomial-combinations`.)
- **`thm-euclidean-algorithm-polynomials`.** Let f, g ∈ F[x], g ≠ 0. Define (r_k)_{k ∈ ℕ} by recursion (`@fd2:thm-recursion` on pairs (r_k, r_{k+1}) ∈ F[x] × F[x]): r₀ ≔ f, r₁ ≔ g, and, while r_{k+1} ≠ 0, r_{k+2} ≔ the remainder of r_k on division by r_{k+1}; once r_{k+1} = 0, the pair stays. Then (a) ⟨r_k, r_{k+1}⟩ = ⟨f, g⟩ for every k; (b) there is a least K ≥ 1 with r_{K+1} = 0, and K ≤ deg g + 1; deg r_{k+1} < deg r_k for 1 ≤ k < K; (c) gcd(f, g) is the monic associate of r_K. Proof: (a) induction on k with the lemma; (b) the degrees deg r₁ > deg r₂ > … are natural numbers while non-zero, so at most deg g + 1 non-zero terms follow r₀ (induction: deg r_{k} ≤ deg g − (k − 1) for 1 ≤ k ≤ K); (c) ⟨f, g⟩ = ⟨r_K, 0⟩ = ⟨r_K⟩, and the monic generator is unique. **Back-substitution** gives the a, b of `thm-polynomial-bezout` (d) (an `.algorithm` block, computation only, nothing rests on it).
- **`exm-polynomial-euclid-run`** (LA): in ℚ[x], f = x⁴ − x³ − 3x² + x + 2, g = x³ − 2x − 1: f = (x − 1)g + (1 − x²); g = (−x)(1 − x²) + (−x − 1); 1 − x² = (x − 1)(−x − 1) + 0. So gcd = x + 1, and back-substitution gives x + 1 = (−x)·f + (x² − x − 1)·g. Sidecar checks every line.
- **`prp-polynomial-gcd-field-extension`.** Let K ⊆ L be a subfield (`@def-subfield`, checked by `@prp-subfield-test`; examples ℚ ⊆ ℝ ⊆ ℂ, with ℝ ⊆ ℂ by `@prp-reals-embed-complex` and `@thm-complex-numbers-field`), and f, g ∈ K[x] ⊆ L[x]. (a) If g ≠ 0, the quotient and remainder of f on division by g are the same in K[x] and in L[x]. (b) For g ≠ 0: g | f in L[x] iff g | f in K[x]. (c) gcd(f, g) is the same computed in K[x] or in L[x]; in particular f, g are coprime over K iff over L. Proof: (a) the K[x] division is a division in L[x]; uniqueness in L[x]. (b) by (a) and `thm-polynomial-division-remainder` (b). (c) the Euclidean algorithm runs inside K[x] (by (a) at every step), and its output does not depend on the field (`thm-euclidean-algorithm-polynomials` (c)). One sentence: §06 uses (b) for ℝ ⊆ ℂ.
- **Warning.** Bézout coefficients are not unique: x + 1 = (−x)f + (x² − x − 1)g = (−x + g)f + (x² − x − 1 − f)g.

## 03-irreducible-factorization.md — Irreducible Polynomials and Unique Factorization

- **Hook.** x⁴ − 1 = (x − 1)(x + 1)(x² + 1) over ℚ; over 𝔽₅ it splits into four linear factors; over 𝔽₂ it is (x + 1)⁴. The primes of F[x] depend on F. Once they are fixed, factorization into them is unique — as in ℤ, and for the same reason.
- **`def-irreducible-over-field`.** p ∈ F[x] is **irreducible over F** if deg p ≥ 1 and whenever p = gh with g, h ∈ F[x], one of g, h is a (non-zero) constant; otherwise, for deg p ≥ 1, **reducible**. Examples: every polynomial of degree 1 (degrees of a product add); x² + 1 over ℚ (next result). Non-examples: constants (including 0 and 2 over ℚ); x² + x = x(x + 1) and x³ = x·x² over every field. Associates of irreducibles are irreducible.
- **`prp-irreducible-low-degree`.** Let p ∈ F[x]. (a) If deg p ≥ 2 and p has a root in F, p is reducible. (b) If deg p ∈ {2, 3}, then p is irreducible over F iff p has no root in F. Proof: (a) `thm-polynomial-remainder-factor` (b); (b) a factorization of a polynomial of degree 2 or 3 into two non-constants has a factor of degree 1 (degrees add to 2 or 3), hence a root by `thm-polynomial-remainder-factor` (c).
- **`thm-polynomial-euclid-lemma`** (multiply the Bézout identity, reused from F3.5 by name). Let f, g, h ∈ F[x] with f | gh and gcd(f, g) = 1. Then f | h. Proof: 1 = af + bg (`cor-polynomial-coprime-iff-combination`); h = (ah)f + b(gh); `prp-polynomial-divisibility-rules` (c).
- **`cor-irreducible-divides-product`.** Let p be irreducible over F. (a) For every f: p | f or gcd(p, f) = 1. (b) p | gh ⇒ p | g or p | h. (c) If p | q₁⋯q_l (a list of l ≥ 1 polynomials; its product by the recursion of `@fd2:def-sum-product` read in the commutative ring F[x], ledger line), then p | q_j for some j. (d) If p and q are monic irreducible and p | q, then p = q. Proof: (a) d = gcd(p, f) is monic and divides p = du; d or u is constant; d constant ⇒ d = 1; u constant ⇒ d is an associate of p and p | f; (b) by (a) and the lemma; (c) induction on l; (d) q = pu with q irreducible and p non-constant ⇒ u constant; both monic ⇒ u = 1.
- **`prp-polynomial-coprime-divisors-product`.** If m | h, n | h and gcd(m, n) = 1, then mn | h. (h = mk; n | mk; the lemma gives n | k.)
- **`lem-polynomial-irreducible-factor`.** Every f ∈ F[x] with deg f ≥ 1 has a **monic irreducible** divisor. Proof: the set of degrees of monic divisors of f of degree ≥ 1 is non-empty (the monic associate of f) — least element; a monic divisor p of that degree; a factorization p = gh into non-constants would give a monic divisor (the monic associate of g) of smaller positive degree dividing f. Twin of `@fd3:lem-least-divisor-prime`.
- **`thm-polynomial-unique-factorization`** (key proof, uniqueness half; Before reading on: "where is Euclid's lemma used, and why does 'monic' turn p_k = (constant)·q_j into p_k = q_j?"). Let f ∈ F[x] with deg f ≥ 1 and leading coefficient c. (a) **Existence.** f = c·p₁⋯p_k for some k ≥ 1 and monic irreducible p₁, …, p_k. (b) **Uniqueness.** If also f = c′·q₁⋯q_l with c′ ∈ F and monic irreducible q₁, …, q_l, then c′ = c, l = k, and there is a bijection σ: {1, …, k} → {1, …, l} (`@fd2:def-bijective`) with q_{σ(i)} = p_i for every i.
  - Existence: strong induction on deg f (`@fd1:thm-strong-induction` on ω): if the monic associate c⁻¹f is irreducible, k = 1; otherwise c⁻¹f = gh with 1 ≤ deg g, deg h < deg f, both may be taken monic (move the leading coefficients: they multiply to 1), and the hypothesis applies to g and h; concatenate the lists.
  - Uniqueness: c′ = c by comparing leading coefficients (a product of monic polynomials is monic, `@thm-degree-of-product`). Then induction on k (`@fd1:ax-induction`) for P(k): "for every l and all lists of monic irreducibles with p₁⋯p_k = q₁⋯q_l, l = k and a bijection σ as above exists". Base k = 0 (empty product 1): l = 0, since a product of l ≥ 1 monic irreducibles has degree ≥ 1. Step: p_{k+1} divides q₁⋯q_l, so l ≥ 1 and p_{k+1} | q_j for some j (`cor-irreducible-divides-product` (c)), hence p_{k+1} = q_j (`cor-irreducible-divides-product` (d)). Cancel p_{k+1} ≠ 0 (`@cor-polynomial-no-zero-divisors`): p₁⋯p_k = the product of the q-list with entry j removed (re-indexed by the order-preserving bijection {1, …, l − 1} → {1, …, l} ∖ {j}). P(k) gives l − 1 = k and a bijection; extend it by k + 1 ↦ j.
  - *Idea:* an irreducible that divides a product must equal one of the factors (Euclid's lemma); cancel it and repeat.
  - Remark: F3.5 made lists non-decreasing to state uniqueness for ℤ (`@fd3:thm-fundamental-arithmetic`); polynomials have no natural order, so "the same up to order" is said with a bijection of indices.

## 04-multiple-roots.md — Roots, Multiplicity, and the Formal Derivative

- **Hook.** Over ℚ, x⁴ − 2x³ + 2x − 1 vanishes at 1 and −1 only, yet 1 is "three roots at once". How do we count roots so that a polynomial of degree n has at most n of them, and how can we see a repeated root without factoring?
- **`thm-polynomial-root-count`.** Let f ∈ F[x] and c₁, …, c_k ∈ F **distinct** roots of f (k ≥ 1). Then (x − c₁)⋯(x − c_k) | f. If f ≠ 0, then k ≤ deg f: a non-zero polynomial of degree n has at most n distinct roots in F. Proof: induction on k; base `thm-polynomial-remainder-factor` (b); step: f = (x − c₁)⋯(x − c_{k−1})h, evaluate at c_k: 0 = (c_k − c₁)⋯(c_k − c_{k−1})h(c_k); the factors c_k − c_i are non-zero and a field has no zero divisors (`@thm-field-basic-properties`), so h(c_k) = 0 and x − c_k | h. Count: `@thm-degree-of-product`. (LA's cor-root-bound-general; here no linear algebra is needed for the count either.)
- **`thm-polynomial-identity-from-values`.** Let p, q ∈ F[x]. (a) If deg p, deg q ≤ n and p(c) = q(c) at n + 1 distinct c ∈ F, then p = q. (b) If F is infinite and p(c) = q(c) for every c ∈ F, then p = q. (Apply the root count to p − q; for (b), F infinite has n + 1 distinct elements, `@fd2:def-finite`.) This pays F4.4's "over an infinite field a polynomial is determined by its function" (see Promises).
- **Warning (false conclusion over a finite field).** (b) fails for every finite field 𝔽_p: x^p − x ≠ 0 vanishes at every element (`@thm-fermat-little` (a)); for p = 2 this is `@exm-polynomial-vs-function-f2`. No contradiction with (a): degree p needs p + 1 points, and 𝔽_p has p.
- **`exm-polynomial-function-finite-field`.** (a) Over 𝔽₃: x³ + 2x and 0 give the same function (table); x³ + 2x = x³ − x = x(x − 1)(x − 2) (`thm-polynomial-root-count` with the three roots, degree 3, monic). (b) In general x^p − x = Π_{c ∈ 𝔽_p}(x − c) in 𝔽_p[x] (the p roots of the warning, `thm-polynomial-root-count`, compare degrees and leading coefficients), and every root is simple (after `thm-polynomial-repeated-root-test` below: (x^p − x)′ = (p·1)x^{p−1} − 1 = −1, `@thm-formal-derivative-rules`) — the fact F4.4's §05 warning sends here. Sidecar checks (a) and (b) for p ≤ 7.
- **`thm-polynomial-taylor-shift`** (re-centering, by repeated division; no basis). Let f ∈ F[x] with deg f = n ≥ 0 and c ∈ F. (a) There are **unique** b₀, …, b_n ∈ F with f = Σ_{j=0}^{n} b_j (x − c)^j. (b) b₀ = f(c), and b_n is the leading coefficient of f. (c) For every h ∈ F, f(c + h) = Σ b_j h^j. (The link with multiplicity is `lem-polynomial-multiplicity-cofactor` (c), below.) Proof: (a) existence by induction on n: f = (x − c)q + f(c) (`thm-polynomial-remainder-factor`), deg q = n − 1, expand q; uniqueness: a combination Σ d_j(x − c)^j with some d_j ≠ 0 has degree J = the largest such j (`@thm-degree-of-sum`, `@thm-degree-of-product`), so it is not 0 (compare degrees); (b) evaluate at c; compare the x^n coefficients; (c) `@thm-evaluation-respects-operations`. Used in §06 (descent) and §05 (Hermite).
- **`def-polynomial-root-multiplicity`.** For f ≠ 0 and c ∈ F, mult_c(f) is the **largest** m ∈ ℕ with (x − c)^m | f (it exists: m ≤ deg f by `prp-polynomial-divisibility-rules` (d), and 0 qualifies); c is a **simple** root if mult = 1, **multiple** if ≥ 2; mult_c(f) = 0 iff f(c) ≠ 0.
- **`lem-polynomial-multiplicity-cofactor`.** For f ≠ 0, c ∈ F, m ∈ ℕ: (a) mult_c(f) = m iff f = (x − c)^m g with g(c) ≠ 0; (b) such g is unique; (c) in the expansion of `thm-polynomial-taylor-shift`, mult_c(f) is the least j with b_j ≠ 0. Proof: (a) ⇒ f = (x − c)^m g; if g(c) = 0, then x − c | g and (x − c)^{m+1} | f; ⇐ if (x − c)^{m+1} | f, cancel (x − c)^m (`@cor-polynomial-no-zero-divisors`) to get x − c | g, so g(c) = 0; (b) cancellation; (c) group the expansion as (x − c)^J·(b_J + b_{J+1}(x − c) + …), whose cofactor has value b_J at c.
- **`prp-polynomial-multiplicity-product`.** mult_c(fg) = mult_c(f) + mult_c(g) for non-zero f, g. (Cofactors multiply; (uv)(c) = u(c)v(c) ≠ 0.)
- **`thm-polynomial-roots-with-multiplicity`.** Let f ≠ 0, with distinct roots c₁, …, c_k in F (all of them; finitely many by the root count) and multiplicities m_i. Then f = (x − c₁)^{m₁}⋯(x − c_k)^{m_k}·g with g ∈ F[x] having no root in F, and m₁ + … + m_k ≤ deg f. Proof: induction on k (k = 0: g = f). f = (x − c₁)^{m₁}g₁ with g₁(c₁) ≠ 0 (`lem-polynomial-multiplicity-cofactor` (a)); for i ≥ 2, mult_{c_i}((x − c₁)^{m₁}) = 0 (its value at c_i is (c_i − c₁)^{m₁} ≠ 0), so mult_{c_i}(g₁) = m_i (`prp-polynomial-multiplicity-product`), and the roots of g₁ are exactly c₂, …, c_k (a root of g₁ is a root of f, and not c₁); apply the hypothesis to g₁. No coprimality is needed. The degree bound by `@thm-degree-of-product`.
- **`def-splits-over-field`.** f ≠ 0 of degree n **splits over F** if f = a(x − a₁)⋯(x − a_n) with a, a₁, …, a_n ∈ F (repetitions allowed; n = 0: a non-zero constant). Then the multiplicities add up to n. (x² − 2 does not split over ℚ; it does over ℝ.)
- **`exm-polynomial-multiplicity-run`.** f = x⁴ − 2x³ + 2x − 1 ∈ ℚ[x]: divide by x − 1 three times (remainders 0, 0, 0, then 2 at the fourth, whose quotient is x + 1); f = (x − 1)³(x + 1); mult₁ = 3, mult_{−1} = 1; f splits over ℚ. (LA.) Sidecar.
- **The formal derivative, recalled from F4.4** (one paragraph, no new label): `@def-formal-derivative` (f′ by coefficients, k·a the k-th multiple; no limits), `@thm-formal-derivative-rules` (linearity, product rule, ((x − c)^m)′ = (m·1)(x − c)^{m−1}), `@prp-formal-derivative-zero` (in characteristic 0, f′ = 0 iff f is constant and deg f′ = deg f − 1; in characteristic p, f′ can vanish for non-constant f). Over 𝔽₂, (x²)′ = 0.
- **`thm-polynomial-repeated-root-test`.** Let f ≠ 0, c ∈ F. (a) c is a multiple root of f iff f(c) = 0 and f′(c) = 0. (b) If mult_c(f) = m ≥ 1 and m·1 ≠ 0 in F, then f′ ≠ 0 and mult_c(f′) = m − 1. Proof: f = (x − c)^m g, g(c) ≠ 0; f′ = (x − c)^{m−1}((m·1)g + (x − c)g′) by `@thm-formal-derivative-rules`; (a) if m ≥ 2 both vanish at c; if m = 1, f′(c) = g(c) ≠ 0; (b) the bracket has value (m·1)g(c) ≠ 0 at c, `lem-polynomial-multiplicity-cofactor` (a). Example of (b) failing: f = x³ in 𝔽₃[x], f′ = 0.
- **`cor-polynomial-squarefree-gcd`.** Let f ∈ F[x], deg f ≥ 1. (a) (any field) If p is irreducible and p² | f, then p | gcd(f, f′); so if gcd(f, f′) = 1, no irreducible divides f twice, and f has no multiple root in F. (b) If F has characteristic 0 (`@def-characteristic`) and gcd(f, f′) ≠ 1, then p² | f for some irreducible p. Proof: (a) f = p²g gives f′ = (2·1)pp′g + p²g′ = p((2·1)p′g + pg′) by `@thm-formal-derivative-rules`, so p divides f and f′, hence gcd(f, f′) (`thm-polynomial-bezout` (b)); (b) an irreducible p | gcd(f, f′); write f = p^m g with p ∤ g, m ≥ 1 (largest m, as for multiplicity; m ≤ deg f); if m = 1: p | f′ = p′g + pg′ gives p | p′g, so p | p′ or p | g (`cor-irreducible-divides-product` (b)); p ∤ g; p′ ≠ 0 has degree deg p − 1 < deg p (`@prp-formal-derivative-zero` (a)), so p ∤ p′; hence m ≥ 2.
- **Check.** Is 1 a multiple root of x³ − 3x + 2? (f(1) = 0 and f′(1) = 3 − 3 = 0: yes; x³ − 3x + 2 = (x − 1)²(x + 2).)

## 05-interpolation-and-congruences.md — Interpolation, Quotient Rings, and the Chinese Remainder Theorem

- **Hook.** A quadratic through (0, 1), (1, 3), (2, 7): is there one, and only one? The answer is a polynomial version of F3.5's Chinese remainder theorem, with the moduli x, x − 1, x − 2.
- **`def-lagrange-polynomials-for-nodes`.** For n ∈ ℕ and distinct c₀, …, c_n ∈ F: ℓ_i ≔ Π_{j ≠ i} (c_i − c_j)⁻¹(x − c_j), i = 0, …, n (products by the `@fd2:def-sum-product` template in F[x]).
- **`thm-polynomial-interpolation`** (Try it first: "find a quadratic through (0, 1), (1, 3), (2, 7) by building, for each node, a polynomial that vanishes at the other two nodes; ten minutes"). Let c₀, …, c_n ∈ F be distinct and y₀, …, y_n ∈ F. (a) ℓ_i(c_j) = 1 if i = j and 0 otherwise; deg ℓ_i = n. (b) p ≔ Σ y_i ℓ_i satisfies deg p ≤ n and p(c_i) = y_i for every i. (c) It is the **only** polynomial of degree ≤ n with these values. (d) Every q ∈ F[x] with deg q ≤ n equals Σ q(c_i)ℓ_i.
  - Proof: (a) **evaluate at a root**: every factor x − c_j with j ≠ i vanishes at c_j; at c_i the product is 1; degree by `@thm-degree-of-product`; (b) `@thm-evaluation-respects-operations`; (c) `thm-polynomial-identity-from-values` (a); (d) (c) applied to q.
  - *Idea* (from the try-it-first's obstacle): solving three equations in three unknown coefficients works but gives no formula; the formula comes from polynomials that are 1 at one node and 0 at the others.
  - Remark: (d) is what the linear algebra book calls "the Lagrange basis"; here it is only a formula (prose, one sentence; no basis is defined).
- **`exm-polynomial-interpolation-cubic`** (LA): nodes −1, 0, 1, 2 with values 2, 1, 2, 11: the ℓ_i, and p = x³ + x² − x + 1. Sidecar.
- **`def-polynomial-congruence`.** For m ∈ F[x]: f ≡ g (mod m) iff m | f − g, that is, f − g ∈ ⟨m⟩: F3.5's relation `a ~_I b` with I = ⟨m⟩. Examples: x² ≡ −1 (mod x² + 1); f ≡ f(c) (mod x − c) (`thm-polynomial-remainder-factor` (a)).
- **`thm-polynomial-quotient-ring`.** Let m ∈ F[x] with d ≔ deg m ≥ 1, and write [f] = f + ⟨m⟩. (a) F[x]/⟨m⟩, with [f] + [g] ≔ [f + g] and [f][g] ≔ [fg], is a commutative ring with zero [0] and one [1] ≠ [0] (`@fd3:thm-quotient-ring-by-ideal` (a)–(d), with I = ⟨m⟩ ≠ F[x] since m ∤ 1 by degree). (b) Each class contains **exactly one** polynomial of degree < d: the remainder on division by m; so f ↦ the remainder induces a bijection from F[x]/⟨m⟩ onto {r ∈ F[x] : deg r < d}. (c) a ↦ [a] (constants) is injective and preserves + and ·. (d) [f] is a unit (`@fd3:def-ring-unit`) iff gcd(f, m) = 1, and then [f]⁻¹ = [a] for any a, b with af + bm = 1. Proof: (b) existence and uniqueness of the remainder (`thm-polynomial-division-remainder`), with "f ≡ g iff equal remainders"; (c) by (b) (distinct constants have degree < d and are distinct remainders); (d) ⇐ Bézout; ⇒ af − 1 ∈ ⟨m⟩, so af + bm = 1 and `cor-polynomial-coprime-iff-combination`. Twin: `@fd3:thm-integers-mod-n-ring` (b) and `@fd3:thm-units-mod-n` (compared with).
- **`thm-polynomial-quotient-field`.** Let m ∈ F[x] with deg m ≥ 1. F[x]/⟨m⟩ is a field (`@fd3:def-commutative-ring-and-field`) iff m is irreducible over F. Proof: ⇐ [f] ≠ [0] means m ∤ f, so gcd(m, f) = 1 (`cor-irreducible-divides-product` (a)) and [f] is a unit; [1] ≠ [0]. ⇒ if m = gh with 1 ≤ deg g, deg h < deg m, then [g], [h] ≠ [0] (degree < deg m, not divisible by m) and [g][h] = [0]; in a field a product of non-zero elements is non-zero (`@fd3:prp-ring-algebra`, as in `@fd3:thm-integers-mod-prime-field`). Twin: `@fd3:thm-integers-mod-prime-field`.
- **`exm-polynomial-quotient-four-elements`.** 𝔽₂[x]/⟨x² + x + 1⟩ has the four elements [0], [1], α ≔ [x], α + [1]; α² = α + 1, α(α + 1) = [1], (α + 1)² = α: a field with four elements (x² + x + 1 has no root in 𝔽₂, so it is irreducible); α³ = [1]. And ℝ[x]/⟨x² + 1⟩: classes [a + bx], with [x]² = [−1] — the complex numbers again (B3, prose pointer back to F4.1). Sidecar checks the table.
- **Warning (false conclusion: "no root ⇒ the quotient is a field").** (x² + 1)² has no real root, but ℝ[x]/⟨(x² + 1)²⟩ is not a field: [x² + 1] ≠ [0] and [x² + 1]² = [0]. Irreducible is the hypothesis, not rootless (§03 warning, again).
- **`lem-polynomial-coprime-to-product`.** (a) If p is coprime to each of q₁, …, q_s, then p is coprime to q₁⋯q_s. (b) If m₁, …, m_k are pairwise coprime and each m_i | h, then m₁⋯m_k | h. Proof: (a) multiply the s Bézout identities a_jp + b_jq_j = 1: the product is (multiple of p) + (Π b_j)(Π q_j) = 1 (expand: every term but one contains a factor a_jp), induction on s; (b) induction on k with (a) and `prp-polynomial-coprime-divisors-product`.
- **`thm-polynomial-chinese-remainder`.** Let m₁, …, m_k ∈ F[x] (k ≥ 1) be non-zero and pairwise coprime, m ≔ m₁⋯m_k. (a) For all r₁, …, r_k ∈ F[x] there is f with f ≡ r_i (mod m_i) for every i; explicitly f = Σ r_i e_i, where e_i ≔ a_i·(m/m_i) with a_i(m/m_i) + b_im_i = 1 (Bézout, by (a) of the lemma), so that e_i ≡ 1 (mod m_i) and e_i ≡ 0 (mod m_j) for j ≠ i. (b) f′ satisfies the same congruences iff f′ ≡ f (mod m). (c) Exactly one such f has deg f < deg m. (d) Φ([f]_m) ≔ ([f]_{m₁}, …, [f]_{m_k}) is a well-defined bijection F[x]/⟨m⟩ → F[x]/⟨m₁⟩ × ⋯ × F[x]/⟨m_k⟩ that preserves + and · (componentwise) and sends [1] to ([1], …, [1]). Proof: (a) compute e_i modulo each m_j; (b) ⇒ each m_i | f′ − f, then `lem-polynomial-coprime-to-product` (b); ⇐ m_i | m; (c) (b) and `thm-polynomial-quotient-ring` (b); (d) well defined coordinate by coordinate, since ⟨m⟩ ⊆ ⟨m_i⟩ (f ≡ g mod m ⇒ f ≡ g mod m_i; the check of F3.2's `@fd3:thm-well-defined-on-classes` (a)); surjective by (a); injective by (b); operations because both sides are classes of f + g and fg. Twin: `@fd3:thm-chinese-remainder-two` (two moduli in ℤ), compared with in a remark.
- **`prp-interpolation-as-chinese-remainder`.** For distinct c₀, …, c_n, the moduli x − c_i are pairwise coprime (`thm-polynomial-remainder-factor`, Bézout with constants: (x − c_i) − (x − c_j) = c_j − c_i ≠ 0), f ≡ y_i (mod x − c_i) iff f(c_i) = y_i, and the idempotents e_i of the theorem, taken of degree ≤ n, are the ℓ_i. ## 06-the-fundamental-theorem.md — The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

- **Hook.** Over ℚ, x² − 2 has no root; over ℝ, x² + 1 has none. Over ℂ, every non-constant polynomial has one. No algebraic identity can prove this, because ℚ(i) = {a + bi : a, b ∈ ℚ} (`@exm-subfields-gaussian`) satisfies every field axiom ℂ does and x² − 2 has no root there: some property of ℝ beyond the field axioms must enter. Here it enters once, as the supremum property of fd3's ℝ.
- **Modulus facts, recalled from F4.1** (one paragraph, no new label): |z|² = (Re z)² + (Im z)² (`@def-conjugate-modulus`); |Re z|, |Im z| ≤ |z| ≤ |Re z| + |Im z| (`@lem-real-part-at-most-modulus`); |zw| = |z||w|, so |z^j| = |z|^j (`@thm-conjugate-properties`, induction); |z₁ + … + z_m| ≤ Σ|z_i| and |z + w| ≥ |z| − |w| (`@cor-complex-triangle-variants`); for real a the modulus is fd3's absolute value (`@prp-modulus-of-real`), so real inequalities between moduli are inequalities in ℝ.
- **`lem-complex-polynomial-growth`** (LA, kept). p = Σ a_j x^j ∈ ℂ[x], n = deg p ≥ 1, A ≔ |a₀| + … + |a_{n−1}|, R ≔ max{1, 2A/|a_n|}. For |z| ≥ R: |p(z)| ≥ ½|a_n||z|^n ≥ ½|a_n||z|. Proof as LA: |Σ_{j<n} a_jz^j| ≤ A|z|^{n−1} for |z| ≥ 1; reverse triangle inequality (`@cor-complex-triangle-variants`).
- **`lem-complex-polynomial-lipschitz`.** Let p = Σ_{j=0}^{n} a_j x^j ∈ ℂ[x] and S ∈ ℝ, S ≥ 0. For z, w ∈ ℂ with |z|, |w| ≤ S: |p(z) − p(w)| ≤ L|z − w|, where L ≔ Σ_{j=1}^{n} j|a_j|S^{j−1}. Proof: z^j − w^j = (z − w)Σ_{i=0}^{j−1} z^i w^{j−1−i} (`@lem-difference-of-powers`); each of the j terms has modulus ≤ S^{j−1}. One sentence: this is the only "continuity" the proof needs, and it comes with a constant.
- **`thm-complex-polynomial-minimum`** (key proof; Before reading on: "why keep a quarter whose **infimum** equals the square's, rather than a quarter containing a point where |p| is smallest?"). For every p ∈ ℂ[x] there is z* ∈ ℂ with |p(z*)| ≤ |p(z)| for **every** z ∈ ℂ.
  - Proof. Constant p: any z*. Let n ≥ 1; R as in the growth lemma; ρ ≔ max{R, 2|p(0)|/|a_n|}. **Step 1 (a square that suffices).** S₀ ≔ S(−ρ, −ρ, 2ρ). If z ∉ S₀, then |Re z| > ρ or |Im z| > ρ, so |z| > ρ ≥ R and |p(z)| ≥ ½|a_n||z| > |p(0)|; and 0 ∈ S₀. **Step 2 (infimum; completeness, first use).** For a square S, V(S) ≔ {|p(z)| : z ∈ S} ⊆ ℝ is non-empty and bounded below by 0; its infimum m(S) exists (the set of lower bounds is non-empty and bounded above by any element of V(S); its supremum, which exists by `@fd3:thm-reals-complete-ordered-field` and `@fd3:def-complete-ordered-field`, is the infimum in the sense of `@fd2:def-order-supremum`; two lines in the text). **Step 3 (quarters).** The four quarters of S(u, v, s), in the fixed order S(u, v, s/2), S(u + s/2, v, s/2), S(u, v + s/2, s/2), S(u + s/2, v + s/2, s/2), cover it; so m(S) = min of the four infima (each quarter's infimum is ≥ m(S), and the least of the four is a lower bound of V(S)). Hence some quarter has m(quarter) = m(S); the **first** such in the fixed order is a rule. **Step 4 (recursion).** By `@fd2:thm-recursion` (read on ω) define S_k = S(u_k, v_k, s_k), s_k = 2ρ/2^k (powers in ℝ, `@fd3:def-naturals-in-ordered-field`), S_{k+1} the first quarter of S_k with m(S_{k+1}) = m(S_k). By induction, m(S_k) = m ≔ m(S₀) and S_{k′} ⊆ S_k for k′ ≥ k, so u_k ≤ u_{k′} ≤ u_k + s_k − s_{k′} (and likewise v). **Step 5 (the common point; completeness, second use).** u* ≔ sup{u_k : k ∈ ℕ} (bounded above by u₀ + s₀); then u_k ≤ u* ≤ u_k + s_k for every k (for k′ ≥ k by Step 4, for k′ < k because u_{k′} ≤ u_k); likewise v*; z* ≔ u* + iv* lies in every S_k. **Step 6 (the value).** For z, w ∈ S_k, |z − w| ≤ |Re(z − w)| + |Im(z − w)| ≤ 2s_k; every point of S₀ has modulus ≤ 2ρ; let L be the Lipschitz constant for S = 2ρ. Let ε ∈ ℝ_{>0}. Choose k with 4ρL/2^k < ε/2 (Archimedes, `@fd3:thm-constructed-reals-archimedean`, with 2^k ≥ k + 1, `@fd3:lem-power-two-bound`). Since m(S_k) = m, **there is** w ∈ S_k with |p(w)| < m + ε/2 (∃E, rule tag shown: the w is used only inside this ε-argument). Then |p(z*)| ≤ |p(w)| + |p(z*) − p(w)| < m + ε/2 + L·2s_k ≤ m + ε. As ε is arbitrary, |p(z*)| ≤ m; and |p(z*)| ≥ m since z* ∈ S₀. **Step 7.** For z ∈ S₀, |p(z)| ≥ m = |p(z*)|; for z ∉ S₀, |p(z)| > |p(0)| ≥ m.
  - *Idea:* the infimum over the big square is what we want to attain; halving keeps a quarter with the **same** infimum, and the squares shrink to one point, where the Lipschitz bound forces the value to equal the infimum.
  - Answer to the prompt: a quarter containing a minimum point is what we are trying to prove exists; the infimum always exists (completeness), and "first in a fixed order" makes the step a rule, so no choice is used (F3.10's `@fd3:exm-representatives-need-choice` is the contrast).
  - Remark (one sentence): completeness is used in Steps 2 and 5 and nowhere else in the chapter; over ℚ(i) Step 2 fails (§06 C1 (c)).
- **`lem-complex-direction-negative-real-part`** (Try it first: "for c = i, find ζ with Re(cζ³) < 0; then with Re(cζ²) < 0; ten minutes"). Let c ∈ ℂ, c ≠ 0, and k ∈ ℕ, k ≥ 1. Then there is ζ ∈ ℂ with Re(cζ^k) < 0.
  - Proof: strong induction on k (`@fd1:thm-strong-induction`). **k odd:** i^k ∈ {i, −i} and (−i)^k = −i^k, so {cζ^k : ζ ∈ {1, −1, i, −i}} = {c, −c, ic, −ic}. If Re c ≠ 0, ζ = 1 or −1 works. If Re c = 0, c = bi with b ∈ ℝ ∖ {0}, and Re(ic) = −b, Re(−ic) = b: one is negative. **k even**, k = 2j with 1 ≤ j < k: by the hypothesis some η has Re(cη^j) < 0; let ζ be a square root of η (`@prp-complex-square-roots`; η ≠ 0 since Re(cη^j) ≠ 0); then ζ^k = (ζ²)^j = η^j (`@prp-power-laws-commutative-ring` (b)).
  - *Idea* (from the obstacles of the try-it-first): for k = 3 the four "axis" directions already turn c around; for k = 2 they do not (ζ² ∈ {1, −1} for ζ ∈ {±1, ±i}, which fails when c = i), and a square root of a direction that works for k = 1 does.
  - Remark: with polar form one would take a k-th root of −c̄/|c|; this proof needs only square roots, which fd3 supplies.
- **`lem-complex-polynomial-descent`** (d'Alembert; self-explanation prompt: "where is k, the first index with b_k ≠ 0, used?"). Let p ∈ ℂ[x] be non-constant and z₀ ∈ ℂ with p(z₀) ≠ 0. Then |p(z)| < |p(z₀)| for some z ∈ ℂ.
  - Proof: expand p = Σ_{j=0}^{n} b_j(x − z₀)^j (`thm-polynomial-taylor-shift`): b₀ = p(z₀) ≠ 0, b_n ≠ 0 with n ≥ 1, so k ≔ the least j ≥ 1 with b_j ≠ 0 exists. c ≔ b_k/b₀; ζ with Re(cζ^k) < 0 (previous lemma); u ≔ cζ^k, α ≔ −Re u > 0 (so α ≤ |u|); B ≔ Σ_{j>k} |b_j/b₀||ζ|^j. For real t with 0 < t ≤ 1: p(z₀ + tζ) = b₀(1 + t^k u + E), |E| ≤ t^{k+1}B (t^j ≤ t^{k+1} for j ≥ k + 1). For 0 < s ≤ α/|u|²: |1 + su|² = 1 − 2sα + s²|u|² ≤ 1 − sα ≤ (1 − sα/2)², and 1 − sα/2 > 0 (sα ≤ α²/|u|² ≤ 1), so |1 + su| ≤ 1 − sα/2 (`@fd3:prp-ordered-ring-rules`: squares of non-negatives preserve order). Choose t ≔ min{1, α/|u|², α/(4B)} (omit the last if B = 0); then s ≔ t^k ≤ t ≤ α/|u|² and tB ≤ α/4, so |p(z₀ + tζ)| ≤ |b₀|(1 − t^kα/2 + t^{k+1}B) ≤ |b₀|(1 − t^kα/4) < |b₀|.
  - *Idea:* near z₀, p(z₀ + h) ≈ b₀ + b_kh^k; choose the direction of h so that b_kh^k points back toward 0, and h so small that the higher terms cannot undo the gain.
- **`exm-complex-polynomial-descent-step`.** p = x³ + x² + 1, z₀ = 0: b₀ = 1, k = 2, b₂ = 1, c = 1. Direction: for k = 1 the lemma gives η = −1 (Re(−1) < 0), and a square root of −1 is ζ = i; u = −1, α = 1, |u|² = 1, B = |b₃||ζ|³ = 1, t = min{1, 1, 1/4} = 1/4. p(i/4) = 15/16 − i/64, |p(i/4)|² = 3601/4096 < 1; the proof's bound 1 − (1/16)(1/4) = 63/64 holds (3601/4096 ≤ (63/64)² = 3969/4096). - **`thm-fundamental-theorem-algebra`.** Every non-constant p ∈ ℂ[x] has a root in ℂ. Proof: z* from `thm-complex-polynomial-minimum`; if p(z*) ≠ 0, `lem-complex-polynomial-descent` gives a smaller value: contradiction. This proves F4.1's `@thm-fta-statement`: Σ a_k z^k with a_n ≠ 0, n ≥ 1, is the value at z of the non-constant polynomial Σ a_k x^k (`@def-polynomial-evaluation`); the text says so in one sentence after the proof.
- **`cor-complex-polynomials-linear-factors`.** (a) Every non-zero p ∈ ℂ[x] splits over ℂ (`def-splits-over-field`): p = a(x − z₁)⋯(x − z_n), n = deg p; the multiplicities of its roots add up to n. (b) The irreducible polynomials of ℂ[x] are exactly those of degree 1. Proof: `thm-polynomial-roots-with-multiplicity`, the rootless cofactor is constant by the theorem; (b) by (a).
- **`exm-factor-x4-plus-one-reals`.** Over ℂ: x⁴ + 1 = (x² − i)(x² + i); square roots of ±i by the formula: ±(√2/2)(1 + i), ±(√2/2)(1 − i); so x⁴ + 1 = Π (x − z) over these four. Sidecar checks the product identity with √2 symbolic (a² = 2).
- **`prp-real-polynomial-conjugate-roots`.** For f ∈ ℂ[x] let f̄ be f with conjugated coefficients. (a) f ↦ f̄ is a ring homomorphism ℂ[x] → ℂ[x] (`@thm-polynomial-substitution` with φ = conjugation followed by the constants, a ring homomorphism by `@exm-complex-conjugation-automorphism`, and s = x), and conj(f(z)) = f̄(z̄) (for real coefficients this is F4.1's `@prp-real-coefficients-conjugate-roots`; in general the same computation with `@thm-conjugate-properties`). (b) If f ∈ ℝ[x] (f̄ = f) and f ≠ 0, then z is a root iff z̄ is, and mult_{z̄}(f) = mult_z(f). Proof: (b) conjugate f = (x − z)^m g, g(z) ≠ 0: f = (x − z̄)^m ḡ with ḡ(z̄) = conj(g(z)) ≠ 0; `lem-polynomial-multiplicity-cofactor` (a).
- **`cor-real-irreducible-polynomials`.** The irreducible polynomials of ℝ[x] are exactly those of degree 1 and those of degree 2 with no real root. Proof: ⇐ `prp-irreducible-low-degree`. ⇒ p irreducible has a complex root z (theorem); z real ⇒ x − z | p in ℝ[x] and p is an associate of x − z; z non-real ⇒ z̄ ≠ z is a root too, so q ≔ (x − z)(x − z̄) = x² − 2(Re z)x + |z|² ∈ ℝ[x] divides p in ℂ[x] (`thm-polynomial-root-count`), hence in ℝ[x] (`prp-polynomial-gcd-field-extension` (b)); irreducible ⇒ p is an associate of q.
- **`thm-real-polynomial-factorization`.** Every non-constant f ∈ ℝ[x] is f = a(x − c₁)⋯(x − c_r)q₁⋯q_s with a the leading coefficient, c_i ∈ ℝ, q_j monic quadratics without real root; r + 2s = deg f; unique up to order (`thm-polynomial-unique-factorization` with the corollary).
- **`cor-real-odd-degree-root`.** A real polynomial of odd degree has a real root (r + 2s odd ⇒ r ≥ 1). One sentence: an1 proves this again with the intermediate value theorem (prose, no `@ref`; the forward-pointer budget of §06 is spent here).
- **Warning.** "No real root" does not make a real polynomial irreducible beyond degree 2: x⁴ + 1 (the example).
