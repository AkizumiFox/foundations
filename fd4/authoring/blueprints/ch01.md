**Purpose.**
- fd3 ended with a complete ordered field ℝ (`@fd3:thm-reals-complete-ordered-field`), unique up to unique isomorphism (F3.4). This chapter builds ℂ ≔ ℝ² on it and proves it is a **field** in fd3's sense (`@fd3:def-commutative-ring-and-field`), containing ℝ as the pairs (a, 0).
- Conjugation and modulus, division, the triangle inequality; every nonzero complex number has exactly two square roots (by formula, from real square roots alone); the quadratic formula; **no order makes ℂ an ordered field**.
- Polar form, multiplication as "multiply moduli, add angles", De Moivre, n-th roots and roots of unity. These rest on five facts about cos, sin and π that this volume **borrows** from the analysis volume (AN1.10), stated once as `ax-cosine-sine`, ledgered, and used nowhere before §05's second half.
- State the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra (proof in Chapter 5, F4.5) and prove the conjugate-pair property of real coefficients.
- Serve the volumes that need ℂ: linear algebra (LA1–LA4, MA, NLA: conjugation and modulus for inner products, the triangle inequality, De Moivre, roots of unity for the DFT), the algebra volume AL1 and AL4.8 (roots of unity, characters), and fd4 itself (F4.2 fields, F4.3 groups, F4.5 the FTA).
- The reader leaves able to (i) compute in ℂ by hand, including division and square roots; (ii) turn a modulus statement into an algebraic one by squaring (|u|² = u ū); (iii) solve zⁿ = c by comparing moduli and angles, and count the solutions; (iv) say exactly which facts about ℂ come from ℝ's field axioms, which from ℝ's completeness, and which from trigonometry.

**Signature moves** (`index.md`, "Named moves").
- **Compare real and imaginary parts.** An equation in ℂ is two equations in ℝ (a + bi = c + di ⟺ a = c and b = d). Used for every law in §01, for square roots (§04), for i's two square roots of −1.
- **Square the modulus.** |u|² = u ū is algebra, |u| is not; so square, expand with conjugates, bound or compare, and take the nonnegative square root back (`@fd3:prp-ordered-ring-rules` (g)). **Main move: the faded triple** (§03).
- **Multiply moduli, add angles.** In polar form, multiplication is a product of moduli and a sum of arguments; an equation zⁿ = c splits into rⁿ = ρ (a real equation) and nθ ∈ α + 2πℤ (an angle equation).

## index.md

- **Story.** No real number squares to −1 (F3.9's §01 C1: in an ordered field nonzero squares are positive). Rather than "invent i and hope", build the new numbers from objects already trusted: pairs of real numbers, multiplied by the formula that i² = −1 would force. Then check, law by law, that nothing breaks: ℂ is a field. Two habits from ℝ do not survive: order (no ordering of ℂ is compatible with + and ·) and the product rule for √. One new habit replaces order: measure a complex number by its real modulus, and compute with |u|² = u ū. The picture of multiplication (rotate and stretch) needs angles, and angles need cos and sin, which belong to analysis; the chapter borrows exactly five facts about them, says so, and derives everything else (special values, De Moivre, n-th roots) from those five.
- **What you need:** fd3's ℝ as a complete ordered field (F3.10), its square roots (F3.4), its absolute value (F3.9); fd3's field axioms (F3.2); division with remainder in ℤ (F3.5); fd2's ordered pairs, functions and recursion. **Nothing from analysis** before §05's box of borrowed facts.
- **Time:** core ≈ 14 hours; full ≈ 22 (drafter re-estimates per STYLE §11.9).
- **Which ℝ?** (one paragraph). ℂ is built on F3.10's ℝ; by F3.4 (`@fd3:thm-cof-uniqueness`) any complete ordered field would give "the same" ℂ, so nothing below depends on the Cauchy-sequence model; no proof looks inside a real number.
- **What this chapter borrows** (one paragraph, then the list in §05). Everything in §§01–04 and in the first part of §05 is proved from fd3's ℝ. From `ax-cosine-sine` on, five facts about cos, sin and π are used without proof; the analysis volume (*Analysis on the Real Line*, its chapter on power series and the elementary functions) proves them. Results that use them say so in their dependency ledger.
- **Roadmap; Named moves:** the three above. §01 pairs and the laws; §02 conjugate, modulus, division (ℂ is a field); §03 the triangle inequality; §04 square roots, quadratics, and the missing order; §05 the unit circle and polar form; §06 n-th roots, roots of unity, and a look ahead at the FTA.

## 01-pairs-of-reals.md — The Complex Numbers as Pairs of Reals

- **Hook.** (Migrated, lightly edited.) x² + 1 = 0 has no real solution; later volumes meet real problems (a rotation of the plane) whose key numbers are not real. "Invent i with i² = −1" invites the worry that 0·z = 1 would be just as inventable. Build instead. Expand (a + bi)(c + di) pretending i² = −1: the product should be (ac − bd) + (ad + bc)i; take this as the definition of a product of pairs.
- **`def-complex-numbers`** (migrated statement, with fd2/fd3 citations). ℂ ≔ ℝ² = ℝ × ℝ (`@fd2:def-cartesian-product`, ℝ from `@fd3:thm-reals-complete-ordered-field`), with (a, b) + (c, d) ≔ (a + c, b + d) and (a, b)(c, d) ≔ (ac − bd, ad + bc). Notation i ≔ (0, 1) and a for (a, 0) **once `prp-reals-embed-complex` is proved** (the definition introduces the symbol i only; the a = (a, 0) convention is stated after the proposition); Re z ≔ a, Im z ≔ b for z = (a, b). Equality of pairs is `@fd2:def-ordered-pair`'s characteristic property: this is **compare real and imaginary parts**, named here.
- **`prp-reals-embed-complex`.** The map ι: ℝ → ℂ, ι(a) ≔ (a, 0), is injective and satisfies ι(a + c) = ι(a) + ι(c), ι(ac) = ι(a)ι(c), ι(1) = (1, 0). Moreover (a, b) = ι(a) + ι(b)·i for all a, b ∈ ℝ, and i·i = ι(−1). Proof: the two "checks" of the source (`(a, 0)(c, 0) = (ac − 0·0, a·0 + 0·c) = (ac, 0)`, using `@fd3:prp-ring-algebra` (b)); injectivity by `@fd2:def-ordered-pair`; (a, 0) + (b, 0)(0, 1) = (a, 0) + (0, b). Then the convention: write a for ι(a), so ℝ ⊆ ℂ and (a, b) = a + bi, "a convenient abuse of notation made honest by the injective embedding" (fd3's phrasing for ℕ ⊆ ℤ ⊆ ℚ, F3.2 §02, pointed back to). One sentence: Re z and Im z are real numbers; Im(3 − 4i) = −4, not −4i.
- **`exm-complex-arithmetic`** (migrated): (2 + 3i)(1 − i) = 5 + i; i⁰, …, i⁷ (period 4; i⁰ = 1 by the power recursion of `prp-power-laws-commutative-ring`, so the example comes **after** that proposition, or computes i², i³, i⁴ as products and defers "period 4" to B1); (a + bi)(a − bi) = a² + b², a nonnegative real, "met again as |z|²".
- **Non-example by minimal change** (migrated; main line, since it is the first place the reader meets it, STYLE §12.3): (a, b) ∗ (c, d) ≔ (ac + bd, ad + bc) ("j² = +1"): commutative with unit (1, 0), but (1, 1) ∗ (1, −1) = (0, 0), and (1, 1) has no inverse ((1, 1) ∗ (c, d) = (c + d, c + d) ≠ (1, 0)). "The single sign in ac − bd is what makes division possible." Exercise B3 finds all zero divisors.
- **`prp-complex-arithmetic-laws`** (migrated statement and proof). For all z, w, u ∈ ℂ: (a) commutativity of + and ·; (b) associativity of + and ·; (c) distributivity; (d) z + 0 = z, z·1 = z, z + (−z) = 0 with −(a + bi) ≔ (−a) + (−b)i. Proof: coordinatewise for +; (a), (c), (d) one line each; (b) expand both sides, both real parts equal ace − adf − bcf − bde, both imaginary parts acf + ade + bce − bdf (sidecar checks the polynomial identity). Remark after: in fd3's language these are (F1)–(F7) and (F9) of `@fd3:def-commutative-ring-and-field` (the numbering is fd3's; the drafter matches each clause), together with 1 ≠ 0 ((1, 0) ≠ (0, 0)): **ℂ is a commutative ring with 1 ≠ 0**, so every result fd3 proved for commutative rings holds in ℂ (`@fd3:prp-ring-algebra`: −(−z) = z, z·0 = 0, (−z)w = −(zw), (z + w)² = z² + 2zw + w²). The field axiom (F8) waits for §02.
- **`prp-power-laws-commutative-ring`.** Let R be a commutative ring (`@fd3:def-commutative-ring-and-field`). Powers zⁿ (n ∈ ℕ) are defined by the recursion of `@fd3:def-naturals-in-ordered-field` (b), which uses no order: z⁰ ≔ 1, zⁿ⁺¹ ≔ zⁿz (`@fd2:thm-recursion` on ω, `@fd3:thm-omega-recursion`). Then for z, w ∈ R and m, n ∈ ℕ: (a) z^{m+n} = z^m zⁿ; (b) (z^m)ⁿ = z^{mn}; (c) (zw)ⁿ = zⁿwⁿ; (d) if R is a field and z ≠ 0, then zⁿ ≠ 0. Proof: `@fd1:ax-induction` on n each time ((a) uses associativity; (b) uses (a); (c) commutativity; (d) no zero divisors, `@fd3:lem-field-fraction-rules` (d)). Remark: for R = ω this is F3.1's `@fd3:prp-omega-powers-parity` (a) (compared with); stated once here for every commutative ring so that F4.2–F4.5 can cite it.

## 03-triangle-inequality.md — The Triangle Inequality

- **Hook.** |z − w| is the distance between the points z and w of the plane (Pythagoras, prose). The side of a triangle is at most the sum of the other two. In an ordered field F3.9 proved |x + y| ≤ |x| + |y| by trapping x between ±|x| (`@fd3:prp-absolute-value-ordered-field` (e)). That route needs x ≤ |x|, a comparison ℂ cannot make (§04 proves there is no order). The replacement: compare **real** numbers only, by squaring.
- **`lem-real-part-at-most-modulus`.** For u ∈ ℂ: |Re u| ≤ |u|, |Im u| ≤ |u|, and |u| ≤ |Re u| + |Im u|. In particular Re u ≤ |u|. Proof: with u = x + yi, x² ≤ x² + y² (`@fd3:prp-ordered-ring-rules` (e), (b)), and for nonnegatives a < b iff a² < b² (`@fd3:prp-ordered-ring-rules` (g)), with `prp-modulus-of-real` for |x|; the last: (|x| + |y|)² = x² + y² + 2|x||y| ≥ |u|² (`@fd3:prp-absolute-value-ordered-field` (b)), then (g).
- **`thm-complex-triangle-inequality`** (migrated statement; key proof; Try it first: "show |z + w|² ≤ (|z| + |w|)² by expanding both sides with |u|² = u ū; ten minutes, and note which term you cannot yet bound"; Before reading on: "where is Re u ≤ |u| used, and why may we compare squares?"). ## 04-square-roots-and-order.md — Square Roots, Quadratic Equations, and the Missing Order

- **Hook.** fd1 (F1.4 §11, a check about models) said, as a school fact, that every complex number has a complex square root. Now prove it, without angles: from real square roots alone. Then see what ℂ loses: order, and the rule √x√y = √(xy).

## 05-polar-form.md — The Unit Circle and Polar Form

- **Hook.** Addition in ℂ is addition of arrows. Multiplication also has a picture (migrated figure: z, w, zw with moduli 1.5, 1.2 and angles 20°, 50°): "multiply the lengths, add the angles". The lengths are already here; the angles need cos and sin, which this volume does not construct.

## 06-roots-of-unity.md — n-th Roots, Roots of Unity, and a Look Ahead

- **Hook.** Polar form turns zⁿ = c into a question about angles (migrated). Before solving it, one real equation must be solvable: rⁿ = ρ for ρ > 0.
- **`lem-reals-nth-root`.** Let n ∈ ℕ, n ≥ 1. (a) For s, t ∈ ℝ with 0 ≤ s < t: sⁿ < tⁿ. (b) For every a ∈ ℝ with a ≥ 0 there is exactly one b ∈ ℝ with b ≥ 0 and bⁿ = a, written ⁿ√a; for n = 2 it is `@fd3:prp-cof-square-roots`'s √a.
  - Proof (the move **construct a point as a supremum**, fd3's, recalled from F3.4 where it built square roots; the second use in this volume, so no fading). (a) tⁿ − sⁿ = (t − s)Σ_{k=0}^{n−1} t^k s^{n−1−k} (`lem-difference-of-powers`), and the sum is ≥ t^{n−1} > 0 (every term ≥ 0, the k = n − 1 term is t^{n−1}). (b) a = 0: b = 0. a > 0: S ≔ {y ∈ ℝ : y ≥ 0, yⁿ ≤ a} contains m ≔ min{1, a} (yⁿ ≤ y for 0 ≤ y ≤ 1) and is bounded above by M ≔ max{1, a} (y > M gives yⁿ ≥ y > a); s ≔ sup S exists (`@fd3:def-complete-ordered-field`, `@fd3:thm-reals-complete-ordered-field`) and s ≥ m > 0. If sⁿ < a: for 0 < h ≤ 1, (s + h)ⁿ − sⁿ ≤ h·n(s + 1)^{n−1} (difference of powers, each of the n terms ≤ (s + 1)^{n−1}); h ≔ min{1, (a − sⁿ)/(2n(s + 1)^{n−1})} puts s + h in S, against s = sup S. If sⁿ > a: h ≔ (sⁿ − a)/(n s^{n−1}) satisfies 0 < h < s, and sⁿ − (s − h)ⁿ ≤ h·n s^{n−1} = sⁿ − a, so (s − h)ⁿ ≥ a; every y ∈ S has yⁿ ≤ a ≤ (s − h)ⁿ, hence y ≤ s − h by (a) (contrapositive), so s − h < s is an upper bound: contradiction. Hence sⁿ = a. Uniqueness by (a). Constants derived backwards in the Idea, placed first in the proof (as F3.4 does). Sidecar checks the two inequalities numerically for sample (a, n).
  - Ledger: finite sums and "a sum of n terms each ≤ M is ≤ n·M" (induction on n, `@fd1:ax-induction`; template line).
- **`thm-complex-nth-roots`** (key proof; Before reading on: "where is the uniqueness half of the polar form used, and where the uniqueness of the real n-th root?"). Let n ≥ 1 and c ∈ ℂ, c ≠ 0, with polar form c = ρ e^{iα} (`prp-polar-form`). For k ∈ ℤ put z_k ≔ ⁿ√ρ · e^{i(α + 2πk)/n}. Then: (a) for z ∈ ℂ, zⁿ = c iff z = z_k for some k ∈ ℤ; (b) z_k = z_l iff n | k − l (`@fd2:def-divides`, on fd3's ℤ); (c) the map {0, 1, …, n − 1} → {z ∈ ℂ : zⁿ = c}, k ↦ z_k, is a bijection, so c has exactly n n-th roots (`@fd2:def-finite`). In particular every nonzero complex number has an n-th root, and every u ∈ 𝕋 has one in 𝕋.
  - Proof. z = 0 is no solution (0ⁿ = 0 ≠ c for n ≥ 1). Write z = r e^{iθ} (`prp-polar-form` (a)); zⁿ = rⁿ e^{inθ} (`thm-de-moivre`); by `prp-polar-form` (b), zⁿ = c iff rⁿ = ρ and nθ − α ∈ 2πℤ, iff r = ⁿ√ρ (`lem-reals-nth-root` (b), uniqueness) and θ = (α + 2πk)/n for some k ∈ ℤ; then z = z_k (and conversely z_kⁿ = c by the same computation). (b): `prp-polar-form` (b) again: z_k = z_l iff 2π(k − l)/n ∈ 2πℤ iff (k − l)/n ∈ ℤ iff n | k − l. (c): injective on {0, …, n − 1} by (b) (0 < |k − l| < n, `@fd3:prp-divisibility-rules-integers` (e) in prose form: a positive multiple of n is ≥ n); surjective by (a) and division with remainder (`@fd3:thm-integers-division-remainder`: k = qn + r, z_k = z_r by (b)).
  - Answer to the prompt: uniqueness of polar form converts zⁿ = c into the two equations; uniqueness of the real root forces r; the 2πℤ ambiguity is exactly what produces n roots instead of one.
