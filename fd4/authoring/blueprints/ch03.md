**Purpose.**
- The reader has met several structures that behave alike: (ℤ, +) and the commutative rings of fd3, the units R^× of a commutative ring (F3.5), the non-zero elements of a field (F3.2, F4.2), and the bijections of a set under composition (fd2). Name the common structure, prove its basic laws **once** from three axioms, and show that every later proof that uses only those axioms holds in all of them at once.
- Make "drop the brackets" and powers g^n (n ∈ ℤ) honest: products of finite lists by recursion, with the concatenation and exponent laws proved, so that later volumes can write g_1 ⋯ g_r and g^n without comment.
- Pay F3.5's two pointers: the subgroups of (ℤ, +) are exactly the sets nℤ (F3.5 §04 C2 is an exercise there; here it is a theorem), and the units of a commutative ring form a group (F3.5 §05).
- Give linear algebra what its determinant chapter uses: S_n, cycle notation with the right-to-left convention, and "every permutation is a product of transpositions"; and homomorphisms with kernels, which LA1.2's linear maps and LA2.7's det : GL_n(F) → F^× instantiate.
- The reader leaves able to (i) decide whether a set with an operation is a group, checking closure first; (ii) prove a subgroup by the one-step test; (iii) compute products of cycles right to left and decompose a permutation into disjoint cycles and into transpositions; (iv) check that a map is a homomorphism and compute its kernel.

**Signature moves** (`index.md`, "Named moves").
- **Multiply by the inverse.** To remove a factor from one side of an equation in a group, multiply both sides **on the same side** by its inverse, then regroup with (G1). Cancellation, uniqueness of identity and inverses, φ(e) = e, all come from it. (Faded triple.)
- **Check closure first.** Before (G1)–(G3), check that the operation lands in the set (the warning of §01; the subgroup test packs closure into one condition).
- **Least positive element, then divide** (fd3's move from F3.5, reused): in a subgroup of ℤ the least positive element divides every element.
- **Follow the orbit, then peel it off.** Start at a moved point, apply σ until you return, record the cycle, remove it, and induct on what is left (§04's decomposition); "fix one point, then induct" is its one-transposition version (§05).

## index.md

- **Story.** Four rules keep recurring: in (ℤ, +), among the units of ℤ/nℤ, among the non-zero rationals, among the bijections of a set, you can combine two things and stay in the set, the combining is associative, something does nothing, and everything can be undone. Name that pattern a group, prove its laws once, and it holds in all of them. Then the structure's substructures (subgroups: the subgroups of ℤ are exactly the sets nℤ, by the move that gave gcds in F3.5), its maps (homomorphisms, whose kernels measure what they forget), and finally one group in detail: the permutations of {1, …, n}, which every determinant in linear algebra is built from. Every permutation breaks into disjoint cycles, and every one is a product of swaps; how many swaps, the book's later volumes settle.
- **What you need:** fd2 Chapter 1 (functions, composition, bijections and inverses) and finite sets (fd2 Chapter 3 §01); fd3 Chapter 2's commutative rings and ℤ, Chapter 3's division with remainder, ℤ/nℤ and units. ℂ (Chapter 1) and fields (Chapter 2) of this volume appear in examples only.
- **Time:** core ≈ 11 hours; full ≈ 18 (drafter re-estimates per STYLE §11.9).
- **"A language more than a subject" (paragraph).** Linear algebra and algebra both use groups; this chapter gives the vocabulary and the few facts used constantly. Order of an element, cyclic groups, cosets and Lagrange's theorem, the sign of a permutation, and quotient groups belong to *Groups* (AL1) and to the linear algebra volumes; the index lists them once, as pointers, so the reader knows where they are.
- **Roadmap; Named moves:** the four above.

## 01-groups.md — Groups

- **Hook** (adapted from the source's opening, with matrices replaced). Four situations side by side: (ℤ, +), with 0 doing nothing and −a undoing a; ℚ ∖ {0} under ·; the units of ℤ/12ℤ under ·, [1], [5], [7], [11], each its own inverse (F3.5's `@fd3:thm-units-mod-n`); the bijections of a set under ∘. The objects differ, the rules used are the same three.
- **`def-group`** (source wording kept): a set G with a binary operation G × G → G, (a, b) ↦ ab (a function, `@fd2:def-function`, on `@fd2:def-cartesian-product`), with (G1) associativity, (G2) an identity e with ea = ae = a for every a, (G3) for every a some b with ab = ba = e. **Abelian** if ab = ba for all a, b. The "In words" paragraph of the source kept: closure is built into "G × G → G"; (G2) and (G3) are two-sided. Additive notation for abelian groups only.
- **`exm-groups`** (moved label; content changed: no GL_n(F)). Check each is a group, decide which are abelian: (a) (ℤ, +) and, for any commutative ring R, (R, +): (G1)–(G3) are clauses (F2)–(F4) of `@fd3:def-commutative-ring-and-field` with (F1) making 0 and −a two-sided; abelian. In particular (ℚ, +), (ℝ, +) (`@fd3:thm-reals-ordered-field`; example only) and (ℤ/nℤ, +) (`@fd3:thm-integers-mod-n-ring` (a)). (b) (ℚ ∖ {0}, ·): closure because a product of non-zero rationals is non-zero (`@fd3:thm-rationals-field`; in a field xy = 0 with x ≠ 0 gives y = x^{-1}xy = x^{-1}·0 = 0 by `@fd3:prp-ring-algebra` (b), the argument F3.5 writes in the proof of `@fd3:thm-integers-mod-prime-field`); identity 1 ≠ 0; inverses by (F8); abelian. Same for any field F, so (ℤ/pℤ ∖ {[0]}, ·) for p prime (`@fd3:thm-integers-mod-prime-field`). (c) The bijections X → X of a set X under ∘: closure by `@fd2:thm-composition-injective-surjective` (c), (G1) and (G2) with id_X by `@fd2:prp-composition-associative`, (G3) by `@fd2:thm-bijection-iff-inverse` and `@fd2:def-inverse-function` (the inverse is a bijection, being invertible). ## 03-homomorphisms.md — Homomorphisms and Isomorphisms

- **Hook.** Two groups can be "the same table with different names": ℤ/2ℤ under + and {1, −1} under ·; ℤ/4ℤ and the rotations of a square. A map that respects the operation makes this precise, and the elements it sends to the identity measure what it forgets.
- **`def-group-homomorphism`** (moved label; source wording kept): φ : G → H with φ(ab) = φ(a)φ(b); bijective ⇒ **isomorphism**; ker φ ≔ {g ∈ G : φ(g) = e_H}. Added: G and H are **isomorphic**, G ≅ H, if an isomorphism G → H exists. The source's paragraph (products on each side taken in different groups) kept.
- **Product group** (unlabelled paragraph, proved in the text, before the examples). For groups G, H, the set G × H (`@fd2:def-cartesian-product`) with (g, h)(g′, h′) ≔ (gg′, hh′) is a group: (G1) holds in each component; (e_G, e_H) is an identity; (g^{-1}, h^{-1}) is an inverse of (g, h) (three componentwise lines, written out). Abelian when G and H are. In additive notation for abelian groups, as in F3.5's componentwise + on ℤ/mℤ × ℤ/nℤ.

## 04-permutations-and-cycles.md — Permutations and Cycle Notation

- **Hook.** Shuffle three cards; shuffle again. Two shuffles in a row are a shuffle, and the order matters. This section writes shuffles compactly, multiplies them, and shows that each one breaks into independent loops.
- **`def-symmetric-group`.** For a set X, S_X is the group of bijections X → X under composition (`exm-groups` (c)); its elements are **permutations** of X. S_n ≔ S_{{1,…,n}} for n ∈ ℕ, the **symmetric group**; S_0 = {the empty function} (`@fd2:prp-empty-function`). Two-line notation. **Convention:** στ ≔ σ ∘ τ, first τ, then σ; products of permutations are read right to left (source's paragraph kept). **Support:** supp σ ≔ {i ∈ X : σ(i) ≠ i}.
- **`def-cycle`.** For k ≥ 2 and **distinct** i_1, …, i_k ∈ {1, …, n}, the **k-cycle** (i_1 i_2 ⋯ i_k) ∈ S_n sends i_j ↦ i_{j+1} (j < k), i_k ↦ i_1, and fixes every other point; it is a bijection (its inverse is (i_k ⋯ i_2 i_1), checked pointwise). A 2-cycle is a **transposition**. Cycles are **disjoint** if their supports are disjoint (supp (i_1 ⋯ i_k) = {i_1, …, i_k}). Rotation of the written cycle: (1 2 3) = (2 3 1) = (3 1 2) ≠ (1 3 2). The identity is not a cycle (k ≥ 2); it is the empty product (`def-group-list-product`).
- **`exm-cycle-notation`** (moved label; source's statement and solution kept): (1 3)(1 2) = (1 2 3), (1 2)(1 3) = (1 3 2), so S_n is not abelian for n ≥ 3 (the same two transpositions, fixing everything ≥ 4); the σ ∈ S_5 with bottom row 3 5 1 2 4 (1 ↦ 3, 2 ↦ 5, 3 ↦ 1, 4 ↦ 2, 5 ↦ 4) is (1 3)(2 5 4).
- **Warning (source's "Mind the order", matrices removed):** στ applies τ first, as g ∘ f applies f first (`@fd2:def-composition`); reading a product of cycles left to right gives (1 3)(1 2) "=" (1 3 2), wrong.

- **Check** (source): (1 2 3)(1 2) = (1 3).
- **`lem-permutations-fixing-last-point`.** Let n ∈ ℕ and S′ ≔ {σ ∈ S_{n+1} : σ(n + 1) = n + 1} (a subgroup, `exm-subgroups` (e)). For σ ∈ S′, the restriction σ′ of σ to {1, …, n} is a permutation of {1, …, n}, and σ ↦ σ′ is an isomorphism S′ → S_n whose inverse extends ρ ∈ S_n by n + 1 ↦ n + 1. Proof: σ(i) ≠ n + 1 for i ≤ n (injectivity), so σ′ maps into {1, …, n}; injective as a restriction; **surjective directly**: for j ≤ n, j = σ(i) for some i, and i ≠ n + 1 since σ(n + 1) = n + 1 ≠ j. (This replaces the source's appeal to "an injective map of a finite set to itself is surjective", the linear algebra book's thm-finite-injective-iff-surjective, which fd2 does not state in that form.) Restriction respects composition; the two maps are mutually inverse (`@fd2:prp-function-equality`).
- **`prp-symmetric-group-order`.** card S_n = n! for every n ∈ ℕ (n! by the recursion of `@fd2:def-sum-product`, used as a pattern on ℕ). Proof outline: `@fd1:ax-induction` on n. n = 0: S_0 has one element, 0! = 1. Step: for k ∈ {1, …, n + 1}, A_k ≔ {σ ∈ S_{n+1} : σ(n + 1) = k}; the A_k partition S_{n+1}. A_{n+1} = S′ has n! elements (lemma, induction hypothesis). For k ≤ n, σ ↦ (k n+1)σ maps A_k into A_{n+1} and is its own inverse in the sense that τ ↦ (k n+1)τ maps A_{n+1} back ((k n+1)^2 = id); so card A_k = n! (compose with a numbering of A_{n+1}, `@fd2:def-finite` and `@fd2:thm-composition-injective-surjective` (c)). Then card S_{n+1} = (n + 1)·n! by `@fd2:prp-disjoint-union-count` applied n times (induction on the number of pieces).
- **`prp-disjoint-cycles-commute`.** If σ, τ ∈ S_n have disjoint supports, then στ = τσ; and σ maps supp σ onto itself. Proof (the source's §10 C2 (b) solution, promoted): σ(supp σ) ⊆ supp σ (if σ(i) were fixed, σ(σ(i)) = σ(i) and injectivity gives σ(i) = i); and supp σ ⊆ σ(supp σ): for j ∈ supp σ let i ≔ σ^{-1}(j); if i ∉ supp σ, then j = σ(i) = i ∉ supp σ, a contradiction; so σ(supp σ) = supp σ; then three cases i ∈ supp σ, i ∈ supp τ, neither.
- **`thm-disjoint-cycle-decomposition`** (key proof; Before reading on: "why must the orbit of i return to i itself, and not to some later point?"). Every σ ∈ S_n is a product of pairwise disjoint cycles, each with support contained in supp σ (the empty product when σ = id). (Existence only; uniqueness up to order: AL1.3, pointer.) The clause on supports is part of the statement because the induction needs it.
  - Proof. `@fd1:thm-strong-induction` on m ≔ card supp σ (supp σ ⊆ {1, …, n} is finite, `@fd2:prp-subset-finite`). m = 0: σ = id. Otherwise pick i ∈ supp σ. **Follow the orbit:** the n + 1 values σ^0(i), …, σ^n(i) lie in {1, …, n}, so two coincide (`@fd2:cor-pigeonhole-finite-sets` (a)), σ^a(i) = σ^b(i) with a < b; multiply by (σ^a)^{-1}: σ^{b−a}(i) = i. Let r be the least positive integer with σ^r(i) = i (`@fd3:prp-integer-least-element`); r ≥ 2 since σ(i) ≠ i. The points i, σ(i), …, σ^{r−1}(i) are distinct (σ^c(i) = σ^d(i) with 0 ≤ c < d < r gives σ^{d−c}(i) = i, against minimality). Let γ ≔ (i σ(i) ⋯ σ^{r−1}(i)). **Peel it off:** ρ ≔ γ^{-1}σ fixes every point of the orbit O = {σ^c(i)} (σ and γ agree on O) and every point σ fixes (they lie outside O, where γ is the identity: σ(O) = O). So supp ρ ⊆ supp σ ∖ O, card supp ρ < m (`@fd2:prp-subset-finite`, `@fd2:prp-disjoint-union-count`, O ≠ ∅); by the induction hypothesis (the strengthened statement) ρ = γ_1 ⋯ γ_s with pairwise disjoint cycles, each with support inside supp ρ ⊆ supp σ ∖ O, hence disjoint from O = supp γ. Then σ = γρ = γγ_1 ⋯ γ_s, a product of pairwise disjoint cycles, and the statement's clause holds again: supp γ = O ⊆ supp σ and supp γ_j ⊆ supp ρ ⊆ supp σ.
  - Answer to the prompt: injectivity: if the first repeat were σ^b(i) = σ^a(i) with a ≥ 1, then σ^{b−1}(i) = σ^{a−1}(i) earlier; multiplying by the inverse moves the repeat back to i.

## 05-transpositions.md — Transpositions Generate the Symmetric Group

- **Hook.** (1 2 3) = (1 3)(1 2): a 3-cycle is two swaps. Every shuffle is a sequence of swaps of two cards — and the determinant of linear algebra will be built on exactly this fact.
