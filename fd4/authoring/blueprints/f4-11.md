**Purpose.**
- fd3 proved the rules of the absolute value in any ordered field (F3.9) and used one of them, the triangle inequality, in every limit proof. F4.8 built the Euclidean length on ℝⁿ and proved its triangle inequality. This chapter asks what a *distance* has to satisfy for those proofs to keep working, takes the answer as a definition, and collects the examples AN1 needs: three distances on ℝⁿ, the discrete metric, the sup distance between bounded functions, subspaces and products.
- It is the second turn of the series' spiral (ℝ → ℝⁿ → metric), and it stops before topology: no sequence converges and no set is called open here. AN2.1 (now AN1's third chapter) starts from these definitions.
- The reader leaves able to (i) check that a proposed distance is a metric, knowing the triangle inequality is the clause that bites; (ii) compare two distances with best constants, and turn a comparison into an inclusion of balls; (iii) compute a supremum distance and a diameter, proving both halves; (iv) build new metric spaces from old ones as subspaces and products, by transporting a triangle inequality.

## 1. Named moves, method boxes, scaffolding levels

Three named moves of this chapter (index "Named moves", each linked to its box), and one reused from F4.7:

1. **Check a metric axiom by axiom; the triangle inequality is the clause that bites.** Box `exm-method-metric-axioms` (§01). Template: (1) d is defined on every pair and is real; (2) (M1) in both directions, the "d(x, y) = 0 ⇒ x = y" half separately; (3) (M2); (4) (M3): fix x, y, z, write d(x, z), and look for a known inequality that splits it at y. When to use: any proposed distance. Usual failures: proving (M3) only for y "between" x and z; forgetting the "only if" half of (M1).
2. **Compare by the largest coordinate, then test the spike and the flat vector.** Box `exm-method-compare-distances` (§02). Template: write both distances through aᵢ ≔ |xᵢ − yᵢ|; bound each aᵢ by the largest, or by the sum; for a bound in the other direction look for a known inequality (Cauchy–Schwarz, the triangle inequality); then test sharpness on e₁ (the spike: one coordinate) and (1, …, 1) (the flat vector: all coordinates equal). Usual failure: calling a constant best without exhibiting the pair that attains it.
3. **Bound every value, then find a witness** (F4.7's move, reused, not re-introduced): F4.7's box `@exm-method-sup-two-halves` (sup ≤ M by bounding each value; sup ≥ M by a witness above each M − ε). This chapter cites it at **Idea** level in §04 and §06 and does not create a box of its own; it is not listed among this chapter's Named moves, but the index's Named-moves paragraph ends with one sentence pointing to it.
4. **Transport a triangle inequality.** Box `exm-method-transport-triangle` (§05). Template: write the new distance as a known metric or norm applied to old distances (or to images f(x), f(y)); apply the old triangle inequalities coordinate by coordinate; pass the resulting coordinatewise ≤ through the outer norm by its **monotonicity** on vectors with non-negative coordinates; finish with the outer norm's triangle inequality. Usual failure: skipping monotonicity (the old distances satisfy only ≤, so the outer norm must be monotone for the bound to survive). §01's `prp-metric-pullback` is its first, unannounced instance; §05 points back to it.

## 5. index.md

- **Title:** Metrics and Norms.
- **Story** (two paragraphs). Every limit proof in *Number Systems* used three facts about |x − y|: it is zero only when x = y, it does not care about order, and a detour through a third point never shortens the way. F4.8 proved the same three facts for the Euclidean length on ℝⁿ. Analysis will need distances between other things too: between functions (how far is x² from x on [0, 1]?), between points of a subset, between pairs of points. This chapter takes the three facts as the definition of a **metric**, finds that the triangle inequality is the only one that ever needs work, and builds the examples the analysis volumes start from. Motivating question: *what must a distance satisfy for the triangle-inequality arguments of a limit proof to go through?* Second paragraph: the spiral (ℝ, then ℝⁿ, then any metric space); the chapter stops before convergence and open sets, which are the subject of AN1's chapter on metric spaces; the metric on ℂ, |z − w|, is the Euclidean distance on ℝ² (F4.1's modulus) in prose.
- **What you need:** fd3's ℝ (complete ordered field, absolute value, square roots: *Number Systems*, Chapters 4–6); fd2's functions, products, finite maxima and countability; F4.6 (finite sums, square roots and their inequalities), F4.7 (bounded functions and their suprema), F4.8 (ℝⁿ and its Euclidean length). The borrowed-exponential sentence of §2. F4.9, F4.1 and F4.10 are not needed; two remarks point to them.
- **Time:** from the B1 formula once drafted; expect about **30 hours core, 40 full** (§6).
- **Roadmap:** six bullets, one sentence each (§01–§06 below).
- **Named moves:** the four of §1, each linked to its box.
- **Summary** (four to eight sentences) and **Terms** (alphabetical glossary of the chapter's definitions, linked; STYLE §12.13.5). Summary closes with what is open: when does a sequence of points get close to a point, and which sets have room around each of their points (AN1's metric chapter)?

### §01 `01-metrics.md` — What a Distance Must Do

*Method section: metric axioms (full).*

- **Opening frame.** Three distances already met: |x − y| on ℝ (fd3), the Euclidean length ‖x − y‖ on ℝⁿ (F4.8), and, informally, "how many characters differ" between two words. What do limit proofs use?
- **Hook: the naive attempt.** "A distance is any non-negative function of two points that is zero exactly on equal points and symmetric." Test it with d(x, y) ≔ (x − y)² on ℝ: non-negative, zero iff x = y, symmetric. Then d(0, 2) = 4 while d(0, 1) + d(1, 2) = 2: walking via 1 is "shorter" than walking straight. Every limit proof that splits |aₙ − a| at an intermediate point would break. The missing rule is the triangle inequality. (One sentence recalls that *Number Systems*, Section 4.1 met the square as a non-example of an absolute value for the same reason; prose, no label.)
- **`def-metric`** (major: full explain template; slogan *a metric is a distance that obeys the three rules the limit proofs used*). Let X be a set. A **metric** on X is a function d: X × X → ℝ (`@fd2:def-cartesian-product`, `@fd2:def-function`) such that for all x, y, z ∈ X: (M1) d(x, y) ≥ 0, and d(x, y) = 0 if and only if x = y; (M2) d(x, y) = d(y, x); (M3) d(x, z) ≤ d(x, y) + d(y, z). A **metric space** is a pair (X, d) with d a metric on X; "the metric space X" when d is understood. In symbols too (STYLE §10). The degenerate cases on the main line (first meeting, STYLE §12.3): X = ∅ (the empty function is a metric) and X a singleton (only d(x, x) = 0).
- **`exm-metric-real-line`.** d(x, y) ≔ |x − y| on ℝ: (M1) [ABS] (a) applied to x − y; (M2) [ABS] (b), |y − x| = |−(x − y)|; (M3) [ABS] (e), second form. "The usual metric on ℝ"; whenever ℝ appears as a metric space without a named metric, this is it.
- **`exm-method-metric-axioms`** [Method: checking that a function is a metric]: the box of §1 item 1, placed right after `exm-metric-real-line`, which it names as the trivial instance.
- **`exm-metric-discrete`** (first worked example under the box). For any set X, d_disc(x, y) ≔ 0 if x = y, 1 if x ≠ y. (M1), (M2) read off. (M3) by cases on whether x = z: if x = z, the left side is 0 ≤ right side (each term ≥ 0); if x ≠ z, then y ≠ x or y ≠ z (else x = y = z), so the right side is ≥ 1 = the left side. Aftermath: every set carries a metric; a metric need not look like a length.
- **`prp-metric-pullback`** (second worked example; *Scratch work* and the **Givens/Goal table** here). Let (Y, ρ) be a metric space, X a set and f: X → Y injective (`@fd2:def-injective`). Then d_f(x, x′) ≔ ρ(f(x), f(x′)) is a metric on X. If f is not injective, d_f fails (M1). *Scratch work:* (M2) and (M3) are ρ's, read at f(x), f(x′), f(x″); the only work is "d_f(x, x′) = 0 ⇒ x = x′". Givens/Goal table (prose rows): Givens d_f(x, x′) = 0 | Goal x = x′; then Givens ρ(f(x), f(x′)) = 0 | same Goal; then Givens f(x) = f(x′) (ρ's (M1)) | Goal x = x′, closed by injectivity. Proof: four lines. Non-injective case: f(x) = f(x′) with x ≠ x′ gives d_f(x, x′) = 0. *Self-explain:* which line uses injectivity?
- **`exm-metric-bounded-real-line`.** With f(x) ≔ x/(1 + |x|), injective by `@fd2:exm-fraction-map` (it is a bijection ℝ → (−1, 1)), `prp-metric-pullback` makes d(x, y) ≔ |x/(1 + |x|) − y/(1 + |y|)| a metric on ℝ. Values: d(0, 1) = 1/2, d(1, 3) = 1/4, d(−1, 1) = 1; every value is < 2, since both images lie in (−1, 1). Points equally spaced in the usual sense crowd together: d(n, n + 1) = 1/((n + 1)(n + 2)) for n ≥ 0. Aftermath: "near" and "far" depend on the metric; same set, different metric space.
- **`exm-not-metrics`** (Warning, three non-examples by minimal change, one clause each): (x − y)² fails (M3) (the hook); d((x₁, x₂), (y₁, y₂)) ≔ |x₁ − y₁| on ℝ² fails (M1) at (0, 0), (0, 1); d(x, y) ≔ x − y on ℝ fails (M1) (negative values) and (M2).
- **Quick checks** (three). (1) What are all metrics on a one-point set? on the empty set? (One each.) (2) Show that d(x, y) ≥ 0 follows from d(x, x) = 0, (M2) and (M3) (0 = d(x, x) ≤ d(x, y) + d(y, x) = 2d(x, y)); so (M1)'s first half is a consequence, kept in the definition for readability. (3) Is d(x, y) ≔ |x³ − y³| a metric on ℝ? Is |x² − y²|? (Yes: x ↦ x³ is injective, since x³ − y³ = (x − y)(x² + xy + y²) and x² + xy + y² = (x + y/2)² + 3y²/4 > 0 unless x = y = 0; `prp-metric-pullback`. No: |1² − (−1)²| = 0.)
- **Fold** (≤ 400 words) "Distances that may vanish": the second clause of (M1) is what the last non-example lacks; such functions (pseudometrics) appear in analysis when functions equal almost everywhere are identified; identifying points at distance 0 turns one into a metric. Instructor note, nothing cited.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** (a) State (M1)–(M3). (b) For each function on ℝ, name the first clause that fails, or say it is a metric: |x − y| + 1; |x² − y²|; max{x, y} − min{x, y}; (x − y)². *Answers:* (M1) (d(x, x) = 1); (M1) (d(1, −1) = 0); a metric: max{x, y} − min{x, y} = |x − y| (cases x ≤ y, y < x); (M3) (d(0, 2) = 4 > 2).
  - **A2.** On X = {a, b, c} put d(a, b) = d(b, c) = 1 and d(a, c) = t, with d symmetric and zero on the diagonal. For which real t is d a metric? *Answer:* 0 < t ≤ 2. (M1) needs t > 0. (M3) for the three ways to split: d(a, c) ≤ d(a, b) + d(b, c) gives t ≤ 2; d(a, b) ≤ d(a, c) + d(c, b) gives 1 ≤ t + 1, true for t ≥ 0; d(b, c) ≤ d(b, a) + d(a, c) likewise; splits through a repeated point are trivial. For t = 2 the triangle is "flat": b is a midpoint.
  - **B1.** Let d be a metric on X and c > 0. Show cd is a metric, and that min{d, 1} is a metric. *Solution:* cd: each clause scales ([OF]). min{d, 1}: (M1), (M2) clear; (M3): if d(x, y) ≥ 1 or d(y, z) ≥ 1, the right side is ≥ 1 ≥ min{d(x, z), 1}; otherwise the right side is d(x, y) + d(y, z) ≥ d(x, z) ≥ min{d(x, z), 1}.
  - **B2 [Prove or disprove].** d and ρ are metrics on X. Which are always metrics? (a) d + ρ; (b) max{d, ρ}; (c) min{d, ρ}; (d) d²; (e) √d; (f) d/(1 + d). *Verdicts:* (a) true: add the two triangle inequalities ([OF] (b)). (b) true: d(x, z) ≤ d(x, y) + d(y, z) ≤ max{d, ρ}(x, y) + max{d, ρ}(y, z), likewise ρ(x, z), so the max of the two left sides is ≤ the right side. (c) false: X = {a, b, c}, d(a, b) = 1, d(b, c) = 3, d(a, c) = 3; ρ(a, b) = 3, ρ(b, c) = 1, ρ(a, c) = 3 (both metrics: check the three splits each); min gives 3 between a and c but 1 + 1 = 2 via b. (d) false: d = |x − y| on ℝ, the hook. (e) true: √d(x, z) ≤ √(d(x, y) + d(y, z)) ≤ √d(x, y) + √d(y, z) by [ROOT-MONO]; (M1) by √a = 0 ⇔ a = 0. (f) true: g(t) ≔ t/(1 + t) = 1 − 1/(1 + t) is increasing on t ≥ 0 ([HALF] (c)), so with s = d(x, z), a = d(x, y), b = d(y, z), s ≤ a + b gives g(s) ≤ g(a + b) = a/(1 + a + b) + b/(1 + a + b) ≤ a/(1 + a) + b/(1 + b); (M1): g(t) = 0 ⇔ t = 0.
  - **B3 (uses the borrowed exponential).** d(x, y) ≔ |exp(x) − exp(y)| on ℝ. (a) Show d is a metric. (b) Show d(x, y) < 1 for all x, y < 0. (c) In the usual metric, −1 and −100 are 99 apart; how far apart are they in d, at most? *Solution:* (a) exp is strictly increasing [EXP], hence injective (x ≠ y gives exp(x) < exp(y) or the reverse); `prp-metric-pullback` with the usual metric. (b) For x < 0: 0 < exp(x) < exp(0) = 1 [EXP]; two numbers in (0, 1) are less than 1 apart ([ABS] (d)). (c) Less than 1, by (b). Ledger: [EXP], F4.7's box; the only use of the box in this chapter.
  - **C1.** Let d: X × X → ℝ satisfy (i) d(x, y) = 0 ⇔ x = y and (ii) d(x, z) ≤ d(x, y) + d(z, y) for all x, y, z (note the order in the last term). Prove d is a metric. *Hints:* rung 1 "choose two of x, y, z equal in (ii)"; rung 2 "with y ≔ x, (ii) compares d(x, z) with d(z, x)". *Solution:* (ii) with y = x: d(x, z) ≤ d(x, x) + d(z, x) = d(z, x); exchanging x and z gives the reverse, so (M2). Then (ii) with z = x: 0 = d(x, x) ≤ d(x, y) + d(x, y), so d ≥ 0, which with (i) is (M1). (M3): d(x, z) ≤ d(x, y) + d(z, y) = d(x, y) + d(y, z) by (M2). **Marking scheme** (10): 3 — symmetry from a correct specialization; 2 — non-negativity; 2 — (M1) assembled with (i); 3 — (M3) from (ii) and symmetry. Fatal: using (M2) before proving it; specializing so that the inequality becomes 0 ≤ 0.
  - **C2 [Find the error; the conclusion is true].** "Claim: d_disc satisfies (M3). Proof. If x, y, z are distinct, then d(x, z) = 1 ≤ 1 + 1. If x = y, then d(x, z) = d(y, z) ≤ d(x, y) + d(y, z). So (M3) holds." *Solution:* The conclusion is true (`exm-metric-discrete`). The error: the case split is not exhaustive. Of the five equality patterns of x, y, z (all distinct; x = y ≠ z; y = z ≠ x; x = z ≠ y; x = y = z) the proof treats "all distinct" and "x = y" only. Counterexample to the step "so (M3) holds": with x = z ≠ y, neither case applies, and the proof says nothing about d(x, x) ≤ d(x, y) + d(y, x) (true: 0 ≤ 2, but unproved). Repair: split on x = z, as `exm-metric-discrete` does. (The y = z case is the one a reader most often believes is covered by symmetry; it is, but the proof never says so.)

### §02 `02-three-distances.md` — Three Distances on ℝⁿ

*Method section: compare distances (full); metric axioms at Idea level.*

- **Opening frame.** ℝ had one obvious distance; ℝⁿ has several. A taxi in a grid of streets travels |x₁ − y₁| + |x₂ − y₂|; a king on a chessboard needs max{|x₁ − y₁|, |x₂ − y₂|} moves; a bird flies the Euclidean length of F4.8. Which is "the" distance? All three are metrics, and they are never far apart: that is the theorem.
- **`def-three-distances`** (major). For n ≥ 1 and x, y ∈ ℝⁿ [RN]: d₁(x, y) ≔ Σᵢ |xᵢ − yᵢ|; d₂(x, y) ≔ √(Σᵢ (xᵢ − yᵢ)²) = ‖x − y‖ (F4.8's Euclidean length [EUC]); d_∞(x, y) ≔ max{|x₁ − y₁|, …, |xₙ − yₙ|} (exists by [MAX]). For n = 1 all three are |x − y| ([ROOT-MONO]: √(a²) = |a|).
- **`exm-three-distances-computed`.** x = (3, −4), y = 0 in ℝ²: d₁ = 7, d₂ = 5, d_∞ = 4. x = (1, 2, −2), y = 0 in ℝ³: 5, 3, 2. The ordering d_∞ ≤ d₂ ≤ d₁ in both; the reader is asked (Quick check) whether it always holds.
- **`prp-three-distances-metrics`.** d₁, d₂, d_∞ are metrics on ℝⁿ. Idea level, citing `exm-method-metric-axioms`: (M1), (M2) coordinatewise from [ABS]; (M3) for d₂ from [EUC]'s triangle inequality applied to x − z = (x − y) + (y − z); for d₁ from the coordinatewise triangle inequality summed ([Σ-RULES]); for d_∞, fix i: |xᵢ − zᵢ| ≤ |xᵢ − yᵢ| + |yᵢ − zᵢ| ≤ d_∞(x, y) + d_∞(y, z); as i was arbitrary, the right side bounds every coordinate, so also the largest (the line-by-line version is B1's ordering item).
- **`thm-compare-three-distances`** (key proof; *Try it first*: "find constants making d_∞ ≤ d₂ ≤ d₁ ≤ C d_∞ true on ℝⁿ, and decide whether yours are the best; ten minutes"). For n ≥ 1 and x, y ∈ ℝⁿ:
  (a) d_∞(x, y) ≤ d₂(x, y) ≤ d₁(x, y) ≤ n d_∞(x, y);
  (b) d₁(x, y) ≤ √n d₂(x, y) and d₂(x, y) ≤ √n d_∞(x, y);
  (c) each of the five constants 1, 1, n, √n, √n is the best possible: for each inequality there are x ≠ y with equality (y = 0 and x = e₁ for the first two; y = 0 and x = (1, …, 1) for the other three).
  *Idea* (starts from the obstacles of the attempt: the reader usually finds d₁ ≤ n d_∞ and d_∞ ≤ d₁ at once and gets stuck on where d₂ sits): put aᵢ ≔ |xᵢ − yᵢ| and m ≔ max aᵢ = aⱼ. Each comparison is "bound every aᵢ by m, or by the sum", except d₁ ≤ √n d₂, which needs Cauchy–Schwarz applied to (a₁, …, aₙ) and (1, …, 1). *Scratch work* and the **Givens/Goal table** for d₁ ≤ √n d₂: Givens aᵢ ≥ 0 | Goal Σ aᵢ ≤ √n √(Σ aᵢ²); Givens Σ aᵢ = Σ 1·aᵢ, [CS-FIN] | Goal (Σ 1·aᵢ)² ≤ n Σ aᵢ²; the gap is closed by [CS-FIN] and [ROOT-MONO]. *Proof:* m² = aⱼ² ≤ Σ aᵢ², so m ≤ d₂ ([ROOT-MONO]); d₂ = ‖Σ (xᵢ − yᵢ)eᵢ‖ ≤ Σ |xᵢ − yᵢ| ‖eᵢ‖ = d₁ ([EUC]: triangle inequality n − 1 times and homogeneity, ‖eᵢ‖ = 1; this avoids expanding (Σ aᵢ)²); Σ aᵢ ≤ Σ m = nm ([Σ-RULES]); (Σ 1·aᵢ)² ≤ (Σ 1)(Σ aᵢ²) = n d₂² ([CS-FIN]), then roots; Σ aᵢ² ≤ n m², then roots. (c): e₁ gives d_∞ = d₂ = d₁ = 1; (1, …, 1) gives d_∞ = 1, d₂ = √n, d₁ = n. *Self-explain:* where is Cauchy–Schwarz used, and for which two vectors?
- **`exm-method-compare-distances`** [Method: comparing two distances], placed after the theorem's proof, which it names as its full instance: the box of §1 item 2.
- **Warning.** The constants grow with n. For two distances on the same space, "comparable" means constants that work for **all** pairs; §06 C3 shows a space where no constant works. Prose pointer: *Analysis in Several Variables* proves that any two norms on ℝⁿ are comparable (AN3.1).
- **Remark (prose).** ℂ with |z − w| is ℝ² with d₂: F4.1's modulus of z − w is the Euclidean length of the pair (no label, no proof step).
- **Quick checks.** (1) Check (a) and (b) for x = (3, −4), y = 0 (4 ≤ 5 ≤ 7 ≤ 8; 49 ≤ 50 so 7 ≤ 5√2; 25 ≤ 32 so 5 ≤ 4√2). (2) For which x, y is d₂ = d_∞? (Exactly when x and y differ in at most one coordinate: Σ aᵢ² = m² with aᵢ ≥ 0 forces all but one aᵢ to be 0.)
- **Fold** (≤ 300 words) "Other exponents": for 1 ≤ p < ∞ the formula (Σ |xᵢ − yᵢ|ᵖ)^{1/p} also gives a metric; the triangle inequality (Minkowski's) needs convexity that this volume does not prove; for 0 < p < 1 it fails (§03 `exm-half-power-not-norm`). Prose pointer to the analysis volumes; nothing cited.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1 (comprehension, key proof).** (a) Which inequality of (a)–(b) needs Cauchy–Schwarz? (b) Run the proof of d₂ ≤ d₁ on x = (1, 2, −2), y = 0: write x as Σ xᵢeᵢ and list the three norms added. (c) Which pairs show the constant √n in d₁ ≤ √n d₂ cannot be lowered, and why does e₁ not show it? (d) The idea of the theorem in two sentences. *Answers:* (a) d₁ ≤ √n d₂; (b) ‖x‖₂ ≤ |1|‖e₁‖ + |2|‖e₂‖ + |−2|‖e₃‖ = 5, and ‖x‖₂ = 3; (c) y = 0, x = (1, …, 1): n = √n · √n; e₁ gives 1 ≤ √n, strict for n ≥ 2; (d) a model answer.
  - **A2.** Compute d₁, d₂, d_∞ between (1, 1, 1, 1) and 0 in ℝ⁴ (4, 2, 1), and between (2, −1) and (−1, 3) in ℝ² (7, 5, 4); check the chain of (a) in both.
  - **B1 [Proof ordering].** Lines of the d_∞ triangle inequality, shuffled: (i) let x, y, z ∈ ℝⁿ; (ii) let i ∈ {1, …, n} be arbitrary; (iii) |xᵢ − zᵢ| ≤ |xᵢ − yᵢ| + |yᵢ − zᵢ|, by the triangle inequality in ℝ; (iv) |xᵢ − yᵢ| ≤ d_∞(x, y) and |yᵢ − zᵢ| ≤ d_∞(y, z), since a maximum is ≥ each element; (v) so |xᵢ − zᵢ| ≤ d_∞(x, y) + d_∞(y, z); (vi) as i was arbitrary, d_∞(x, y) + d_∞(y, z) is ≥ every element of {|x₁ − z₁|, …, |xₙ − zₙ|}; (vii) d_∞(x, z) is one of those elements, so d_∞(x, z) ≤ d_∞(x, y) + d_∞(y, z). Distractors: (D1) "choose i with |xᵢ − yᵢ| = d_∞(x, y)"; (D2) "then d_∞(x, z) = |xᵢ − zᵢ| for the i of (ii)". *Solution:* admissible orders: (i)(ii)(iii)(iv)(v)(vi)(vii), with (iv) allowed before (iii) (each uses only (ii)). (D1) fixes the index that is largest for the pair x, y; nothing makes it largest for x, z, so a bound at that index bounds one coordinate of x − z only. (D2) confuses an arbitrary index with the maximizing one.
  - **B2 [Prove or disprove].** (a) d₁ ≤ 2d₂ on ℝ⁴; (b) d₁ ≤ 2d₂ on ℝ⁵; (c) d_∞ ≥ d₁/n on ℝⁿ; (d) d₂² ≤ d₁ d_∞ on ℝⁿ; (e) d₁ d_∞ ≤ d₂² on ℝⁿ for n ≥ 2; (f) d₁ + d_∞ is a metric on ℝⁿ. *Verdicts:* (a) true, √4 = 2 in (b) of the theorem. (b) false: (1, …, 1) and 0 give 5 > 2√5, since 25 > 20. (c) true, (a) of the theorem rearranged. (d) true: Σ aᵢ² ≤ Σ aᵢ m = m Σ aᵢ ([Σ-RULES]). (e) false: n = 2, x = (2, 1), y = 0: 3 · 2 = 6 > 5. (f) true: (M3) by adding the two triangle inequalities; (M1) since d₁ + d_∞ = 0 forces d₁ = 0.
  - **B3.** Prove d₂ ≤ √n d_∞ on ℝⁿ and that √n cannot be replaced by any smaller constant. *Solution:* the theorem's last line in full; for c < √n, the flat vector gives √n > c · 1.
  - **B4 (Hamming).** On {0, 1}ⁿ ⊆ ℝⁿ let h(x, y) be the number of i with xᵢ ≠ yᵢ. (a) Show h(x, y) = d₁(x, y) = d₂(x, y)² for x, y ∈ {0, 1}ⁿ, and conclude that h is a metric on {0, 1}ⁿ. (b) Show d₂² is not a metric on ℝ². (c) Compute h((0, 0, 0), (1, 1, 0)) and find all y with h((0, 0, 0), y) = 1. *Solution:* (a) |xᵢ − yᵢ| ∈ {0, 1}, equal to 1 exactly when xᵢ ≠ yᵢ, and equal to its square; so all three count the differing coordinates. h is d₁ on the smaller set, and the clauses of a metric, true for all points of ℝⁿ, are true for those of {0, 1}ⁿ (stated directly; §05 names this the subspace metric). (b) (0, 0), (1, 0), (2, 0): 4 > 1 + 1. (c) 2; e₁, e₂, e₃.
  - **C1 (weighted distances).** For w ∈ ℝⁿ with every wᵢ > 0 put d_w(x, y) ≔ Σ wᵢ |xᵢ − yᵢ|. (a) d_w is a metric. (b) The best constants c, C with c d₁ ≤ d_w ≤ C d₁ are c = min wᵢ, C = max wᵢ. (c) If some wᵢ = 0, d_w is not a metric. *Solution:* (a) as d₁, each term scaled by wᵢ > 0. (b) min wᵢ · aᵢ ≤ wᵢaᵢ ≤ max wᵢ · aᵢ, summed; equality at x = e_j, y = 0 with w_j the minimum (resp. the maximum). (c) x = eᵢ, y = 0 give d_w = 0. **Marking scheme** (10): 3 — metric; 3 — both inequalities; 3 — sharpness with explicit pairs; 1 — (c). Fatal: claiming best constants without pairs.

Fatal: Cauchy–Schwarz applied to x and y instead of (aᵢ) and (1, …, 1); squaring an inequality between possibly negative quantities.

### §03 `03-norms.md` — Norms: Length Measured from Zero

*Metric axioms at Idea level.*

- **Opening frame.** All three distances of §02 have the form d(x, y) = N(x − y): a length of the difference. Two features follow that the discrete metric and the bounded metric of §01 lack: shifting both points does not change the distance, and doubling both doubles it. Which properties of N make N(x − y) a metric?
- **Hook (weird example as a Quick check before the definition).** On ℝ², which of these "lengths" make N(x − y) a metric: |x₁| (no: (M1) fails at (0, 0), (0, 1)); 2|x₁| + |x₂| (yes); x₁² + x₂² (no: (M3) fails, it is d₂²)? The reader is asked to guess the pattern before reading on.
- **`def-linear-function-set`** (minor, introduced just before use). Let S be a set; ℝ^S is the set of all functions S → ℝ. A **linear set of functions on S** is a subset V ⊆ ℝ^S that contains the zero function and is closed under pointwise sums and real multiples: f, g ∈ V and c ∈ ℝ give f + g ∈ V and cf ∈ V. Examples: ℝ^S itself; the functions on [0, 1] vanishing at 0. Non-examples: the non-negative functions (not closed under multiplication by −1); the functions with f(s₀) = 1 (no zero function). **Remark** (the one place [TUPLE] is used): read a point x of ℝⁿ as the function i ↦ xᵢ on {1, …, n}; for n ≥ 3 this is literally what an n-tuple is (`@fd3:prp-tuples-as-sets`), for n = 1, 2 a relabelling; F4.8's coordinatewise operations are the pointwise ones, so ℝⁿ is the linear set ℝ^{1, …, n}. Prose pointer: LA1.1 calls such sets vector spaces and studies them abstractly; nothing here needs that.
- **`def-norm-linear-set`** (major; slogan *a norm is a length*). Let V be a linear set of functions on S. A **norm** on V is a function V → ℝ, written f ↦ ‖f‖, such that for all f, g ∈ V and c ∈ ℝ: (N1) ‖f‖ ≥ 0, and ‖f‖ = 0 iff f = 0; (N2) ‖cf‖ = |c| ‖f‖; (N3) ‖f + g‖ ≤ ‖f‖ + ‖g‖. The pair (V, ‖·‖) is a **normed linear set**. Degenerate case: V = {0}, whose only norm is 0.
- **`prp-norm-metric`.** If ‖·‖ is a norm on V, then d(f, g) ≔ ‖f − g‖ is a metric on V, and for all f, g, h ∈ V and c ∈ ℝ: d(f + h, g + h) = d(f, g) (translation invariance) and d(cf, cg) = |c| d(f, g) (homogeneity). Idea citing `exm-method-metric-axioms`: (M1) is (N1); (M2) is (N2) with c = −1; (M3) is (N3) applied to f − h = (f − g) + (g − h). *Self-explain:* which norm axiom gives symmetry?
- **`prp-norm-reverse-triangle`.** | ‖f‖ − ‖g‖ | ≤ ‖f − g‖. Proof as fd3's (f) for the absolute value, which it recalls by name (`@fd3:prp-absolute-value-ordered-field` (f)): (N3) on f = (f − g) + g and on g = (g − f) + f, then (N2) with c = −1 and [ABS] (d).
- **`exm-three-norms`.** ‖x‖₁ ≔ Σ |xᵢ|, ‖x‖₂ ≔ √(Σ xᵢ²) (F4.8's ‖x‖), ‖x‖_∞ ≔ max |xᵢ| are norms on ℝⁿ, and their metrics are d₁, d₂, d_∞. (N1), (N3) are `prp-three-distances-metrics` read at y = 0; (N2): Σ |cxᵢ| = |c| Σ |xᵢ| ([ABS] (b), [Σ-RULES]); √(Σ c²xᵢ²) = |c| √(Σ xᵢ²) ([ROOT-MONO]); max |cxᵢ| = |c| max |xᵢ| (the index attaining one attains the other, since t ↦ |c| t is increasing for c ≠ 0; c = 0 directly). Subscript convention stated here.
- **`prp-metric-not-from-norm`.** Let V be a linear set of functions with V ≠ {0} and ‖·‖ a norm on V. Then the metric of ‖·‖ takes every value r ≥ 0: for f ≠ 0, d((r/‖f‖) f, 0) = r. Consequently neither d_disc on ℝⁿ (values 0, 1) nor the bounded metric of §01 on ℝ (values < 2) is the metric of any norm. (The reader might try translation invariance first; §03 B3 does that for the bounded metric.)
- **`exm-half-power-not-norm`** (an almost-norm, to repair). N(x) ≔ (√|x₁| + √|x₂|)² on ℝ². (N1) holds; (N2): N(cx) = (√|c|)² (√|x₁| + √|x₂|)² = |c| N(x) ([ROOT-MONO]); (N3) fails: N(1, 0) = N(0, 1) = 1, N(1, 1) = 4 > 2. The set {N ≤ 1} is pinched inward between the axes; §03 C1 shows that this pinching is exactly what (N3) forbids. Prose pointer: convex sets are F4.9's; not cited.
- **Warning.** Not every metric on ℝⁿ comes from a norm (`prp-metric-not-from-norm`), and a function satisfying (N1) and (N2) need not satisfy (N3) (`exm-half-power-not-norm`).
- **Quick checks.** (1) Is N(x) ≔ max{|x₁|, |x₁ + x₂|} a norm on ℝ²? (Yes: N = 0 forces x₁ = 0 and x₂ = 0; (N2), (N3) coordinatewise as for ‖·‖_∞.) (2) Instance of the reverse triangle inequality: x = (3, 4), y = (1, 0) with ‖·‖₂: |5 − 1| = 4 ≤ ‖(2, 4)‖₂ = √20, since 16 ≤ 20.
- **Fold** (≤ 400 words) "Why only sets of functions": every linear set of functions is a vector space; conversely LA1.1 shows any vector space can be handled this way after choosing coordinates or a basis, but that needs LA; the fd4 version avoids it. Instructor note.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** Which are norms on ℝ²? (a) |x₁| + 2|x₂|; (b) |x₁ − x₂|; (c) x₁² + x₂²; (d) 3‖x‖_∞. *Answers:* (a) yes; (b) no, (N1) at (1, 1); (c) no, (N2): N(2x) = 4N(x); (d) yes.

  - **C1 (a norm is a function satisfying (N1), (N2) whose unit ball is convex).** Let V be a linear set of functions and N: V → ℝ satisfy (N1) and (N2). Call K ≔ {f : N(f) ≤ 1} convex if (1 − t)f + tg ∈ K whenever f, g ∈ K and 0 ≤ t ≤ 1. Prove: N satisfies (N3) if and only if K is convex. (⇐) If f = 0 or g = 0, (N3) is an equality. Otherwise put t ≔ N(g)/(N(f) + N(g)) ∈ (0, 1); then (1 − t) f/N(f) + t g/N(g) = (f + g)/(N(f) + N(g)) ∈ K, so by (N2) N(f + g) ≤ N(f) + N(g). ### §04 `04-balls-and-diameter.md` — Balls, Bounded Sets, and Diameter

*Method section: bound a supremum (full); compare distances at Idea level.*

- **Opening frame.** In ℝ, the points within ε of a are an interval; every limit proof lives in one. The same set, "all points at distance less than r from x", makes sense in any metric space, and its shape tells which metric is in use.
- **`def-ball`** (major). Let (X, d) be a metric space, x ∈ X and r > 0. The **open ball** B(x, r) ≔ {y ∈ X : d(x, y) < r}, the **closed ball** B̄(x, r) ≔ {y ∈ X : d(x, y) ≤ r}; B_d(x, r) when the metric is named. x ∈ B(x, r) ⊆ B̄(x, r). In ℝ with the usual metric, B(a, ε) = (a − ε, a + ε) ([ABS] (d), as fd3 used it).
- **Warning (names only).** "Open" and "closed" here are parts of two names. Open and closed **sets** are defined in AN1's chapter on metric spaces, which proves that open balls are open sets; this chapter proves nothing about openness and uses no such word as a property.
- **`exm-balls-plane`** (figure: three panels, the unit balls about 0 in ℝ² for d₁, d₂, d_∞: a diamond, a disc, a square, drawn to the same scale and nested as `cor-compare-balls-rn` says). Membership checks by inequalities only: (0.6, 0.6) has d₁ = 1.2 (not in B₁), d₂ = √0.72 < 1 (in B₂, since 0.72 < 1), d_∞ = 0.6 (in B_∞). The pictures illustrate; no proof uses them.
- **`exm-balls-discrete`.** In (X, d_disc): B(x, r) = {x} for r ≤ 1 and = X for r > 1; B̄(x, 1) = X while B(x, 1) = {x}. And in the bounded metric of §01, B(0, 1) = ℝ, since d(0, y) = |y|/(1 + |y|) < 1 for every y. Balls need not look round, and a "small" radius need not give a small set.
- **`prp-compare-balls`.** Let d, ρ be metrics on X and c, C > 0. If c d ≤ ρ on X × X, then B_ρ(x, cr) ⊆ B_d(x, r); if ρ ≤ C d, then B_d(x, r/C) ⊆ B_ρ(x, r); for all x ∈ X, r > 0. (Two lines each.) Idea: a comparison of distances is a nesting of balls with the same centre.
- **`cor-compare-balls-rn`.** In ℝⁿ, for all x and r > 0: B_∞(x, r/n) ⊆ B₁(x, r) ⊆ B₂(x, r) ⊆ B_∞(x, r), and B₂(x, r/√n) ⊆ B₁(x, r), B_∞(x, r/√n) ⊆ B₂(x, r). From `thm-compare-three-distances` and `prp-compare-balls`. Aftermath (prose): this is why AN1 can say "the same open sets for all three metrics" once open sets exist.
- **`exm-method-bound-supremum`** [Method: computing a supremum], placed before the diameter: the box of §1 item 3 (or the citation of F4.7's box, open question 2). The box recalls fd3's `@fd3:exm-cof-sup-one-minus-reciprocals` (sup{1 − 1/n : n ≥ 1} = 1) by name as an instance of the (≥) half with M′ < M, without redoing it; its own worked instances are the diameter of the square below (supremum attained) and `prp-diameter-ball` (b) (supremum approached).
- **`def-bounded-set-diameter`** (major). A ⊆ X is **bounded** if A = ∅ or there is M ∈ ℝ with d(a, b) ≤ M for all a, b ∈ A. For A non-empty and bounded, the **diameter** diam A ≔ sup{d(a, b) : a, b ∈ A}, which exists by [COF] (the set is non-empty, since a ∈ A gives d(a, a), and bounded above by M). diam ∅ is not defined (NOTATION). diam A = 0 iff A is a singleton.
- **`exm-diameter-square`** (first worked example of the method). A ≔ {x ∈ ℝ² : 0 ≤ x₁, x₂ ≤ 1}: diam A is 2 for d₁, √2 for d₂, 1 for d_∞. (≤) for a, b ∈ A each |aᵢ − bᵢ| ≤ 1 ([ABS] (d)), so d₁ ≤ 2, d₂ ≤ √2, d_∞ ≤ 1; (≥) the pair (0, 0), (1, 1) attains each bound, so each supremum is a maximum.
- **`prp-bounded-in-ball`.** Let A ⊆ X be non-empty. The following are equivalent: (i) A is bounded; (ii) A ⊆ B(x, r) for some x ∈ X and r > 0; (iii) for every x ∈ X there is r > 0 with A ⊆ B(x, r). Proof: (i) ⇒ (iii): fix a₀ ∈ A; d(x, a) ≤ d(x, a₀) + d(a₀, a) ≤ d(x, a₀) + M < d(x, a₀) + M + 1 ≕ r. (iii) ⇒ (ii): X ≠ ∅ since A ⊆ X. (ii) ⇒ (i): d(a, b) ≤ d(a, x) + d(x, b) < 2r. Moreover diam A ≤ 2r in (ii).
- **`cor-bounded-three-distances`.** A ⊆ ℝⁿ is bounded for d₁ iff for d₂ iff for d_∞ (`thm-compare-three-distances`). Contrast (prose and Quick check): every subset of ℝ is bounded in the bounded metric of §01 and in d_disc; "bounded" belongs to the metric, not to the set.
- **`prp-diameter-ball`** (*Try it first*: "the ball B(x, r) obviously has diameter 2r: prove it in ℝ² with d₂, and then find a metric space where it fails; ten minutes"). (a) In any metric space, diam B(x, r) ≤ 2r and diam B̄(x, r) ≤ 2r. (b) If V is a linear set of functions with V ≠ {0} and ‖·‖ a norm on V, then diam B(f, r) = diam B̄(f, r) = 2r for every f ∈ V, r > 0. (c) In (X, d_disc) with X having at least two points, diam B(x, 1) = 0 < 2. *Idea* (starts from the obstacle: the attempt finds the two "endpoints" f ± r u at distance exactly 2r, which lie in the closed ball but not in the open one; so for the open ball the supremum is approached, not attained; that is the method's (≥) half with M′ < M). Proof of (b): (≤) is (a). (≥): choose g ≠ 0 in V and put u ≔ g/‖g‖, so ‖u‖ = 1 (N2). For 0 < t < r, f + tu and f − tu lie in B(f, r) and are 2t apart. Suppose diam B(f, r) = D < 2r; put t ≔ (D/2 + r)/2, so D/2 < t < r ([HALF] (b)); then 2t > D, a contradiction. For the closed ball t = r is allowed and the bound is attained. (c) the ball is {x}.
- **False-conclusion item (Warning).** "Every ball of radius r has diameter 2r" is false: (c). "B(x, r) ⊆ B(y, s) implies r ≤ s" is false in d_disc (B(x, 1/2) = {x} ⊆ B(x, 1/4)); true for a norm (B2 (e)).
- **Quick checks.** (1) Is (0.9, −0.9) in B_∞(0, 1)? In B₁(0, 1)? (Yes; no, d₁ = 1.8.) (2) diam{0, 1, 3} ⊆ ℝ (3, a maximum over three pairs). (3) Is (1/2, 1/2) in B₁(0, 1)? In B̄₁(0, 1)? (No, d₁ = 1; yes.)
- **Fold** (≤ 400 words) "Why diam ∅ is left undefined": sup ∅ has no value in ℝ (NOTATION's sup row; extended values only from MT on); a convention diam ∅ = 0 would be harmless for monotonicity and is used in later volumes; this volume does not need it.
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1.** (a) Describe B(0, 1) and B̄(0, 1) in ℝ with the usual metric and in ℝ with d_disc. (b) Compute diam of {(0, 0), (1, 0), (0, 2)} for d₁, d₂, d_∞. *Answers:* (a) (−1, 1) and [−1, 1]; {0} and ℝ. (b) The three distances are (1, 1, 1), (2, 2, 2), (3, √5, 2) for d₁, d₂, d_∞; diam = 3, √5, 2.
  - **A2 (comprehension of the method).** In `exm-diameter-square`, which half of the method is the inequality |aᵢ − bᵢ| ≤ 1, and which the pair (0, 0), (1, 1)? Why is no "M′ < M" argument needed there, while `prp-diameter-ball` (b) needs one? *Answer:* upper bound; attaining element; in the square the supremum is attained, in the open ball it is not.
  - **B1.** In the bounded metric d of §01, show diam ℝ = 2 and diam{0, 1, 2, …} = 1. *Solution:* d(x, y) < 2 always, since both images lie in (−1, 1). d(−n, n) = 2n/(n + 1) = 2 − 2/(n + 1); for M′ < 2, [ARCH] gives n with 2/(n + 1) < 2 − M′, so 2 is the supremum. For ℕ: images n/(n + 1) ∈ [0, 1), so all distances are < 1; d(0, n) = 1 − 1/(n + 1), and the same argument gives supremum 1.
  - **B2 [Prove or disprove].** For non-empty bounded A, B in a metric space: (a) A ⊆ B ⇒ diam A ≤ diam B; (b) diam(A ∪ B) ≤ diam A + diam B; (c) if A ∩ B ≠ ∅, then diam(A ∪ B) ≤ diam A + diam B; (d) B(x, r) = B(y, r) ⇒ x = y; (e) in a normed linear set V ≠ {0}, B(f, r) ⊆ B(g, s) ⇒ r ≤ s. *Verdicts:* (a) true: diam B is an upper bound of the smaller set of distances. (b) false: A = {0}, B = {10} in ℝ: 10 > 0 + 0. (c) true: with c ∈ A ∩ B, for a ∈ A, b ∈ B, d(a, b) ≤ d(a, c) + d(c, b) ≤ diam A + diam B; pairs inside A or inside B are bounded by one diameter. Then show that the "only if" direction fails for d_disc. *Hints:* rung 1 "for ⇒, assume ‖f − g‖ < r + s and look for a common point on the way from f to g"; rung 2 "try h ≔ f + t(g − f) with t ≔ r/(r + s)". *Solution:* (⇐) a common h gives ‖f − g‖ ≤ ‖f − h‖ + ‖h − g‖ < r + s. ### §05 `05-subspaces-and-products.md` — Subspaces and Products

*Method section: transport a triangle inequality (full; the faded triple starts here).*

- **Opening frame.** Two ways to make new metric spaces from old: keep only some of the points, or pair points of two spaces. The first is free; the second needs a triangle inequality to be carried from one space to another, which is the chapter's main move.
- **`def-subspace-metric`.** Let (X, d) be a metric space and A ⊆ X. The restriction d_A ≔ d restricted to A × A is a metric on A (each clause is a statement about all points of X, hence about the points of A); (A, d_A) is a **subspace** of X, and A carries the **inherited** metric. §02 B4's Hamming metric is named here as an instance.
- **`prp-subspace-balls`.** For a ∈ A and r > 0: B_A(a, r) = B_X(a, r) ∩ A, and the same for closed balls. Balls of A are centred at points of A only.
- **`exm-subspaces`** (four short cases). (1) ℤ ⊆ ℝ: B_ℤ(0, 1) = {0}, B_ℤ(0, 3/2) = {−1, 0, 1}; ℤ's small balls look like d_disc's, its large ones do not (d(0, 5) = 5). (2) [0, 1] ⊆ ℝ: B_{[0,1]}(0, 1/2) = [0, 1/2), a ball that is not an interval symmetric about its centre. (3) The unit circle S¹ ≔ {p ∈ ℝ² : ‖p‖₂ = 1} with d₂: distances are chord lengths; diam S¹ = 2, attained at p and −p. (4) ℚ ⊆ ℝ: B_ℚ(0, 1) = (−1, 1) ∩ ℚ.
- **Warning.** A set's balls depend on the space it is seen in: [0, 1/2) is a ball of [0, 1] but not of ℝ (it has no centre c with [0, 1/2) = (c − r, c + r)). AN1's metric chapter makes this precise for open sets.
- **`def-product-metrics`.** For metric spaces (X, d_X), (Y, d_Y) and p = (x, y), p′ = (x′, y′) in X × Y: d₁(p, p′) ≔ d_X(x, x′) + d_Y(y, y′); d₂(p, p′) ≔ √(d_X(x, x′)² + d_Y(y, y′)²); d_∞(p, p′) ≔ max{d_X(x, x′), d_Y(y, y′)}. In words: the three norms of §03 applied to the pair of distances (d_X(x, x′), d_Y(y, y′)) ∈ ℝ².
- **`lem-norm-monotone-coordinates`.** For u, s ∈ ℝⁿ with 0 ≤ uᵢ ≤ sᵢ for every i: ‖u‖₁ ≤ ‖s‖₁, ‖u‖₂ ≤ ‖s‖₂, ‖u‖_∞ ≤ ‖s‖_∞. (Sum of termwise inequalities [Σ-RULES]; squares [OF] (g) then sum then roots [ROOT-MONO]; the maximum of s bounds every uᵢ.) Remark: the hypothesis uᵢ ≥ 0 is needed for ‖·‖₂: u = (−2), s = (1) in ℝ¹ has u ≤ s and ‖u‖₂ = 2 > 1.

### §06 `06-the-sup-metric.md` — Functions as Points: the Sup Metric

*Bound a supremum and metric axioms at Idea level; the R group.*

- **Opening frame / hook.** How far is the function x² from the function x on [0, 1]? Measure the largest vertical gap: x − x² = 1/4 − (x − 1/2)² ≤ 1/4, with equality at 1/2 (no calculus). So the distance should be 1/4. Two functions are close when their graphs are close everywhere at once.
- **`def-bounded-function-space`** (major). Let S be a non-empty set. ℓ^∞(S) is the set of bounded functions f: S → ℝ ([BDD-F]: some M has |f(s)| ≤ M for all s). For f ∈ ℓ^∞(S), ‖f‖_∞ ≔ sup{|f(s)| : s ∈ S} (exists by [COF]: non-empty since S is, bounded above by M). ℓ^∞ ≔ ℓ^∞(ℕ), the bounded sequences (x_k)_{k ∈ ℕ}. Why S ≠ ∅: sup ∅ has no value (NOTATION).
- **`thm-sup-norm`** (key proof). ℓ^∞(S) is a linear set of functions on S, and ‖·‖_∞ is a norm on it; its metric d_∞(f, g) = sup_s |f(s) − g(s)| is the **sup metric**. *Idea* citing `exm-method-bound-supremum` and `exm-method-metric-axioms`: every clause is "a bound at each point s gives a bound on the supremum" (the method's ≤ half, since a supremum is the least upper bound), plus, for (N2), the same in reverse. *Proof:* linear set: |f(s) + g(s)| ≤ M + M′ and |cf(s)| ≤ |c|M ([ABS]); zero function bounded. (N1): ‖f‖_∞ ≥ |f(s₀)| ≥ 0 for some s₀ ∈ S; ‖f‖_∞ = 0 gives |f(s)| ≤ 0, so f(s) = 0, for all s. (N2): c = 0 directly; for c ≠ 0, |cf(s)| = |c||f(s)| ≤ |c|‖f‖_∞ for all s gives ‖cf‖_∞ ≤ |c|‖f‖_∞, and the same applied to cf and 1/c gives the reverse. (N3): |f(s) + g(s)| ≤ |f(s)| + |g(s)| ≤ ‖f‖_∞ + ‖g‖_∞ for every s, so the right side is an upper bound and ‖f + g‖_∞ ≤ it. *Self-explain:* why does a bound at every point give a bound on the supremum?
- **`prp-sup-norm-finite-set`.** For S = {1, …, n}, every function is bounded ([MAX]), ℓ^∞(S) = ℝ^S is ℝⁿ (§03 remark), and ‖·‖_∞ is §03's max norm: a finite set's supremum is its maximum. So §02's d_∞ is a sup metric.
- **`exm-sup-distances`** (worked, the method in both halves). On S = [0, 1]: d_∞(x, x²) = 1/4 (hook; attained); d_∞(0, xᵏ) = 1 for k ≥ 1 (|sᵏ| ≤ 1, attained at 1). On S = [0, 1): d_∞(0, x) = 1, **not attained**: s < 1 for every s, and for M′ < 1 the point s ≔ max{0, (M′ + 1)/2} ∈ [0, 1) has s > M′ ([HALF] (b)). In ℓ^∞: x_k ≔ (−1)ᵏ/(k + 1) has ‖x‖_∞ = 1, attained at k = 0.
- **`exm-sup-ball-band`.** g ∈ B̄(f, r) iff f(s) − r ≤ g(s) ≤ f(s) + r for every s: the closed ball is the band of half-width r around the graph of f (figure: a graph and its band). **Warning (the open-ball trap):** g ∈ B(f, r) is **not** the same as |g(s) − f(s)| < r for every s. On S = [0, 1), f = 0, g(s) = s: |g(s)| < 1 at every point, yet ‖g‖_∞ = 1, so g ∉ B(0, 1). The correct reading: g ∈ B(f, r) iff some r′ < r has |g(s) − f(s)| ≤ r′ for every s (B1). Prose: this difference between "at every point" and "uniformly" is the subject of AN1's sequences of functions.
- **`exm-zero-one-sequences`.** Let E ⊆ ℓ^∞ be the set of sequences with every term 0 or 1. Distinct x, y ∈ E have d_∞(x, y) = 1: each |x_k − y_k| ∈ {0, 1}, and some k gives 1. So the subspace metric on E is d_disc, and E is uncountable (`@fd2:thm-binary-sequences-uncountable`; E is {0, 1}^ℕ). Aftermath (prose): ℓ^∞ contains uncountably many points pairwise 1 apart; R3 shows ℝⁿ does not.
