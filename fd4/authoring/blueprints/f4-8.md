**Purpose.**
- fd3 ended with ℝ, a complete ordered field (`@fd3:thm-reals-complete-ordered-field`); F4.6 supplied finite sums and inequalities. This chapter builds **ℝⁿ as the set of n-tuples of reals** and does in it everything that AN1, AN2 and CA use about Euclidean space without stopping: vector operations, the dot product, the norm, distance, Cauchy–Schwarz with its equality case, the triangle inequality, coordinate bounds, projection onto a line, and the cosine of the angle between two vectors.
- **No abstract vector spaces** (spec/08: "不引入抽象向量空間，那屬於 LA1.1"). The eight laws of `prp-vector-space-laws-rn` are proved for ℝⁿ and named in a prose pointer as what LA1.1 will take as axioms. No matrices, no linear maps, no dimension, no bases beyond the standard one.
- **No topology** (open sets, convergence): AN1.4 and AN2.1. Sets like "the points at distance less than r from a" appear in examples only, so AN1.4 owns the word "open ball" and F4.11 the metric version.
- **No angle θ itself.** The chapter defines the number cos∠(x, y) ≔ ⟨x, y⟩/(‖x‖‖y‖) and proves it lies in [−1, 1]; the angle θ ∈ [0, π] with cos θ equal to it needs cos, which fd4 has only through F4.1's borrowed facts (T1)–(T5), and F4.10 comes after F4.1. See "Borrowed results" below.
- The reader leaves able to (i) prove an identity in ℝⁿ by reducing it to n identities in ℝ; (ii) turn a statement about lengths into inner-product algebra by squaring and expanding; (iii) prove a homogeneous inequality by first scaling to unit vectors; (iv) split a vector into a part along a line and a part orthogonal to it, and use Pythagoras on the split.

**Signature moves** (`index.md`, "Named moves"; each links to its method box, STYLE §2 item 6).
- **Work coordinatewise** (`exm-method-coordinatewise`, §02). An equation between vectors is n equations between reals. Usual failure: a condition that is not coordinatewise ("x ≠ 0" is "some x_k ≠ 0", not "every x_k ≠ 0").
- **Expand the square** (`exm-method-expand-the-square`, §04). Replace ‖v‖ by ‖v‖² = ⟨v, v⟩, expand by bilinearity, compare, and return by `@fd3:prp-ordered-ring-rules` (g) (both sides nonnegative). **Main move: the faded triple** (Scaffolding).
- **Normalize** (`exm-method-normalize`, §05). If both sides of a statement scale the same way when x is replaced by ax with a > 0, prove it for unit vectors and scale back; treat 0 separately.
- **Subtract the projection** (`exm-method-subtract-the-projection`, §06). Write x = p + (x − p) with p a multiple of y chosen so that x − p ⊥ y; then Pythagoras.

**Scaffolding levels** (STYLE §12.14): coordinatewise **full** in §02, **Idea** in §03, **terse** from §04; expand the square **full** in §04, **Idea** in §05, **terse** in §06; normalize **full** in §05, **Idea** in §06; subtract the projection **full** in §06.

## index.md

- **Story.** Analysis in several variables needs one fact on almost every page: the distance between two points is at most the length of any path of straight segments between them. In ℝ that is the triangle inequality for |·|, proved by cases in *Number Systems*. In the plane and in space, cases do not work: there are infinitely many directions. This chapter builds ℝⁿ from n-tuples, measures length with the dot product, and proves the inequality with no picture at all, through Cauchy–Schwarz. ## 01-points-and-coordinates.md — Points and Coordinates

*Budget: 2,500–3,200 main-line words. Not a method section.*

- **Hook.** In school the plane is a picture first and pairs of numbers second. Analysis reverses the order: a point **is** its list of coordinates, and every geometric statement must be a statement about reals. One line of history, carrying mathematics: the coordinate plane replaced figures by equations, and with it the plane became ℝ × ℝ.
- **Standing setup** (one paragraph): ℝ is the complete ordered field of *Number Systems* (`@fd3:thm-reals-complete-ordered-field`); all its rules are used without restating them.
- **`def-real-n-space`** (major: full template; slogan *a point is its list of coordinates*): for n ∈ ℕ, n ≥ 1, ℝⁿ is the set of n-tuples x = (x_1, …, x_n) of reals (F2.1 §04, "Longer lists"; for n = 2 it is ℝ × ℝ, `@fd2:def-cartesian-product`). x_k is the k-th **coordinate**. Equality: x = y iff x_k = y_k for every k. Convention: ℝ¹ is identified with ℝ, (x) with x (say it is a convention, as F2.1 does for ((1, 2), 3)). The words "point" and "vector" are synonyms for elements of ℝⁿ; §02 says when each is used.
- **Remark** (the model of the line, Lang 8's idea in our words): choosing an origin and a unit on a line matches its points with reals; choosing two perpendicular lines through an origin matches points of the plane with pairs. This is the motivation only; the book never uses the picture as a reason.
- **`exm-subsets-of-the-plane`**: subsets of ℝ² described by conditions on coordinates: the first axis {x : x_2 = 0}; the closed first quadrant {x : x_1 ≥ 0, x_2 ≥ 0}; the diagonal {x : x_1 = x_2}; the graph of f: ℝ → ℝ as {(t, f(t)) : t ∈ ℝ} (prose pointer to F4.7's graphs); the set {x : x_1² + x_2² ≤ 1} (named "the closed unit disc" only in prose; §04 rewrites it as ‖x‖ ≤ 1). For each, one point in it and one not.
- **`def-box-rn`**: for reals a_k ≤ b_k (k = 1, …, n), the **closed box** [a_1, b_1] × ⋯ × [a_n, b_n] ≔ {x ∈ ℝⁿ : a_k ≤ x_k ≤ b_k for every k}; open box with strict inequalities, a_k < b_k. For n = 1 a closed interval, for n = 2 a rectangle. (The hypothesis a_k ≤ b_k makes the closed box non-empty: it contains (a_1, …, a_n). Open boxes need a_k < b_k for the same reason.)
- **Warning.** ℝ² is not "two copies of ℝ": the pair (1, 2) is not the set {1, 2}, and (1, 2) ≠ (2, 1). And ℝ² × ℝ is not literally ℝ³ (its elements are pairs whose first entry is a pair); C1 builds the bijection that lets us identify them.
- **Quick check.** Which of (0, 0, 0), (1, 2), ((1, 2), 3) are elements of ℝ³? (Only the first. The second is in ℝ², the third is a pair, by F2.1's convention.) How many points has {0, 1}³? (Eight.)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1:** (a) for which reals s, t is (s + t, s − t) = (4, 2)? (s = 3, t = 1: two equations in ℝ.) (b) Is (1, 1/2) in the closed box [0, 1] × [1/2, 1]? Is (1/2, 0)? (Yes; no, since 0 < 1/2.) (c) Describe {x ∈ ℝ² : x_1 = x_2 and x_1 + x_2 = 1} (the single point (1/2, 1/2)).
  - **B1:** let P = ∏[a_k, b_k] and Q = ∏[c_k, d_k] be closed boxes in ℝⁿ (so a_k ≤ b_k, c_k ≤ d_k). Prove P ⊆ Q iff c_k ≤ a_k and b_k ≤ d_k for every k. Answer: (⇐) if x ∈ P then c_k ≤ a_k ≤ x_k ≤ b_k ≤ d_k for each k, so x ∈ Q. (⇒) the corners a = (a_1, …, a_n) and b = (b_1, …, b_n) lie in P (because a_k ≤ b_k), hence in Q; reading coordinate k of a ∈ Q gives c_k ≤ a_k, and of b ∈ Q gives b_k ≤ d_k. Remark in the solution: the hypothesis a_k ≤ b_k is used for the corners; if P were allowed empty, P ⊆ Q would hold for every Q.
  - **B2:** (a) the intersection of the closed boxes P, Q of B1 is {x : max{a_k, c_k} ≤ x_k ≤ min{b_k, d_k} for every k}, a closed box if max{a_k, c_k} ≤ min{b_k, d_k} for every k and empty otherwise. (b) The union [0, 1] × [0, 1] ∪ [2, 3] × [0, 1] is not a closed box. Answer (b): suppose it equals ∏[p_k, q_k]; it contains (0, 0) and (3, 0), so p_1 ≤ 0, q_1 ≥ 3 and p_2 ≤ 0 ≤ q_2; then (3/2, 0) is in the box but in neither rectangle (3/2 is in neither [0, 1] nor [2, 3]).
  - **C1:** for m, n ≥ 1, the map κ: ℝᵐ × ℝⁿ → ℝ^{m+n}, ((x_1, …, x_m), (y_1, …, y_n)) ↦ (x_1, …, x_m, y_1, …, y_n), is a bijection (`@fd2:def-bijective`). Answer: injective — equal images agree in all m + n places, so the two first entries agree in every place and so do the second entries; by tuple equality and `@fd2:def-ordered-pair` the pairs are equal. Surjective — (z_1, …, z_{m+n}) is the image of ((z_1, …, z_m), (z_{m+1}, …, z_{m+n})). Closing sentence: this is the identification ℝ² × ℝ = ℝ³ of the warning; the book uses it only in words.

## 02-vector-operations.md — Adding Points and Scaling Them

*Budget: 3,500–4,500. Method section: **work coordinatewise**, full.*

- **Hook.** Two operations make ℝⁿ more than a set: adding two points and stretching one by a real factor. Every law they obey comes from a law of ℝ, applied in each coordinate separately; the section turns that observation into a method.
- **`def-vector-operations-rn`** (major): for x, y ∈ ℝⁿ and c ∈ ℝ: x + y ≔ (x_1 + y_1, …, x_n + y_n); cx ≔ (cx_1, …, cx_n); 0 ≔ (0, …, 0); −x ≔ (−1)x; x − y ≔ x + (−y). Words: "point" when we locate, "vector" when we add or scale; the same objects. Prose (Lang 9's "located vectors" in our words): the **displacement** from a to b is b − a, and b = a + (b − a); we do not make arrows into a new kind of object.
- **`[Scratch work]` idea** before the laws, with the **Givens/Goal table** (prose form, STYLE §12.5): Givens "x, y ∈ ℝⁿ, c ∈ ℝ"; Goal "c(x + y) = cx + cy". Row 2 (unwind the goal by tuple equality): Goal "for every k: the k-th coordinate of c(x + y) equals the k-th coordinate of cx + cy". Row 3 (unwind both sides by `def-vector-operations-rn`): Goal "c(x_k + y_k) = cx_k + cy_k for every k". Gap closed by the distributive law of ℝ (`@fd3:def-commutative-ring-and-field`).
- **`exm-method-coordinatewise`** `[Method: work coordinatewise]`: *Template.* To prove an equation between vectors, fix k, compute the k-th coordinate of each side from the definitions, and prove the resulting equation in ℝ. *When.* The statement is an equation (or a conjunction of equations) built from +, scalar multiples and coordinates. *Usual failure.* Applying it to a statement that is not "for every k": "x ≠ 0" means **some** x_k ≠ 0; "x and y are parallel" is not a coordinatewise condition. Two worked examples follow (the laws, and (j) below, which needs the failure case).
- **`prp-vector-space-laws-rn`**: for x, y, z ∈ ℝⁿ and c, d ∈ ℝ: (a) (x + y) + z = x + (y + z); (b) x + y = y + x; (c) x + 0 = x; (d) x + (−x) = 0; (e) c(dx) = (cd)x; (f) 1x = x; (g) c(x + y) = cx + cy; (h) (c + d)x = cx + dx; (i) 0x = 0, c0 = 0, (−c)x = −(cx) = c(−x); (j) if cx = 0, then c = 0 or x = 0. Proof: (a)–(i) coordinatewise (worked example 1: (g) in full, the rest "in the same way", each named with the field law it uses). (j) is worked example 2: suppose cx = 0 and x ≠ 0; **x ≠ 0 gives one k with x_k ≠ 0** (the failure case: not every coordinate); then cx_k = 0 with x_k ≠ 0, so c = 0 by `@fd3:lem-field-fraction-rules` (d). Prose pointer: LA1.1 takes (a)–(h) as the axioms of a vector space; here they are theorems about ℝⁿ.
- **`lem-vector-sums-coordinatewise`**: finite sums Σ_{j=1}^m v_j of vectors are defined by the recursion of `@fd2:def-sum-product` with X = ℝⁿ (empty sum 0); then (Σ_{j=1}^m v_j)_k = Σ_{j=1}^m (v_j)_k for every k, and Σ_j (c_j v_j + d_j w_j) = Σ_j c_j v_j + Σ_j d_j w_j. Proof: induction on m (`@fd1:ax-induction`); the step is (a) of the laws and one coordinatewise line.
- **`def-standard-basis-rn`**: e_k ≔ the n-tuple with 1 in place k and 0 elsewhere (k = 1, …, n). In ℝ²: e_1 = (1, 0), e_2 = (0, 1).
- **`prp-standard-basis-expansion`**: every x ∈ ℝⁿ satisfies x = Σ_{k=1}^n x_k e_k; and if Σ_k c_k e_k = Σ_k d_k e_k, then c_k = d_k for every k. Proof: by `lem-vector-sums-coordinatewise`, the j-th coordinate of Σ_k c_k e_k is Σ_k c_k (e_k)_j = c_j (all terms but k = j are 0); so the first claim is coordinatewise, and the second compares j-th coordinates.
- **`def-parallel-rn`**: x and y are **parallel** if y = cx for some c ∈ ℝ or x = cy for some c ∈ ℝ. Remarks: 0 is parallel to every vector; the "or" is needed (with only "y = cx", x = 0 and y = e_1 would be parallel one way and not the other); for x ≠ 0, x and y are parallel iff y = cx for some c (C1).
- **`exm-midpoints-and-parallelograms`**: (a) the **midpoint** m ≔ (a + b)/2 of a, b satisfies m − a = b − m (coordinatewise); with a = (1, 4), b = (5, −2): m = (3, 1). (b) For points a, b, c, d: b − a = c − d iff (a + c)/2 = (b + d)/2 (both say a + c = b + d): a quadrilateral whose opposite sides are equal displacements has diagonals with one common midpoint. Picture described in words; no picture is a reason.
- **Warning.** "x ≠ 0" is not "x_k ≠ 0 for every k": e_1 ≠ 0 in ℝ², yet its second coordinate is 0. And there is no product of two vectors here that gives a vector: (1, 2)(3, 4) is not defined (coordinatewise multiplication exists but is not used; §03 gives the product that is used, and it is a number).
- **Quick check.** Is (2, 4, −6) parallel to (−1, −2, 3)? Is (1, 2) parallel to (2, 3)? (Yes, (2, 4, −6) = −2(−1, −2, 3); no: (2, 3) = c(1, 2) needs c = 2 and c = 3/2, and (1, 2) = c(2, 3) needs c = 1/2 and c = 2/3.)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1:** compute 2(1, 0, −1) − 3(0, 1, 1) + (1, 1, 1) and write the result as Σ c_k e_k ((3, −2, −4) = 3e_1 − 2e_2 − 4e_3). **A2:** which of these conditions on x, y ∈ ℝⁿ are "for every k" conditions, so that the method applies directly: (a) x = y; (b) x ≠ y; (c) x + y = 0; (d) x is parallel to e_1? ((a), (c) yes; (b) is "some k"; (d) is "x_k = 0 for every k ≥ 2", which is coordinatewise once rewritten: if x = ce_1 then x_k = 0 for k ≥ 2, and conversely x = x_1e_1; the parallel clause e_1 = cx is covered since then x ≠ 0, c ≠ 0 and x = (1/c)e_1.)
  - **B1 [Prove or disprove]** (verdict first): (a) if x + y = x + z, then y = z (**true**: coordinatewise cancellation in ℝ); (b) if cx = cy, then x = y (**false**: c = 0, x = e_1, y = e_2); (c) if cx = dx and x ≠ 0, then c = d (**true**: (c − d)x = 0 by (h), (i), then (j)); (d) if x is parallel to y and y is parallel to z, then x is parallel to z (**false**: y = 0, x = e_1, z = e_2 in ℝ²); (e) if x and y are parallel and both nonzero, then y = cx with c ≠ 0 (**true**: y = cx or x = cy; in the first case c ≠ 0 since y ≠ 0; in the second c ≠ 0 since x ≠ 0, and y = (1/c)x).
  - **B2:** (a) find every t with (1, t, 2) parallel to (2, 6, 4) (t = 3: since (2, 6, 4) ≠ 0 and (1, t, 2) ≠ 0, B1(e) gives (2, 6, 4) = c(1, t, 2) with c ≠ 0; the first coordinate forces c = 2, then 6 = 2t); (b) prove (b) of `exm-midpoints-and-parallelograms` in both directions, naming the laws used.
  - **C1:** let y ≠ 0. Prove: x is parallel to y iff x = ty for some t ∈ ℝ; and the set of vectors parallel to y is {ty : t ∈ ℝ}. Answer: (⇐) definition. (⇒) if x = ty done; if y = cx, then c ≠ 0 (else y = 0), so x = (1/c)y by (e), (f). The set statement is the same equivalence.
  - **C2 [Exit check: two vectors that are not parallel].** Let x, y ∈ ℝⁿ. Prove: x and y are not parallel iff, for all s, t ∈ ℝ, sx + ty = 0 implies s = t = 0. Hint ladder: rung 1 "for (⇒), suppose sx + ty = 0 with s ≠ 0, and solve for x"; rung 2 "for (⇐), contrapositive: if y = cx, choose s and t not both zero". Answer: (⇒) suppose sx + ty = 0. If s ≠ 0, then x = (−t/s)y, so x, y are parallel, a contradiction; so s = 0, then ty = 0, and y ≠ 0 (else 0 = y is parallel to x), so t = 0 by (j). (⇐) contrapositive: if y = cx then cx + (−1)y = 0 with −1 ≠ 0; if x = cy then (−1)x + cy = 0. **Marking scheme** (10): 2 — sets up (⇒) with sx + ty = 0 and arbitrary s, t; 3 — the case s ≠ 0 leads to x = (−t/s)y, citing the laws; 2 — the case s = 0 gives t = 0, with y ≠ 0 justified; 2 — (⇐) by contrapositive with explicit s, t not both 0 in each case of `def-parallel-rn`; 1 — conclusion. Fatal: dividing by s without s ≠ 0; treating only "y = cx" and not "x = cy"; concluding t = 0 from ty = 0 without y ≠ 0. Second route (prove (⇐) directly by contradiction) maps to the same last 3 points.

## 03-the-dot-product.md — The Dot Product

*Budget: 3,500–4,500. Uses work coordinatewise at **Idea** level. Not a method section (no new method box); it has the chapter's first find-the-error item.*

- **Hook.** Length in the plane, by Pythagoras, is √(x_1² + x_2²). The expression under the root is a sum of products of coordinates. Taking it apart for two vectors gives a product of x and y that is a **number**, and from that number come length, perpendicularity and (in §06) the cosine of an angle.
- **`lem-nonnegative-sum-zero`** (only if F4.6 does not own it; otherwise a citation of F4.6): for reals a_1, …, a_n ≥ 0, Σ a_k ≥ 0, and Σ a_k = 0 iff a_k = 0 for every k. Proof: induction on n with `@fd3:prp-ordered-ring-rules` (b) (sum of nonnegatives is nonnegative, strict if one term is strict).
- **`def-dot-product-rn`** (major: full template; slogan *multiply coordinate by coordinate, then add*): ⟨x, y⟩ ≔ Σ_{k=1}^n x_k y_k. For n = 1 it is the product xy. One sentence: other books write x · y.
- **`prp-dot-product-laws-rn`**: for x, y, z ∈ ℝⁿ and c ∈ ℝ: (a) ⟨x, y⟩ = ⟨y, x⟩; (b) ⟨x + y, z⟩ = ⟨x, z⟩ + ⟨y, z⟩ and ⟨cx, y⟩ = c⟨x, y⟩, and the same in the second slot; (c) ⟨x, x⟩ = Σ x_k² ≥ 0, and ⟨x, x⟩ = 0 iff x = 0; (d) ⟨x, e_k⟩ = x_k, and ⟨e_j, e_k⟩ = 1 if j = k, 0 if j ≠ k; (e) ⟨x, 0⟩ = 0. Idea (Idea-level back-reference to `exm-method-coordinatewise`): each term is a product in ℝ; the laws of ℝ apply term by term, and F4.6's Σ rules collect them. Proof: (a) commutativity in each term; (b) distributivity in each term and Σ linearity (F4.6); second slot from (a); (c) each x_k² ≥ 0 by `@fd3:prp-ordered-ring-rules` (e) (or = 0 when x_k = 0), then `lem-nonnegative-sum-zero`, and x_k² = 0 iff x_k = 0 (`@fd3:lem-field-fraction-rules` (d)); (d) one nonzero term; (e) from (b) with c = 0.
- **Worked expansion** (displayed, used in §04): ⟨x + y, x + y⟩ = ⟨x, x⟩ + 2⟨x, y⟩ + ⟨y, y⟩ and ⟨x − y, x − y⟩ = ⟨x, x⟩ − 2⟨x, y⟩ + ⟨y, y⟩, each step named ((b) twice, then (a)).
- **`def-orthogonal-rn`** (major): x and y are **orthogonal**, x ⊥ y, if ⟨x, y⟩ = 0. Motivating example first (the hook's picture in words): in ℝ², (1, 2) and (−2, 1) "look perpendicular" and ⟨(1, 2), (−2, 1)⟩ = 0. Remarks: 0 ⊥ every vector; by (c), 0 is the only vector orthogonal to itself; e_j ⊥ e_k for j ≠ k.
- **`prp-orthogonal-test-rn`**: (a) if ⟨x, z⟩ = 0 for every z ∈ ℝⁿ, then x = 0; (b) for x, y ∈ ℝⁿ the following are equivalent: (i) x = y; (ii) ⟨x, z⟩ = ⟨y, z⟩ for every z ∈ ℝⁿ; (iii) ⟨x, e_k⟩ = ⟨y, e_k⟩ for k = 1, …, n. Proof: (a) take z = x and use (c). (b) (i) ⇒ (ii) ⇒ (iii) trivial; (iii) ⇒ (i) by (d): x_k = y_k for every k. Aftermath: "test against every vector" or "against the n standard vectors"; F4.9 uses it to show an isometry fixing 0 is linear.
- **`exm-dot-products-computed`**: (a) ⟨(1, 2, −1), (3, 0, 4)⟩ = 3 + 0 − 4 = −1; (b) (1, 1, 1, 1) ⊥ (1, −1, 1, −1) in ℝ⁴; (c) the vectors of ℝ² orthogonal to (1, 1) are exactly t(1, −1), t ∈ ℝ (x_1 + x_2 = 0 iff x = (x_1, −x_1) = x_1(1, −1)); (d) in ℝ³ the vectors orthogonal to e_3 are those with x_3 = 0.
- **Warning.** No cancellation: ⟨x, y⟩ = ⟨x, z⟩ does not give y = z (x = e_1, y = e_2, z = 2e_2: both products are 0). No associativity: ⟨x, y⟩z and x⟨y, z⟩ are vectors that differ in general (x = y = e_1, z = e_2: e_2 versus 0). And ⟨x, y⟩ = 0 does not mean x = 0 or y = 0 (e_1, e_2).
- **Quick check.** Is (2, −1, 3) ⊥ (1, 5, 1)? (Yes: 2 − 5 + 3 = 0.) What is ⟨x, x⟩ for x = (1, −2, 0, 3)? (14.)
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1:** (a) ⟨(1, −2, 0, 3), (4, 1, 7, −1)⟩ (4 − 2 + 0 − 3 = −1); (b) true or false for all x, y ∈ ℝⁿ, with a reason or counterexample: ⟨2x, 2y⟩ = 2⟨x, y⟩ (false: it is 4⟨x, y⟩; x = y = e_1 gives 4 ≠ 2); ⟨x, x⟩ = 0 ⇒ x = 0 (true, (c)); ⟨x, y⟩ = 0 ⇒ x = 0 or y = 0 (false: e_1, e_2).
  - **B1:** find every x ∈ ℝ³ orthogonal to both (1, 1, 0) and (0, 1, 1) (x_1 + x_2 = 0 and x_2 + x_3 = 0, so x = t(1, −1, 1), t ∈ ℝ; check (1, −1, 1) against both). **B2:** prove ⟨x − y, x + y⟩ = ⟨x, x⟩ − ⟨y, y⟩, naming the law behind each step; deduce that ⟨x, x⟩ = ⟨y, y⟩ iff x − y ⊥ x + y (used again in §04 B1(b)). **B3:** let a ∈ ℝⁿ, a ≠ 0. Show that for every r ∈ ℝ there is x with ⟨a, x⟩ = r, and that x ≔ (r/⟨a, a⟩)a is the only such x parallel to a (existence: ⟨a, a⟩ > 0 by (c), and ⟨a, x⟩ = r; uniqueness: a vector parallel to a ≠ 0 is ta by §02 C1, and ⟨a, ta⟩ = r forces t = r/⟨a, a⟩). Nothing about dimension (LA's).
  - **C1 [Find the error, true conclusion].** "Claim: if x ∈ ℝⁿ and ⟨x, z⟩ = 0 for every z ∈ ℝⁿ, then x = 0. Proof: take z = e_1. Then 0 = ⟨x, e_1⟩ = x_1 by `prp-dot-product-laws-rn` (d). The failing line is "Hence x = 0": from one instance (k = 1) it concludes a statement about every coordinate, which is (N1) of `@fd1:exm-not-allowed-catalogue` (a "for all" from instances), here from a single instance. Counterexample to the step: n = 2, x = e_2: ⟨x, e_1⟩ = 0 yet x ≠ 0 (the hypothesis "for every z" fails for this x, which is why the conclusion survives, but the step from "x_1 = 0" to "x = 0" is invalid). Repair: take z = x, or take z = e_k for every k. Note in the solution: for n = 1 the step is valid.
  - **C2:** (a) prove that if x ⊥ y and x ⊥ z then x ⊥ sy + tz for all s, t; (b) show that if x is orthogonal to e_1 + e_2 and to e_1 − e_2 in ℝ², then x = 0; (c) give nonzero y, z ∈ ℝ³ such that some x ≠ 0 is orthogonal to both. ## 04-length-and-distance.md — Length and Distance

*Budget: 4,000–5,000. Method section: **expand the square**, full. Key proof: `prp-norm-coordinate-bounds` with `cor-balls-and-boxes-rn`.*

- **Hook.** ⟨x, x⟩ is a sum of squares: by Pythagoras in the plane, the square of the length. Its square root is the length, and it exists because ℝ is complete. Lengths are awkward to compute with (a root of a sum); their squares are polynomial. The section's method is to move to squares and back.
- **`lem-square-root-rules`** (citation of F4.6 if F4.6 states these; otherwise proved here): for a ≥ 0, √a denotes the unique b ≥ 0 with b² = a (`@fd3:prp-cof-square-roots` in ℝ). Then: (a) √(a²) = |a| for every a ∈ ℝ; (b) for a, b ≥ 0, a ≤ b iff √a ≤ √b, and a = b iff √a = √b; (c) for a, b ≥ 0, √(ab) = √a √b. Proof: (a) |a| ≥ 0 and |a|² = a² (`@fd3:prp-absolute-value-ordered-field` (b)), so uniqueness; (b) `@fd3:prp-ordered-ring-rules` (g) applied to √a, √b ≥ 0 (with (i) for the non-strict form); (c) √a√b ≥ 0 and (√a√b)² = ab, uniqueness.
- **`def-norm-rn`** (major: full template; slogan *length is the square root of the dot product with itself*): ‖x‖ ≔ √⟨x, x⟩, defined because ⟨x, x⟩ ≥ 0 (`prp-dot-product-laws-rn` (c)). In ℝ²: ‖(3, 4)‖ = 5. Weird example as a Quick check before it: is there a nonzero vector of length 0? (No, by (a) below.)
- **`prp-norm-basic-rn`**: for x ∈ ℝⁿ and c ∈ ℝ: (a) ‖x‖ ≥ 0, and ‖x‖ = 0 iff x = 0; (b) ‖cx‖ = |c|‖x‖, in particular ‖−x‖ = ‖x‖; (c) ‖x‖² = ⟨x, x⟩; (d) for n = 1, ‖x‖ = |x|; (e) ‖e_k‖ = 1. Proof: (a) from (c) of the dot-product laws and `lem-square-root-rules`; (b) ⟨cx, cx⟩ = c²⟨x, x⟩, then `lem-square-root-rules` (c) and (a): √(c²) = |c|; (c) definition; (d) √(x²) = |x|; (e) ⟨e_k, e_k⟩ = 1.
- **`def-unit-vector-rn`**: u is a **unit vector** if ‖u‖ = 1. For x ≠ 0, x/‖x‖ ≔ (1/‖x‖)x is a unit vector (by (b)), and the unit vectors parallel to x are exactly x/‖x‖ and −x/‖x‖ (if u = tx with ‖u‖ = 1, then |t|‖x‖ = 1; `def-parallel-rn` with C1 of §02).
- **`def-distance-rn`**: d(x, y) ≔ ‖x − y‖. In ℝ²: √((x_1 − y_1)² + (x_2 − y_2)²), Lang 5's distance formula in our terms, now a definition, not a theorem about figures. For n = 1, d(x, y) = |x − y|.
- **`prp-distance-basic-rn`**: (a) d(x, y) ≥ 0, = 0 iff x = y; (b) d(x, y) = d(y, x); (c) d(x + a, y + a) = d(x, y); (d) d(cx, cy) = |c| d(x, y). Proof: one line each from `prp-norm-basic-rn`. The triangle inequality waits for §05 (say so: it is the one property that needs an idea).
- **`[Scratch work]`** for Pythagoras, with the **Givens/Goal table** (prose): Givens "x, y ∈ ℝⁿ"; Goal "‖x + y‖² = ‖x‖² + ‖y‖² iff ⟨x, y⟩ = 0". Row 2: replace each ‖v‖² by ⟨v, v⟩ (`prp-norm-basic-rn` (c)): Goal "⟨x + y, x + y⟩ = ⟨x, x⟩ + ⟨y, y⟩ iff ⟨x, y⟩ = 0". Row 3: expand the left side (§03's worked expansion): Goal "⟨x, x⟩ + 2⟨x, y⟩ + ⟨y, y⟩ = ⟨x, x⟩ + ⟨y, y⟩ iff ⟨x, y⟩ = 0". Gap: cancel and divide by 2.
- **`exm-method-expand-the-square`** `[Method: expand the square]`: *Template.* (1) If the statement compares lengths, square both sides; for nonnegative quantities this changes nothing (`lem-square-root-rules` (b)). (2) Write each ‖v‖² as ⟨v, v⟩. (3) Expand by `prp-dot-product-laws-rn`. ## 05-cauchy-schwarz-and-the-triangle-inequality.md — Cauchy–Schwarz and the Triangle Inequality

*Budget: 4,000–5,000. Method section: **normalize**, full; expand the square at Idea level. Key proof: `thm-cauchy-schwarz-rn`.*

- **Hook.** The triangle inequality in ℝ was proved by cases on signs. In ℝⁿ there are no signs to split on. Expanding ‖x + y‖² reduces it to one inequality, ⟨x, y⟩ ≤ ‖x‖‖y‖, and that inequality is the real content. The naive attempt as the hook (Cummings move): "⟨x, y⟩ = Σ x_k y_k ≤ Σ |x_k||y_k| ≤ … ?" stalls, because a coordinate-by-coordinate bound loses the geometry. The section's proof uses no coordinates at all.
- **`thm-cauchy-schwarz-rn`** (key proof; major). For x, y ∈ ℝⁿ: (a) |⟨x, y⟩| ≤ ‖x‖‖y‖; (b) equality holds in (a) iff x and y are parallel (`def-parallel-rn`); (c) ⟨x, y⟩ = ‖x‖‖y‖ iff x = 0 or y = cx for some c ≥ 0.
  - *Try it first (ten minutes): take two unit vectors u, v and write out ‖u − v‖² ≥ 0. What does it say about ⟨u, v⟩? What else could you write out?*
  - **`[Scratch work]`** with the **Givens/Goal table** (prose): Givens "x, y ∈ ℝⁿ"; Goal "|⟨x, y⟩| ≤ ‖x‖‖y‖". Row 2 (both sides change by the factor a > 0 when x is replaced by ax: ⟨ax, y⟩ = a⟨x, y⟩ and ‖ax‖ = a‖x‖): Givens add "x ≠ 0, y ≠ 0 (the zero case is 0 ≤ 0)"; Goal "|⟨u, v⟩| ≤ 1 for u = x/‖x‖, v = y/‖y‖". Row 3 (expand the square on unit vectors): ‖u ∓ v‖² = 2 ∓ 2⟨u, v⟩ ≥ 0. Gap closed: −1 ≤ ⟨u, v⟩ ≤ 1. The attempt of the Try-it prompt yields only ⟨u, v⟩ ≤ 1; the other expansion gives the lower bound.
  - *Proof:* if x = 0 or y = 0, both sides of (a) are 0, the vectors are parallel, and (c) holds with x = 0 or with c = 0. Let x, y ≠ 0, u ≔ x/‖x‖, v ≔ y/‖y‖ (unit, `def-unit-vector-rn`). By the dot-product laws, ⟨x, y⟩ = ‖x‖‖y‖⟨u, v⟩. Expand: 0 ≤ ‖u − v‖² = 2 − 2⟨u, v⟩ and 0 ≤ ‖u + v‖² = 2 + 2⟨u, v⟩, so |⟨u, v⟩| ≤ 1 (`@fd3:prp-absolute-value-ordered-field` (d)), and multiplying by ‖x‖‖y‖ > 0 gives (a). For (c): ⟨x, y⟩ = ‖x‖‖y‖ iff ⟨u, v⟩ = 1 iff ‖u − v‖ = 0 iff u = v iff y = (‖y‖/‖x‖)x, a multiple with c > 0; conversely if y = cx with c ≥ 0, then ⟨x, y⟩ = c‖x‖² = ‖x‖‖cx‖. Likewise ⟨x, y⟩ = −‖x‖‖y‖ iff u = −v iff y = −(‖y‖/‖x‖)x. For (b): equality in (a) means ⟨x, y⟩ = ±‖x‖‖y‖, so (for x, y ≠ 0) y is a nonzero multiple of x; conversely if y = cx (or x = cy) then |⟨x, y⟩| = |c|‖x‖² = ‖x‖‖y‖ (resp. symmetric).

  - Aftermath: in coordinates, (a) says (Σ x_k y_k)² ≤ (Σ x_k²)(Σ y_k²), which is the inequality F4.6 proved by Lagrange's identity (prose, citing F4.6's label as a comparison only). ## 06-projection-and-angle.md — Projection onto a Line and the Cosine of an Angle

*Budget: 4,000–5,000 (R group included in the exercise budget, not in main line). Method section: **subtract the projection**, full; normalize at Idea level; expand the square terse. Key proof: `thm-projection-closest-point`.*

- **Hook.** Which point of the line through 0 in the direction y is nearest to x? In the plane one drops a perpendicular. Without pictures: look for the multiple ty with x − ty ⊥ y. - **`[Scratch work]`** with the **Givens/Goal table** (prose): Givens "x, y ∈ ℝⁿ, y ≠ 0"; Goal "find t with ‖x − ty‖ ≤ ‖x − sy‖ for every s".
