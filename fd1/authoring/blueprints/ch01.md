**Purpose.**
- Teach natural deduction for \( \wedge \), \( \to \), \( \vee \) as a **real calculus**, slowly: what a statement and a connective are; what a derivation is and how to read one; then each rule, defined by its schema, used in many small derivations, and seen at work in a proof about numbers.
- Build, gently and one rule at a time, the two directions of reasoning that Chapter 2 §06 turns into a method: **goal-directed** (the goal's main connective picks the introduction rule, and so the shape of the derivation) and **premise-directed** (an available line's main connective picks the elimination rule that uses it). Every worked derivation has an Idea that says why each rule was chosen, in these terms.
- The reader leaves able to (i) check any derivation in \( \wedge \), \( \to \), \( \vee \) line by line, naming the rule, the cited lines and why they are available; (ii) find a derivation of a short sequent in these connectives; (iii) see the rules underneath ordinary proofs by cases and "assume … then …" arguments about numbers.
- Truth tables are **not** here (decisions 2: rules first, tables in Chapter 3). Negation and contradiction are **not** here: \( \neg \) and \( \bot \) are introduced as symbols in §01 and get their rules in Chapter 2.

**Signature moves** (`index.md`, "Named moves"):
- **Read the skeleton off the goal.** The goal's main connective chooses the introduction rule: \( \wedge \) gives two subgoals, \( \to \) a box, \( \vee \) a choice to postpone.
- **Use a premise by its main connective.** Its elimination rule says what you may do with it: take a conjunction apart, feed an implication its hypothesis, split a disjunction into cases.
- **Open a box, close a box.** An assumption lives exactly as long as its box.
- **Work from both ends.** Premises at the top, the goal at the bottom; fill the gap from both sides.
- **Postpone a disjunction.** Choose a side of a goal \( \varphi \vee \psi \) only when that side is in hand.
- **Cases end in the current goal.** Both boxes of \( \vee \)E close on the statement you are trying to reach.

## 02-derivations.md — Derivations: Premises, Lines and Justifications

*One new thing:* the **derivation format** — numbered lines, premises above a bar, a justification on every line naming one rule and the lines it uses — and how to read a rule **schema**. No logical rule yet, and no boxes.

*Migrates:* from the drafted §02: the paragraph introducing a derivation as "numbered lines, each carrying one statement and, on the right, its justification", the reading of the line "\( \rulename{\wedge I} \) 3, 2" (moved to §03, where \( \wedge \)I exists), `def-derivable` (kept label; wording adjusted, below), `exm-derivations` (kept label; items (a) and a revised (c); item (b), \( \vdash P \to P \), moves to §05's `exm-first-boxes`), and the first half of "How to check a derivation" (the questions *which rule?* and *which lines?*; the availability question and the context column move to §05). New: the toy rule system, `def-rule-schema`, `def-box-free-derivation`, `exm-chaining-derivation`, `exm-not-a-derivation`, the subsection "Reading a derivation", and all exercises.

- **Hook.** §01 said a proof is a chain in which every line has a reason. How would anyone *check* such a chain without understanding its subject? Take a chain of inequalities, \( 0 < \tfrac{1}{3} < \tfrac{1}{2} < 1 \), so \( 0 < 1 \): every step is "if \( a < b \) and \( b < c \), then \( a < c \)", and a reader can check each step by matching it against that one pattern, without knowing anything else about numbers. This section writes such chains in the format the rest of Part 0 uses, with the reasons on the page. The logic comes in the next section; here the only rule is about numbers, so that the **format** can be seen on its own.
- **The toy rule (ours, used only in this section).** \( \mathrm{Tr} \) (chaining): from \( a < b \) and \( b < c \), write \( a < c \). A second toy rule for the exercises: \( \mathrm{Sh} \) (shift): from \( a < b \), write \( a + c < b + c \), for any number \( c \). Say plainly that these are facts about numbers used as rules for practice, not rules of logic, and that the book never uses them again.
- **Definition `def-rule-schema` (new, minor but load-bearing).** A **rule schema** is a pattern of lines: **given** lines at the top and exactly **one new** line at the bottom, whose justification names the rule and cites the given lines. (Every rule of this chapter has at least one given line; Section 1.5 adds given **boxes**, cited as ranges; Chapter 4's =I has none. Word the definition so that none of these needs a new definition: "given lines and boxes, possibly none".) A rule with two forms, such as \( \wedge \)E (left or right conjunct), is two schemas. Letters in the pattern stand for arbitrary **expressions** of the right kind (expressions for numbers \( a, b, c \) in the toy rules; statements \( \varphi, \psi, \chi \) in logic, from the next section on) and are replaced **consistently**: the same letter by the same expression, symbol for symbol, everywhere. (S2 v3: "the same number" would make \( 0 < \tfrac24 \) and \( \tfrac12 < 1 \) chain by Tr, which a checker can see only by doing arithmetic; §02 B1's "Sh writes \( a + c < b + c \) literally" already assumes the symbol-for-symbol reading.) Line numbers in a schema are written \( i, j, k \). A line is **justified by the rule** when it and the lines it cites, with their numbers, are an **instance** of the schema. The toy rule's schema, as it will be printed (inside the definition block, as an example of a schema):

```{=latex}
% schema
\begin{center}
$\begin{nd}[schema]
  \have {i} {a < b}
  \have {j} {b < c}
  \have {k} {a < c} \by{Tr}{i,j}
\end{nd}$
\end{center}
```

  Paraphrase, clause by clause: the given lines may be anywhere above line \( k \), in either order, and the citation "Tr \( i, j \)" says which line plays which part; the middle letter \( b \) must be **the same** expression, symbol for symbol, in both given lines (this is the clause a careless step breaks); nothing else is required. Instance example: \( i = 3 \), \( j = 5 \), \( a = 0 \), \( b = \tfrac{1}{2} \), \( c = 1 \). Non-instance by minimal change: from \( 0 < \tfrac{1}{2} \) and \( \tfrac{1}{3} < 1 \), "Tr" does not give \( 0 < 1 \) (the middle letters differ), although \( 0 < 1 \) is true — the rule was not applied, so the line has no justification. *Why this definition:* a rule is applied by matching, so a checker needs no knowledge of the subject; that is the whole point of a formal proof.
- **Definition `def-box-free-derivation` (new).** Relative to a fixed list of rules: a **box-free derivation** is a finite list of numbered lines \( 1, 2, \dots, n \), each carrying one statement and a justification, such that (i) the first lines are **premises**, justified by nothing, with a short rule drawn under the last of them; (ii) every later line is justified by **one** rule of the list, citing earlier lines, and it and the cited lines form an instance of that rule's schema. Its **premises** are the premise lines, its **conclusion** the last line. Paraphrase clause by clause; say that "earlier" is the only availability condition **for now**, and that Section 1.5 adds assumptions and boxes, after which "earlier" is refined to "available" (`def-derivation`). Degenerate case: a single premise line is a derivation whose premise is its conclusion. Say that the fitch format prints premises with a vertical bar and a short horizontal rule under them, and why (the bar will matter when boxes open in §05).
- **Definition `def-derivable` (kept label, reworded).** For statements \( \Gamma = \varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n \) and \( \psi \), \( \Gamma \vdash \psi \) means: **there is** a derivation, using the rules of logic, whose premises all belong to \( \Gamma \) and whose last line is \( \psi \); \( \vdash \psi \) when \( \Gamma \) is empty. The paraphrase says three things: (1) the sequent of @def-sequent (Section 1.1) is now an exact claim, proved by exhibiting a derivation; (2) "derivation" here means box-free until Section 1.5, which widens the word, and the definition is then read with the wider notion (nothing proved before changes, since a box-free derivation is a derivation); (3) a derivation need not use every premise; (4) \( \vdash \) is always relative to a list of rules: in this section only, with the toy rules in place of the rules of logic, write "\( \Gamma \vdash \psi \) with Tr" (or "with Tr and Sh"), and A1(d), A1(e) and the Quick check use that phrase (S2 v3: as first written, \( \vdash \) meant "using the rules of logic", of which there are none yet, while A1(d), A1(e) and the Quick check asked for toy-system sequents). The rules of logic are introduced from the next section on, one at a time; \( \Gamma \vdash \psi \) always means "with the rules introduced so far" when it is asserted, and later rules never make a derivable sequent underivable (a derivation stays a derivation when more rules are allowed). One sentence (kept from the drafted §02): because \( P, Q, R \) stand for arbitrary statements, a derivation written with letters proves infinitely many sequents at once; Chapter 3 makes this precise.
- **Worked derivations** (toy system; each with an **Idea** saying how the lines were chosen; this is the first appearance of the two directions of reasoning, named only informally here: "what do we want?" and "what do we have?"):
  1. `exm-chaining-derivation` (a): from \( 0 < x \), \( x < y \), \( y < 1 \) derive \( 0 < 1 \) (5 lines: three premises, Tr 1,2 gives \( 0 < y \), Tr 4,3 gives \( 0 < 1 \)). Idea: work forwards, chaining at the shared middle letter. Then read it back line by line in words.
  2. `exm-chaining-derivation` (b): the same premises in a different order (\( y < 1 \), \( 0 < x \), \( x < y \)), same conclusion: the citation order "Tr 2,3" versus the line order; the justification, not the position, says which line plays which part.
  3. `exm-chaining-derivation` (c): from \( a < b \), \( c < d \), \( b < c \) derive \( a < d \), found **backwards**: to get \( a < d \) by Tr we need some \( m \) with \( a < m \) and \( m < d \); try \( m = b \): \( a < b \) is a premise, and \( b < d \) is a new subgoal, got by Tr from \( b < c \), \( c < d \). Write the finished derivation forwards (5 lines). This is the first goal-directed search in the book; say that the Ideas of later sections do the same with logical rules.
  4. `exm-derivations` (kept label; items revised): (a) the single line \( P \) (premise) is a derivation of \( P \vdash P \) (kept); (b) the empty list is not a derivation (it has no last line) — so, since every rule met before Section 1.5 cites at least one line, nothing is derivable from no premises until Section 1.5 changes that (the degenerate case; do not say "in a box-free world": Chapter 4's =I is box-free and cites nothing); (c) the derivation of `exm-chaining-derivation` (a) read against each clause of `def-box-free-derivation`.
  5. `exm-not-a-derivation` (non-example by minimal change): take (a) and make one change — swap the statements of lines 4 and 5 and keep each one's justification ("4. \( 0 < 1 \) — Tr 5, 3"; "5. \( 0 < y \) — Tr 1, 2": both are instances of Tr, but line 4 cites a later line), or let line 5 cite "Tr 3,4" with the lines in the wrong roles (\( y < 1 \) and \( 0 < y \) do not share a middle letter in the schema's order). Each change fails exactly one of the three checks of "Reading a derivation" below; name it. (S2 v3: "line 4 cites line 5" alone was underspecified; with line 4 unchanged, the citation would also fail the instance check.)

- **How this looks in a mathematical proof.** A chain of inequalities in analysis, \( 0 < \tfrac{1}{n+1} < \tfrac{1}{n} \le 1 \) for \( n \in \nZ_{>0} \), and a school proof that reads it (to conclude \( 0 < 1 \)) as one application of chaining and one of "\( < \) then \( \le \) gives \( < \)", said in words: a different pattern, which is why the justification must name the pattern used. - **Warnings.** (1) Consistent replacement: the middle letter must match (the non-instance above; the tempting "\( 0 < \tfrac{1}{2} \), \( \tfrac{1}{3} < 1 \), so \( 0 < 1 \)"). (2) A premise is **given**, not proved: a derivation shows the conclusion follows *from* the premises; \( \Gamma \vdash \psi \) says nothing about whether the premises are true (example: from \( 1 < 0 \) and \( 0 < -1 \), Tr gives \( 1 < -1 \), a correct derivation with a false conclusion from false premises).

## 03-conjunction.md — And: the Rules \( \wedge \)I and \( \wedge \)E

*One new thing:* the first two rules of logic, \( \wedge \)I and \( \wedge \)E, defined by their schemas; and the first appearance, by name, of **goal-directed** and **premise-directed** reasoning.

*Migrates:* from the drafted §02: `def-rules-conjunction` (kept label; now schema-first), `exm-product-in-unit-interval` (kept), the derivation of \( Q \wedge P \) from \( P \wedge Q \) with the reading of "\( \rulename{\wedge I} \) 3, 2" (kept, now inside `prp-and-commutes`), `prp-and-commutes` (kept), the \( \wedge \) rows of "What the rules sound like" (kept). From the drafted §01: the "Each connective, two rules" discussion of \( \wedge \) is recalled (not repeated). New: `prp-and-associative` (book.yaml must-prove), `exm-conjunction-warm-ups`, the goal/premise-directed subsection, all exercises.

- **Hook.** "\( 0 < x < 1 \)" is a conjunction in disguise (§01). A proof that uses it takes it apart; a proof that proves it proves both halves. These are the two moves of §01's preview; now they become exact patterns.
- **Definition `def-rules-conjunction` (kept label).** The block contains the two schemas, then one sentence each naming them: \( \wedge \)I (**and-introduction**) and \( \wedge \)E (**and-elimination**).

```{=latex}
% schema
\begin{center}
$\begin{nd}[schema]
  \have {i} {\varphi}
  \have {j} {\psi}
  \have {k} {\varphi \wedge \psi} \ai{i,j}
\end{nd}$
\qquad
$\begin{nd}[schema]
  \have {i} {\varphi \wedge \psi}
  \have {k} {\varphi} \ae{i}
\end{nd}$
\qquad
$\begin{nd}[schema]
  \have {i} {\varphi \wedge \psi}
  \have {k} {\psi} \ae{i}
\end{nd}$
\end{center}
```

  (S2 v3: \( \wedge \)E was one schema with two new lines \( k \) and \( l \), which `def-rule-schema` ("one new line") does not allow; now two schemas, so "each by its own application" is what the drawing says.)

- **Clause-by-clause reading (after the block).** \( \wedge \)I: the two given lines may be anywhere earlier, in either order; the citation's **order** decides the order of the conjuncts ("\( \wedge \)I \( i, j \)" puts line \( i \) on the left); \( i = j \) is allowed (then \( \varphi \wedge \varphi \)). \( \wedge \)E: from one conjunction, either conjunct, each by its own application; the rule looks only at the **main connective** of the cited line (§01's `def-main-connective`), so it applies to \( (P \wedge Q) \wedge R \) to give \( P \wedge Q \) or \( R \), and **not** directly \( Q \). Metavariables replaced consistently (@def-rule-schema): in \( (P \wedge Q) \wedge R \), \( \varphi \) is \( P \wedge Q \) and \( \psi \) is \( R \). Why these rules and no others: they are exactly "prove both" and "use either"; §01's slogan made exact. Why they are safe: if the given lines are true, so is the new one (one sentence; Chapter 3).
- **Goal-directed and premise-directed (new short subsection, the chapter's first statement of the two directions).** Two questions to ask before writing any line: *What is the goal's main connective?* If \( \wedge \), the last line will be \( \wedge \)I, and the goal splits into two **subgoals**, one per conjunct (goal-directed, reasoning backwards from the bottom). *What are the available lines' main connectives?* A line with main connective \( \wedge \) can be taken apart by \( \wedge \)E (premise-directed, reasoning forwards from the top). Slogan: *Work from both ends: take the premises apart, and build the goal from its parts.* Say that each later section adds one rule to both lists, and that Chapter 2 §06 collects them into a method.
- **Worked derivations** (each with an Idea naming, for each line, whether it came from the goal or from a premise):
  1. `exm-conjunction-warm-ups`: (a) \( P, Q \vdash Q \wedge P \) (3 lines; \( \wedge \)I 2,1: the citation order). (b) \( P \vdash P \wedge P \) (2 lines; \( \wedge \)I 1,1: the degenerate case, a line cited twice). (c) \( P \wedge Q \vdash Q \) (2 lines). Idea for (a): the goal is a conjunction, so two subgoals, both premises.
  2. `prp-and-commutes` (kept): \( P \wedge Q \vdash Q \wedge P \) (4 lines, D1 of `authoring/reviews/ch01-S2-derivations.md`). Idea: from the goal, \( \wedge \)I needs \( Q \) and \( P \); from the premise, \( \wedge \)E gives both. The two ends meet. Keep the drafted reading of "\( \wedge \)I 3, 2" ("line 3 becomes the left half").
  3. `prp-and-associative` (new; book.yaml must-prove): \( (P \wedge Q) \wedge R \vdash P \wedge (Q \wedge R) \) (7 lines: \( \wedge \)E twice on line 1, twice on line 2, \( \wedge \)I for \( Q \wedge R \), \( \wedge \)I for the goal). Idea: the goal's main connective is the **outer** \( \wedge \), whose right conjunct \( Q \wedge R \) is itself a subgoal; the premise must be taken apart in two layers, because \( \wedge \)E reaches only one layer at a time. Remark: the converse \( P \wedge (Q \wedge R) \vdash (P \wedge Q) \wedge R \) is derived the same way (B1 asks for it); with both directions, brackets in a chain of \( \wedge \)s do not matter, which is why \( 0 < x < 1 < y \) needs none. (No later text may cite B1 for this.)
  4. \( P \wedge Q, R \vdash (P \wedge R) \wedge (Q \wedge R) \) (7 lines; line 2 cited twice, in two different \( \wedge \)I lines). Idea: a line is not used up when cited; every subgoal that needs \( R \) cites the same premise.
  5. (Stretch, in prose with the derivation) \( (P \wedge Q) \wedge (R \wedge S) \vdash (S \wedge Q) \wedge (P \wedge R) \) (10 lines): the goal has four leaves, the premise has four leaves, and the derivation is "take everything apart, rebuild in the new order". Idea: this is the whole theory of \( \wedge \); any rearrangement of a conjunction of letters is derivable from any other in the same way (say it as an observation about this pattern, not as a theorem; Chapter 3's completeness theorem makes it a consequence, prose pointer only).
- **How this looks in a mathematical proof: `exm-product-in-unit-interval` (kept).** \( 0 < x < 1 \) and \( 0 < y < 1 \) give \( 0 < xy < 1 \); the solution names \( \wedge \)E, \( \wedge \)I for the logical joints and "school algebra" for the arithmetic, and ends with the drafted sentence "the facts about numbers supply the content; the rules supply the joints". ## 04-using-an-implication.md — Using an Implication: the Rule \( \to \)E

*One new thing:* \( \to \)E (modus ponens), defined by its schema, and chains of implications, used forwards and found backwards.

*Migrates:* from the drafted §02: the \( \to \)E clause of `def-rules-implication` (now the separate `def-rule-implication-elimination`), the paragraph "The elimination rule is the everyday use of a theorem" with the \( \sqrt 2 < 1.5 < 2 \) example, the \( \to \)E row of "What the rules sound like". From the drafted §01: the "affirming the consequent" discussion in the second Quick check's solution (the \( n = 2 \) witness) is recalled, and the form \( P \to Q, \ Q \vdash P \) is named here as **not** a rule. New: `exm-wason-selection` (book.yaml key example), `exm-implication-chains`, backward chaining, all exercises.

- **Hook: `exm-wason-selection` (our own version of the selection task; book.yaml key example "the conditional is not the biconditional").** Four cards, each with a whole number on one side and a colour on the other; showing \( 6 \), \( 7 \), red, green. Claim: "if a card shows an even number, its other side is red." Which cards must be turned to test the claim? Most people pick \( 6 \) and red. Work it out: the claim is an implication; it can be used (and broken) only by a card with an **even** number (its hypothesis) — turn \( 6 \); the red card satisfies the conclusion, and nothing about implications lets us conclude anything about its number (the converse error again); the \( 7 \) card is irrelevant; the green card **can** break the claim (if its number is even), which needs negation to state exactly, so it is recorded here as an observation; the rule behind it is modus tollens, Chapter 2 §03 (prose pointer to the rule only: `ch02.md` does not revisit the cards, so do not promise that it does; coordinator, add one sentence to Chapter 2 §03 if the card should be paid off there). Say what the task shows: people read "if" as "if and only if". Keep the description of the experiment brief and in our words; no numbers from the literature.
- **Definition `def-rule-implication-elimination` (new).** The schema, then "This rule is called \( \to \)E (**implication-elimination**, or **modus ponens**)":

```{=latex}
% schema
\begin{center}
$\begin{nd}[schema]
  \have {i} {\varphi \to \psi}
  \have {j} {\varphi}
  \have {k} {\psi} \ie{i,j}
\end{nd}$
\end{center}
```

- **Clause-by-clause reading.** The implication and its **hypothesis** must both be on earlier lines; the hypothesis must match \( \varphi \) **exactly**, symbol for symbol (not a statement that "means the same", not a part of it); the rule gives the **conclusion** \( \psi \) and nothing else. Citation convention: implication first, then hypothesis ("\( \to \)E \( i, j \)"), as in fitch-book's `\ie`. Why the rule: it is what "by Theorem so-and-so" means (the drafted \( \sqrt 2 < 2 \) example, kept). Why nothing more: from \( \varphi \to \psi \) alone, or with \( \psi \), nothing follows about \( \varphi \) — Argument B's repair pattern and the red card. Name the pattern \( \varphi \to \psi, \ \psi \vdash \varphi \) ("affirming the consequent") and say that it is not an instance of any rule; the §01 Quick check's witness \( n = 2 \) shows that it can lead from true premises to a false conclusion; Chapter 3 proves no derivation of \( P \to Q, Q \vdash P \) exists (prose pointer). A remark: an implication cannot yet be **proved**, only used; the next section gives the rule that proves one.
- **Premise-directed and goal-directed, for \( \to \)E (subsection "Chains of implications").** *Forwards* (premise-directed): an available implication whose hypothesis is available fires, and its conclusion becomes available; keep firing. *Backwards* (goal-directed): to reach a goal \( \psi \), look for an available \( \varphi \to \psi \); then \( \varphi \) becomes the new subgoal. The two meet in the middle. This is the logical form of "to show \( \psi \), it suffices to show \( \varphi \)" (say so; Chapter 5's phrasebook will return to it).
- **Worked derivations** (`exm-implication-chains`, parts (a)–(e); each with an Idea):
  - (a) \( P \to Q, \ P \vdash Q \): the rule itself (3 lines). Idea: nothing to choose.
  - (b) \( P, \ P \to Q, \ Q \to R \vdash R \) (5 lines): forward chaining; then the same derivation found backwards (goal \( R \); \( Q \to R \) is available; subgoal \( Q \); \( P \to Q \) available; subgoal \( P \): a premise).

```{=latex}
% schema
\begin{center}
$\begin{nd}[schema]
  \open
  \hypo {i} {\varphi}
  \have {} {\vdots}
  \have {j} {\psi}
  \close
  \have {k} {\varphi \to \psi} \ii{i-j}
\end{nd}$
\qquad
$\begin{nd}[schema]
  \have {i} {\varphi \to \psi}
  \have {j} {\varphi}
  \have {k} {\psi} \ie{i,j}
\end{nd}$
\end{center}
```

- **Clause-by-clause reading of \( \to \)I.** (1) *Open a box* whose first line is the **assumption** \( \varphi \), any statement at all; the vertical bar marks the lines where \( \varphi \) is in force. (2) *Inside the box*, every rule may be used, and the lines above the box (the premises, earlier lines not in a closed box) may be cited. (3) *The last line* of the box is \( \psi \), a line **of that box itself**, not inside a further box opened within it (the wording Chapter 3's soundness proof relies on; keep it verbatim from the drafted definition). (4) *Close the box* and write \( \varphi \to \psi \) on the next line, outside; the justification cites the box as a **range** \( i \)–\( j \), not its lines one by one. (5) After the box closes, nothing inside it may be cited again: only the implication leaves. The degenerate case \( i = j \): a box of one line gives \( \varphi \to \varphi \). Slogan (kept): *To prove "if \( \varphi \), then \( \psi \)", assume \( \varphi \) in a box, reach \( \psi \) inside it, and close the box.*
- **Definition `def-derivation` (kept label; the full definition, placed right after the first boxed derivation, `prp-implication-transitive`).** Kept verbatim from the drafted §02 in substance: a derivation is a finite list of numbered lines, each with a statement and a justification, with a set of boxes; each box a block of consecutive lines beginning with an assumption; two boxes disjoint or nested; the last line outside every box. **The scope rule** (line \( i \) available at line \( k \) iff \( i < k \) and every box containing \( i \) contains \( k \); a closed box available at \( k \) iff it ends before \( k \) and the boxes containing it are exactly those containing \( k \)). Lines are (D1) premises, (D2) assumptions, (D3) **repetitions** of an available line ("R \( i \)"), (D4) conclusions of one rule applied to available lines and boxes. ## 06-disjunction-introduction.md — Or, Introduced: the Rule \( \vee \)I

*One new thing:* \( \vee \)I, defined by its schema; and the goal-directed habit of **postponing** a disjunction.

*Migrates:* from the drafted §03: the \( \vee \)I clause of `def-rules-disjunction` (now `def-rule-disjunction-introduction`) and its reading ("\( \rulename{\vee I} \) weakens a statement … \( x \le 0 \)"), the inclusive-or reminder, the \( \vee \)I half of the A1 matching part, and one direction of `exm-cases-on-hypothesis` (the direction \( (P \vee Q) \to R \vdash (P \to R) \wedge (Q \to R) \), which needs no \( \vee \)E; now `exm-or-hypothesis-split`; the drafted aftermath "the one place in this section where \( \vee \)I manufactures the hypothesis of a premise" does **not** move with it: in this section `exm-product-zero`, B3 and both parts of C2 manufacture a premise's hypothesis the same way, so "the one place" would be false; say instead "the first place", S2 v3). New: everything else.

- **Hook.** "\( x \ge 0 \)" means "\( x > 0 \) or \( x = 0 \)" (§01). A proof that has shown \( x > 0 \) writes "so \( x \ge 0 \)" without a second thought: it has thrown information away on purpose, because the definition or the goal asks for the weaker statement. That move is a rule.
- **Definition `def-rule-disjunction-introduction` (new).** Two schemas (either side), then "\( \vee \)I (**or-introduction**)" (S2 v3: printed as two schemas, one new line each, as `def-rule-schema` requires; the first version had one schema with two new lines):

```{=latex}
% schema
\begin{center}
$\begin{nd}[schema]
  \have {i} {\varphi}
  \have {k} {\varphi \vee \psi} \oi{i}
\end{nd}$
\qquad
$\begin{nd}[schema]
  \have {i} {\varphi}
  \have {k} {\psi \vee \varphi} \oi{i}
\end{nd}$
\end{center}
```

- **Clause-by-clause reading.** One given line; the new line is a disjunction with the given statement as **either** disjunct; the other disjunct \( \psi \) is **any statement at all** — it appears in no given line, need not be true, need not be related (the rule's one surprising clause; explain why it is safe: "\( \varphi \) or anything" is true whenever \( \varphi \) is). *Slogan:* *\( \vee \)I loses information, never truth.* (book.yaml: "why \( P \vdash P \vee Q \) is not a loss of truth but of information".)
- **Goal-directed: postpone a disjunction (subsection).** When the goal is \( \varphi \vee \psi \), \( \vee \)I is the obvious last step, but it commits to a side. Commit only when that side is **in hand**; otherwise first use the premises (premise-directed steps), and come back. Demonstrate with a stall: try to derive \( P \vee Q \vdash Q \vee P \) with the rules so far. \( \vee \)I needs \( Q \) or \( P \) on a line; neither follows from \( P \vee Q \) by the rules so far (say "we cannot find one", not "none exists": proving non-derivability is Chapter 3's). The missing move is to **use** the disjunction, which is the next section's rule. This stall is the next section's hook.
- **Worked derivations** (`exm-disjunction-introduction-derivations`, (a)–(d), and `exm-or-hypothesis-split`; each with an Idea):
  - (a) \( P \vdash P \vee Q \) and \( P \vdash Q \vee P \) (2 lines each). Idea: nothing to choose except the side.
  - (b) \( P \wedge Q \vdash P \vee Q \) (3 lines). Idea: premise-directed \( \wedge \)E gives both sides; either choice works — say so, and that the choice is free precisely because both sides are in hand.
  - (c) \( P \to Q \vdash P \to (Q \vee R) \) (5 lines): goal-directed box on \( P \); new goal \( Q \vee R \); postpone; premise-directed \( \to \)E gives \( Q \); now the left side is in hand: \( \vee \)I.
  - (d) \( (P \wedge Q) \to R \vdash P \to (Q \to (R \vee S)) \) (8 lines): two nested boxes read off the goal, \( \wedge \)I to feed the premise, \( \vee \)I last. A recap of every rule so far.
  - `exm-or-hypothesis-split` (migrated half of `exm-cases-on-hypothesis`): \( (P \vee Q) \to R \vdash (P \to R) \wedge (Q \to R) \) (10 lines, D12b; S2 v3: not 9). Idea: goal-directed \( \wedge \)I gives two implication subgoals, each a box; inside the box of \( P \), the premise can fire only on \( P \vee Q \), which \( \vee \)I **manufactures**: here \( \vee \)I is chosen not because the goal is a disjunction but because a premise's hypothesis is. Say that its converse needs the next section's rule (`exm-cases-on-hypothesis` there).
- **How this looks in a mathematical proof: `exm-product-zero` (new).** The school theorem "if \( a = 0 \) or \( b = 0 \), then \( ab = 0 \)" (taken as known). Prove: for real \( a \), \( b \), if \( a = 0 \), then \( ab = 0 \). The one-line proof "we found \( a = 0 \), so \( a = 0 \) or \( b = 0 \), and therefore \( ab = 0 \)" is \( \vee \)I then \( \to \)E, inside a \( \to \)I box; write it as a derivation with the theorem as premise. Second instance: \( x > 0 \) gives \( x \ge 0 \) (the hook) as \( \vee \)I, with "\( x \ge 0 \)" unfolded to \( (x > 0) \vee (x = 0) \).
- **Warnings.** (1) \( \vee \)I runs one way: from \( P \vee Q \) no rule gives \( P \) ("in particular" belongs to \( \wedge \)E, not to "or"); the failing case: every real \( x \) satisfies \( x \ge 0 \) or \( x < 0 \), and "in particular \( x \ge 0 \)" fails at \( x = -1 \). (2) Choosing a side too early: in (c), writing \( R \) as the goal's side is a dead end — the box would need \( R \), which nothing supplies. (3) Exclusive reading: \( \vee \)I at \( P \) gives \( P \vee Q \) even when \( Q \) is also true (inclusive "or", §01).
- **Quick check.** Which of these follow from \( x = 2 \) by one application of \( \vee \)I? "\( x = 2 \) or \( x = 3 \)"; "\( x = 3 \) or \( x = 2 \)"; "\( x = 2 \) or \( 1 = 0 \)"; "\( x \ge 2 \)" (only after unfolding \( \ge \) as \( (x > 2) \vee (x = 2) \), and then it is the second form). The third is a correct, silly, true statement.
- **What the rules sound like.** \( \vee \)I "So \( P \) or \( Q \) holds." (the S8 review's suggestion: a phrase distinct from \( \wedge \)E's "in particular") / `left` (or `Or.inl hp`), `right`.
