**Purpose.**
- Chapter 1 gave the rules for \( \wedge \), \( \to \), \( \vee \) and the box. This chapter completes the propositional calculus: the rules for \( \neg \) and \( \bot \), derived rules, \( \leftrightarrow \), the one classical rule RAA, and, at the end, a **method for finding derivations** that systematizes the goal-directed and premise-directed reasoning which Chapter 1 and §§01–05 here introduce one move at a time.
- The reader leaves able to (i) check any propositional Fitch derivation line by line; (ii) find a derivation of a short sequent by the search method, knowing when to reach for RAA; (iii) say which results were derived with RAA (that some of them **cannot** be derived without it is proved in Chapter 3 only for \( \neg\neg P \vdash P \) and excluded middle, and is otherwise stated without proof), and why the three classical principles stand or fall together; (iv) recognise in a mathematical proof the rule behind "contradiction", "this case cannot occur", "we prove the contrapositive" and "suppose not".
- Every rule is **defined by its Fitch schema** (an `nd` block with metavariables φ, ψ, χ, placed **inside** the definition block), followed by a clause-by-clause reading in prose. Every section has 3–5 worked derivations of increasing difficulty, each with an **Idea** that says *why* each rule was chosen, and a subsection "How this rule looks in a mathematical proof" with one example about numbers.
- Truth tables are **not** in this chapter (decisions 2). The chapter ends on the question Chapter 3 answers: *we show that something is derivable by exhibiting a derivation; how could we ever show that something is not?*

**Signature moves** (`index.md`, "Named moves"), continuing Chapter 1's:
- **Goal \( \bot \): find a clash.** Look for \( \varphi \) and \( \neg\varphi \) among the available lines, or make \( \varphi \) a subgoal for some \( \neg\varphi \) you have (§01).
- **An impossible case closes itself.** In a case split, a case that reaches \( \bot \) ends by \( \bot \)E in the current goal (§01).
- **To prove "not \( \varphi \)", assume \( \varphi \).** The box opens with the unnegated statement (§02).
- **Use a derived rule as one line.** A proved sequent is a new rule (§03).
- **Two boxes for an iff** (§04).
- **When stuck, assume the negation of the goal** (RAA), and hunt for a clash (§05).
- **Work from both ends:** the full method (§06).

## 01-contradiction-and-vacuous-truth.md — Contradiction: Using a Negation

*Migrates:* from the draft §04: the \( \neg \)E and \( \bot \)E clauses of `def-rules-negation` (now in the new `def-rules-contradiction`), the "clash from a school fact" paragraph (\( a \ge b \) is the negation of \( a < b \); \( a \ne b \) is \( \neg(a = b) \)), `prp-ex-falso`, `prp-vacuous-implication` and the subsection "Vacuous truth" (with "a promise never tested is never broken"), the facts (E1)–(E3) and `thm-square-even-implies-even` (statement kept; **new proof**, below). From the draft §04 A1, the matching part for \( \neg \)E and \( \bot \)E. The draft's \( \neg \)I material stays for §02; its CP/DS proof of the theorem goes to §03 as `exm-square-even-via-contrapositive`.

**The one new thing:** using a negation. Two rules, \( \neg \)E and \( \bot \)E, which together say what a clash is and what it buys.

- **Opening frame.** Chapter 1's rules never mention "not". Yet proofs are full of "this is impossible", "this case cannot occur", "contradiction". This section gives the two rules that **use** a negation and a contradiction; the rule that **proves** a negation is the next section's.
- **Motivation.** A case split with one absurd case: "\( n \) is even or odd. If \( n \) is odd, then \( n^2 \) is odd, which contradicts \( n^2 \) even; so this case cannot occur. Hence \( n \) is even." Chapter 1's \( \vee \)E (`def-rules-disjunction`, cited) needs **both** boxes to end in the goal "\( n \) is even"; the odd box ends in "impossible". What rule turns "impossible" into the goal?
- **Recall** \( \bot \) and \( \neg \) from Chapter 1 §01 (the connectives and their readings, cited).
- **Definition** `def-rules-contradiction` [Rules for Contradiction]. Slogan: *a statement and its negation clash; from a clash, anything follows.* The two schemas, inside the block (checked):

```{=latex}
% schema
\begin{center}
$\begin{nd}[schema]
  \have {i} {\varphi}
  \have {j} {\neg \varphi}
  \have {k} {\bot} \ne{i,j}
\end{nd}$
\qquad
$\begin{nd}[schema]
  \have {i} {\bot}
  \have {k} {\varphi} \be{i}
\end{nd}$
\end{center}
```

  One sentence each. \( \rulename{\neg E} \) (**not-elimination**): if \( \varphi \) and \( \neg\varphi \) stand on lines available at line \( k \), write \( \bot \), citing both, the unnegated line first. \( \rulename{\bot E} \) (**contradiction-elimination**, *ex falso*): if \( \bot \) stands on an available line, write **any** statement \( \varphi \).
- **Clause-by-clause reading.** \( \neg \)E: the two lines must be *exactly* \( \varphi \) and \( \neg\varphi \), not "something that contradicts \( \varphi \) in spirit" (\( x < 1 \) and \( x > 1 \) are not a \( \neg \)E pair until \( x > 1 \) has been written as \( \neg(x \le 1) \)); both must be available (Chapter 1's scope rule, `def-derivation`, cited); the conclusion is \( \bot \) and nothing else. \( \bot \)E: the conclusion is **any** statement, chosen by the writer; it is the elimination rule of \( \bot \), and \( \bot \) has no introduction rule of its own (it is written by \( \neg \)E). Why \( \bot \)E is honest: a line \( \bot \) can only stand where the premises and open assumptions cannot all hold, so there is no situation in which its conclusion must be checked; Chapter 3's soundness theorem makes this exact (prose pointer).
- **The school facts as clashes** (kept paragraph): for reals, \( a \ge b \) is \( \neg(a < b) \); \( a \ne b \) is \( \neg(a = b) \); "no integer is both even and odd" is \( \neg(E \wedge O) \), so it clashes with a line \( E \wedge O \) built by \( \wedge \)I.
- **Worked derivations** (increasing; every one checked, line counts given):
  1. `exm-first-contradictions`: (a) \( \bot \vdash P \) (2 lines; the degenerate case; book.yaml must-prove); (b) \( P \wedge Q, \ \neg Q \vdash R \) (5 lines: \( \wedge \)E, \( \neg \)E, \( \bot \)E; S2 N4b′; replaces \( P \wedge \neg P \vdash Q \), which is Huth & Ryan's own motivating example for \( \bot \)E). *Idea:* the first premise is a conjunction, so take it apart (premise-directed, Chapter 1 §03); its half \( Q \) and the premise \( \neg Q \) are a \( \neg \)E pair; the goal \( R \) has nothing to do with the premises, so only \( \bot \)E can write it.
  2. `prp-ex-falso`: \( P, \neg P \vdash Q \) (D48, 4 lines). Kept; stated as the proposition §06's table lists.
  3. `prp-vacuous-implication`: \( \neg P \vdash P \to Q \) (D20, 5 lines). *Idea:* the goal is an implication, so Chapter 1's goal-directed move opens a box on \( P \); inside, \( P \) and the premise \( \neg P \) clash; \( \bot \)E writes the box's goal \( Q \). The new move appears here: **when nothing produces the goal, look for a clash.**
  4. `exm-manufactured-clash`: \( \neg(P \vee Q) \vdash P \to R \) (6 lines, scratch N1). *Idea:* inside the box of \( P \) there is no \( \neg P \) to clash with; but the premise is \( \neg(P \vee Q) \), and \( P \vee Q \) is one \( \vee \)I away. The clash is **manufactured**: make the unnegated half a subgoal. (The mirror, \( \neg(P \vee Q) \vdash Q \to R \), is B1.)
  5. `exm-impossible-case`: \( P \vee Q, \ P \to R, \ \neg R \vdash Q \) (9 lines, scratch N2; the second box is a one-line box, as in Chapter 1's `exm-disjunction-first-derivations`, cited). *Idea:* the premise \( P \vee Q \) invites \( \vee \)E with both boxes ending in the current goal \( Q \); the \( Q \) box is its own assumption; the \( P \) box cannot reach \( Q \) honestly, so it reaches a clash (\( R \) by \( \to \)E against \( \neg R \)) and closes by \( \bot \)E. **An impossible case closes itself.** (Do not call it DS here; §03 proves disjunctive syllogism and names it.)

```{=latex}
\begin{center}
$\begin{nd}
  \hypo {1} {P \vee Q}
  \hypo {2} {P \to R}
  \hypo {3} {\neg R}
  \open
  \hypo {4} {P}
  \have {5} {R} \ie{2,4}
  \have {6} {\bot} \ne{5,3}
  \have {7} {Q} \be{6}
  \close
  \open
  \hypo {8} {Q}
  \close
  \have {9} {Q} \oe{1,4-7,8-8}
\end{nd}$
\end{center}
```

- **How this rule looks in a mathematical proof:** `thm-square-even-implies-even` (kept statement: for an integer \( n \), if \( n^2 \) is even then \( n \) is even), with (E1) every integer is even or odd, (E2) no integer is both, (E3) the square of an odd integer is odd (with its computation \( (2k+1)^2 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1 \) and the "some \( k \)" pointer to Chapter 4). **New proof, no CP, no DS, no \( \neg \)I:** suppose \( n^2 \) is even [assumption for \( \to \)I]; \( n \) is even or odd [(E1)]; if \( n \) is even, done; if \( n \) is odd, then \( n^2 \) is odd [(E3), \( \to \)E], so \( n^2 \) is even and odd [\( \wedge \)I], contradicting (E2) [\( \neg \)E], so this case cannot occur and \( n \) is even [\( \bot \)E]; in both cases \( n \) is even [\( \vee \)E]; this proves the implication [\( \to \)I]. Print its skeleton as a 12-line derivation with letters (\( E \): \( n \) even, \( O \): \( n \) odd, \( S \): \( n^2 \) even, \( T \): \( n^2 \) odd; premises \( E \vee O \), \( \neg(S \wedge T) \), \( O \to T \); scratch N10, checked) and a line-to-sentence table as Chapter 1 does. Idea: "the hypothesis hands us nothing to compute with; 'odd' does; so split on (E1) and show the odd case impossible". Aftermath: §03 re-reads this proof with derived rules (`exm-square-even-via-contrapositive`), and §02 uses the theorem twice for \( \sqrt 2 \).

## 03-derived-rules.md — Derived Rules: Proved Sequents as New Rules

*Migrates:* from the draft §04: "The contrapositive, and the first derived rule" (`prp-contrapositive`, `prp-modus-tollens` proved from the primitive rules, `def-derived-rule` with the S5-A iteration clause, the CP route to MT, `exm-modus-tollens-instance`), "Disjunctive syllogism: the impossible case" (`prp-disjunctive-syllogism`), the draft's CP/DS proof of `thm-square-even-implies-even` and the paragraph after it (every negation introduced, none removed), now `exm-square-even-via-contrapositive`; exercises B1 (the other disjunctive syllogism, with its part on \( x^2 = 1 \)) and B2 (proof ordering). From the draft §07 A1(a), the S5-B-corrected remark on "Suppose \( x \ge 0 \)" inside a frame, as a warning here.

**The one new thing:** a derived rule, that is, what it means to use a proved sequent as one line.

- **Opening frame.** §01 closed an impossible case by hand; §02 proved negations by hand. The same few patterns recur (the impossible case of `exm-impossible-case`; the box of the contrapositive). Mathematicians name them ("by contraposition", "since \( P \) fails, \( Q \)"). This section proves three such patterns once and then lets each be a single line.
- **Motivation.** Write out a proof that "if \( n^2 \) is odd then \( n \) is odd" with primitive rules only, and the same box appears twice. A mathematician writes "by the contrapositive". What licenses that?
- **First, from primitives:** `prp-modus-tollens`: \( P \to Q, \ \neg Q \vdash \neg P \) (D15, 6 lines, one \( \neg \)I box). **Order matters (S2 #19):** MT is proved from the primitive rules **before** `def-derived-rule`; `prp-contrapositive` (\( P \to Q \vdash \neg Q \to \neg P \), D14, 7 lines, \( \to \)I around \( \neg \)I; say "uses only the rules so far", not "no RAA", which is §05's vocabulary, S2 #25) may be proved before or after the definition, but it is used as a one-line rule only after it.
- **Definition** `def-derived-rule` [Derived Rule] (kept; a minor notion, explain template steps 4, 7, 8): a sequent \( \varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n \vdash \psi \) already proved may be used as one line: if lines \( i_1, \dots, i_n \) available at line \( k \) hold instances of \( \varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n \), write the same instance of \( \psi \), citing the result and the lines. Schema inside the block, with MT as the instance (checked):

```{=latex}
% schema
\begin{center}
$\begin{nd}[schema]
  \have {i} {\varphi \to \psi}
  \have {j} {\neg \psi}
  \have {k} {\neg \varphi} \mt{i,j}
\end{nd}$
\end{center}
```

  The paraphrase (kept, NOTATION wording): such a line abbreviates pasting in the derivation, with its letters replaced by the statements on the cited lines and its premises replaced by repetitions of those lines; the pasted copy's boxes close before its last line. **Kept S5-A clause:** if the copy itself uses derived rules, expand those lines in the same way; this stops, because each derivation cites only results proved before it. The letters of a proved sequent may be replaced by **any** statements (Chapter 1's `def-rule-schema`, cited: a proved sequent is a schema); Chapter 3 makes this exact (substitution, prose pointer).
- **Clause-by-clause reading:** "already proved" (earlier in the book, never inside its own proof); "available lines" (the scope rule still governs); "the same instance" (each letter replaced by one statement throughout).
- **Worked derivations** (increasing):
  1. MT from primitives (D15), then MT by CP and \( \to \)E (D15b, 4 lines) as the **first use** of a derived rule; the kept observation that lines 3–6 of MT are, line for line, lines 3–6 of CP.
  2. `prp-disjunctive-syllogism`: \( P \vee Q, \neg P \vdash Q \) (D17a, 7 lines): §01's impossible case, now filed as the rule DS. *Idea:* it is `exm-impossible-case` with the clash \( P \to R, \neg R \) replaced by the clash \( P, \neg P \); compare the two derivations line by line.
  3. `exm-derived-rules-in-use`: (a) \( P \to Q, \ Q \to R, \ \neg R \vdash \neg P \) by MT twice (5 lines, scratch N6); (b) \( P \vee Q, \ \neg P, \ Q \to R \vdash R \) by DS, then \( \to \)E (5 lines, scratch N7). *Idea:* a derived rule is a **premise-directed shortcut**: a premise \( \varphi \to \psi \) together with \( \neg\psi \) invites MT; a disjunction with one side refuted invites DS. This is the section's addition to the search thread. Expanded, (a) is 11 lines: each MT line becomes its four lines (box, \( \to \)E, \( \neg \)E, \( \neg \)I), as in D15's lines 3–6 (S2 N6a-expanded, checked). The literal paste of `def-derived-rule` adds R lines for the premises and is longer; say "the natural expansion".
  4. `exm-modus-tollens-instance` (kept). Directly after it, one short paragraph (no new label) **pays the green card** of Chapter 1 §04's `exm-wason-selection` (cited): the claim is \( E \to R \) (\( E \): "this card's number is even", \( R \): "its other side is red"); the green card shows \( \neg R \), so if the claim holds, MT gives \( \neg E \): its number must be odd. - **How this rule looks in a mathematical proof:** `exm-square-even-via-contrapositive` (new label; content from the draft's proof of `thm-square-even-implies-even`): the same theorem proved in the style mathematicians write, **each step tagged with its rule** (this pays book.yaml's must-prove "n² even ⇒ n even (via contrapositive), with each step labelled by its rule"; §01's proof, by cases, is the theorem's proof block), with CP applied to (E3), \( \neg \)I for "\( n^2 \) is not odd", \( \to \)E and DS on (E1), compared with §01's proof: each derived-rule line abbreviates a stretch of §01's derivation (point to the lines). % schema
\begin{center}
$\begin{nd}[schema]
  \open
  \hypo {i} {\varphi}
  \have {} {\vdots}
  \have {j} {\psi}
  \close
  \open
  \hypo {k} {\psi}
