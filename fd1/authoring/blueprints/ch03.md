# Blueprint: Chapter 3 — Truth, Soundness and Completeness

**Directory:** `src/ch03-semantics-soundness-completeness/` (slug `semantics-soundness-completeness`). **Part 0, the third of five logic chapters**, main line. Level reference: Huth & Ryan §1.3–1.5 (formal language, parse trees, semantics, induction, soundness, completeness, normal forms), **depth and coverage only**; no example, formula or exercise of theirs is reused. The soundness proof follows a route of our own (a claim about every line and its context, §05; H&R cut boxes open). The completeness proof does **not**: its key lemma `lem-truth-table-line` is Kalmár's lemma, which is H&R's Proposition 1.38, and assembling the \( 2^n \) lines with LEM is their Step 2. Our presentation differs (signed formulas \( \varphi^{v} \) in one statement instead of two cases; `lem-connective-rows` with only the needed parts as premises; the premises \( \Gamma \) kept, so no reduction to a nested implication; the splitting as a lemma and an induction), and the worked example and exercises are ours; the text must not call the route new (S2 recheck #2). **No Horn clauses, no SAT solvers.**

**Purpose.**
- Chapters 1–2 built a calculus of derivations, and Chapter 2 ended on a question it could not answer: *how can one show that something is not derivable?* This chapter answers it by giving the formulas a **meaning** (valuations, truth tables, semantic entailment \( \Gamma \models \psi \)) and proving that the calculus matches the meaning **exactly**:
  - **Soundness:** \( \Gamma \vdash \psi \Rightarrow \Gamma \models \psi \). The rules never lead from truth to falsehood; so one refuting row proves non-derivability.
  - **Completeness:** \( \Gamma \models \psi \Rightarrow \Gamma \vdash \psi \). Every truth-table consequence has a derivation; so the rules of Chapters 1–2 are *enough*.
- To state and prove these, the chapter makes formulas into mathematical objects (an inductive definition, parse trees, unique readability) and introduces **induction**, just in time: on ℕ, course-of-values, and structural induction on formulas. These are the book's first proofs *about* proofs, and the first long proofs the reader meets: they model the Step-roadmap and Claim structure (STYLE §4) at a level where every step can still be checked mechanically.
- It closes with normal forms (CNF, DNF) and adequate sets of connectives: the constructive content of completeness, and a tool Chapter 4 reuses when negating.
- The pilot's §06 ("truth tables summarize the rules", informal soundness) is absorbed and made exact here. Its central idea survives as the aftermath of completeness: *the truth tables are exactly what the rules prove about each connective* (`lem-connective-rows`).

**Signature moves** (`index.md`):
- **Induction on the shape of a formula.** Prove it for atoms; prove it for each connective, assuming it for the parts.
- **Define by recursion on formulas.** One clause per way of building a formula; unique readability makes it well defined.
- **Refute with one row.** A valuation making every premise true and the conclusion false proves \( \Gamma \nvdash \psi \) (by soundness).
- **Induction on the lines of a derivation.** To prove every derivable sequent has a property, show every rule preserves it.
- **Split on an atom.** \( p \vee \neg p \) and \( \vee \)E: prove the goal once assuming \( p \), once assuming \( \neg p \).

No sets beyond "a finite list of formulas" and "the set of atoms of a formula"; say that Chapter 6 treats sets properly (prose pointer). **No forward citations**; Chapter 7's well-ordering is not used.

## 01-formulas-and-parse-trees.md — Formulas as Objects

- **Hook.** Chapter 1 parsed statements by eye. To prove a theorem about **all** derivations, we must first say exactly what a formula is — the same way that to prove a theorem about all integers we need to know what an integer is. A goal theorem stated first: "every derivable sequent is true in every row" needs "every" to range over a precisely defined collection.
- **Definitions.**
  - The alphabet: atoms, \( \bot \), the connectives, parentheses.
  - `def-formula` (inductive definition, clause by clause): (F1) every atom and \( \bot \) is a formula; (F2) if \( \varphi \) is a formula, so is \( \neg\varphi \); (F3) if \( \varphi, \psi \) are formulas, so are \( (\varphi \wedge \psi) \), \( (\varphi \vee \psi) \), \( (\varphi \to \psi) \), \( (\varphi \leftrightarrow \psi) \); (F4) nothing else. Explain (F4) as "the smallest collection closed under (F1)–(F3)", and its practical meaning: every formula has a **formation sequence**, a finite list in which each entry is an atom, \( \bot \), or built from earlier entries.
  - `def-parse-tree` (TikZ trees; the main connective at the root; leaves are atoms or \( \bot \)). `def-subformula` (the formulas at the nodes). `def-height` (the height of the parse tree; atoms and \( \bot \) have height 1). Say in one sentence that "*the* parse tree" and so the height are well defined only once §02's `thm-unique-readability` shows a formula is built in one way only; §02 then re-obtains the height as a recursive definition (`thm-structural-recursion`). Nothing in §02 before that theorem may use the height.
  - The conventions of Chapter 1 (dropping outer parentheses, precedence, right grouping of \( \to \)) are abbreviations for official formulas; state them as a translation, not as part of the definition.
- **Examples.** Three parse trees, from an atom (degenerate case: a one-node tree) to a formula with every connective; a string that is **not** a formula (\( p \wedge \to q \); \( (p \wedge q \)), with the clause that fails. A formula written in Chapter 1's shorthand and its official form: \( p \to q \to r \) is \( (p \to (q \to r)) \) by right grouping, and its parse tree is drawn beside that of \( ((p \to q) \to r) \) (book.yaml key example; C1 then counts the trees for four atoms).
- **Why this definition.** Fully parenthesized binary clauses are what make parsing unambiguous (the result is §02's `thm-unique-readability`). Show the ambiguity without parentheses: \( p \wedge q \vee r \) has two parse trees and, under a row we exhibit, two different values (forward pointer to §03 for the values; the two trees are enough here).
- **Schemas (one subsection, short).** The \( P, Q \) of Chapters 1–2 were placeholders, and their rules were already stated as schemas with metavariables \( \varphi, \psi, \chi \) (Chapter 1 §02, `def-rule-schema`, cited); a derivation written with them is a pattern whose every instance is a derivation. §04 proves the semantic counterpart; the syntactic statement is obvious rule by rule, since each rule is itself stated for arbitrary formulas — say so, and say it is used in the completeness proof.
- **Warning.** A parse tree is not a truth table: the tree is syntax; values come later. And the height counts nodes on the longest branch, not symbols.
- **Quick checks.** Draw the parse tree of \( (\neg p \vee q) \leftrightarrow \neg(r \wedge p) \) and list its subformulas; what is its height? (Answer: height 4; eight subformulas, \( p \) listed once although it occurs twice. Replaces \( \neg(p \to (q \vee \neg p)) \), a near copy of Huth & Ryan exercise 1.3.4(a).)
- **Exercises.** A1: formula or not (five strings), with the failing clause; height and subformulas. B1: parse trees for three formulas from Chapter 1's derivations. B2: translate two official formulas to shorthand and back. C1: how many parse trees does \( p \to q \to r \to s \) have if we forget the grouping convention? (5; list them; count via the Catalan recursion as a remark only.)

## 02-induction.md — Induction on Numbers and on Formulas

- **Hook.** We want to show that *every* formula has some property, but there are infinitely many. The formation sequence suggests the answer: prove it for the starting pieces and show each building step preserves it. This is induction, and it is the tool for everything in this chapter.
- **The principle.** `ax-induction`: if a property holds for 1, and whenever it holds for \( n \) it holds for \( n + 1 \), then it holds for every \( n \in \nN \). Explain its status (a basic property of ℕ taken as given; the optional ZF chapter proves it; Chapter 7 proves it equivalent to well-ordering — prose pointers). Paraphrase: the domino picture, and why it is **not** an infinite derivation (it is one rule licensing one conclusion).
- **First induction proof, worked in full:** \( 1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 3 + \dots + n(n + 1) = \tfrac{n(n + 1)(n + 2)}{3} \) (base \( 2 = 2 \); step: add \( (n + 1)(n + 2) \)). (Not \( 1 + 3 + \dots + (2n - 1) = n^2 \), which is Huth & Ryan exercise 1.4.7(a).) (Logic-flavoured examples wait until structural induction is available.) Show the base case, the inductive step and the conclusion, each labelled, and the rule-level reading: the step is a \( \to \)I box ("assume it for \( n \)").
- **`thm-strong-induction`** (course-of-values): if, for every \( n \), the property holding for all \( k < n \) implies it holds for \( n \), then it holds for all \( n \). Proof from `ax-induction` applied to "holds for every \( k \le n \)". Say why "no separate base case" is not a gap: for \( n = 1 \) there is no \( k < 1 \), so "the property holds for all \( k < 1 \)" is vacuously true (each instance "\( k < 1 \Rightarrow \dots \)" is a vacuous implication, `prp-vacuous-implication`; the rule for "every" itself is Chapter 4's, prose pointer), and the hypothesis hands us \( n = 1 \) outright.
- **`thm-structural-induction`:** if a property holds for every atom and for \( \bot \), and is preserved by each formation clause (F2), (F3), then every formula has it. Proof: given a formula, take a formation sequence ending in it (`def-formula`, (F4)); by strong induction on \( i \), the \( i \)-th entry has the property, since it is an atom or \( \bot \), or is built by (F2) or (F3) from **earlier** entries. (Not by induction on the height: see Induction, just in time.)
- **`lem-parenthesis-count`** (first structural induction, worked in full): (a) every formula has equally many left and right parentheses; (b) every initial segment of a formula has at least as many left as right parentheses, and **strictly** more if the segment is proper and non-empty and the formula has the form \( (\varphi \circ \psi) \); (c) every occurrence of a binary connective in a formula is preceded (within the formula) by strictly more left than right parentheses. Proof by structural induction. (S2 recheck #9: (a) is Huth & Ryan's Theorem 1.31, also their first structural induction, and the non-strict half of (b) is their exercise 1.4.7(f); these are standard facts every treatment proves, but H&R prove (a) by course-of-values induction on the **height**, which is circular here; write the proof by `thm-structural-induction`, lead with (b) and (c), which unique readability needs, and do not copy their layout.) (S2: the strict clause alone is not inductive: for \( (\neg(p \wedge q) \wedge r) \) the step needs the inequality for the segment \( \neg(p \) of the \( \neg \)-formula, which the strict clause does not cover; and the depth-one argument below needs (c).)
- **`thm-unique-readability`:** every formula that is not an atom or \( \bot \) is of exactly one of the forms \( \neg\varphi \), \( (\varphi \circ \psi) \), with \( \circ \) and the immediate parts **uniquely** determined. Proof from `lem-parenthesis-count` (the main connective is the unique binary connective at parenthesis depth one: an occurrence inside \( \varphi \) or \( \psi \) has depth at least 2 by (c) plus the outer parenthesis; so \( (\varphi \circ \psi) = (\varphi' \circ' \psi') \) forces the two main connectives to the same position, hence \( \varphi = \varphi' \), \( \circ = \circ' \), \( \psi = \psi' \)). Keep the proof to about 12 lines.
- **`thm-structural-recursion`:** clauses giving a value for atoms and \( \bot \) and a rule for each connective define **exactly one** function on all formulas. Proof: strong induction on the number of symbols (the immediate parts are shorter), with unique readability supplying the uniqueness of the decomposition. Examples of recursive definitions: height, the set of atoms, the number of connectives.
- **Warnings.** The inductive step must work for **every** \( n \), including the first transition (a pointer: Chapter 7 has the famous failure at \( 1 \to 2 \); here give a small one of our own, not the all-equal/horses argument, which is Chapter 7's find-the-error item: "\( 2^n \ge n^2 \) for every \( n \)", with base \( n = 1 \) and the step \( 2^{n+1} = 2 \cdot 2^n \ge 2n^2 \ge (n + 1)^2 \), whose last inequality holds only for \( n \ge 3 \); the claim is false at \( n = 3 \) (\( 8 < 9 \)) and true again from \( n = 4 \) — keep it to four sentences). Structural induction must cover **every** clause, including \( \bot \) and \( \neg \).
- **Quick checks.** In the proof of `thm-strong-induction`, what is the property to which `ax-induction` is applied? Which clause of `def-formula` makes the \( \neg \) case necessary?
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1:** base case and step for three statements; which induction (ordinary, strong, structural) fits each.
  - **B1:** \( 2^n \ge n + 1 \) for \( n \in \nN \). **B2:** the number of subformulas of \( \varphi \) is at most the number of symbols of \( \varphi \) (structural). **B3:** every formula of height \( h \) has at most \( 2^{h} - 1 \) nodes in its parse tree.
  - **C1:** prove that a proper initial segment of a formula is never a formula (using `lem-parenthesis-count`), and deduce the uniqueness half of `thm-unique-readability` in a second way. **C2 (find the error, true conclusion):** the even-parentheses "proof" by strong induction on the number of symbols (see Scaffolding): atoms and \( \bot \) have none; for \( \neg\varphi \) remove the \( \neg \) and apply the hypothesis to \( \varphi \) (correct); for \( (\varphi \circ \psi) \) remove the **outer pair of parentheses** and apply the hypothesis to the shorter string \( \varphi \circ \psi \) (the error). The solution names that line (the hypothesis is about formulas, and \( \varphi \circ \psi \) is not one: \( p \wedge q \) from \( (p \wedge q) \)), gives a counterexample to the step (the same move applied to the property "no binary connective lies outside all parentheses", true of every formula, would assert it of \( p \wedge q \), where it is false), and repairs the proof by applying the hypothesis to \( \varphi \) and \( \psi \), or by structural induction. (S2: the earlier version, "remove the last symbol", admits no plausible one-error proof: after a final \( ) \) the count would be odd.)
  - **C3 [C3: Exit check: structural induction]** (book.yaml exit check 1, literal): prove by structural induction that every formula built from atoms with \( \neg \) and \( \wedge \) only has more nodes in its parse tree than occurrences of \( \neg \). The item gives the two counts by recursion (`thm-structural-recursion`): \( \mathrm{nodes}(p) = 1 \), \( \mathrm{nodes}(\neg\varphi) = \mathrm{nodes}(\varphi) + 1 \), \( \mathrm{nodes}(\varphi \wedge \psi) = \mathrm{nodes}(\varphi) + \mathrm{nodes}(\psi) + 1 \), and likewise \( \mathrm{neg} \) (0, \( +1 \), sum). Answer: atoms \( 1 > 0 \); \( \neg \): both sides grow by 1; \( \wedge \): \( \mathrm{nodes}(\varphi) + \mathrm{nodes}(\psi) + 1 > \mathrm{neg}(\varphi) + \mathrm{neg}(\psi) \) from both hypotheses (in fact \( \ge \) plus 3). The structural induction is over the restricted clauses (atoms; \( \neg \); \( \wedge \)), which `thm-structural-induction` covers with the property "if built from atoms with \( \neg \), \( \wedge \) only, then …" (say so in the solution: the \( \bot \) and other binary cases hold vacuously). **Marking scheme** (10): the property stated for every such formula, with the recursive counts (2); atom case (1); \( \neg \) case from the hypothesis for \( \varphi \) (2); \( \wedge \) case using the hypotheses for **both** parts (3); the induction principle named, and why the clauses not used are harmless (2). Fatal: applying the hypothesis to the formula being proved; an \( \wedge \) case that uses one part only and says "similarly".

## 03-valuations-and-truth-tables.md — Valuations and Truth Tables

*Migrates:* pilot §06 "Reading a table off the rules", `def-truth-table`, `exm-truth-table-modus-ponens`; pilot §06 exercises A1 and C2 ("the rows of implication are forced").

- **Hook (kept in spirit).** The pilot read each table off the rules; keep that subsection first, as the motivation for the clauses: \( \wedge \)I and \( \wedge \)E force the \( \wedge \) column, \( \bot \)E and `prp-vacuous-implication` force the vacuous rows of \( \to \).
- **Definitions.** `def-valuation`: an assignment of \( \mathrm{T} \) or \( \mathrm{F} \) to each atom. `def-truth-value`: the recursive extension \( v(\varphi) \), one clause per connective, with \( v(\bot) = \mathrm{F} \) — well defined by `thm-structural-recursion` (cite it: this is what §02 was for). `def-truth-table` (kept): the table lists \( v(\varphi) \) for every restriction of \( v \) to the atoms of \( \varphi \) — which is legitimate only by the next lemma.
- **`lem-coincidence`:** if \( v \) and \( w \) agree on \( \mathrm{atoms}(\varphi) \), then \( v(\varphi) = w(\varphi) \). Structural induction, **all cases written** (faded triple, full version). Consequence: a formula with \( n \) atoms has a table of \( 2^n \) rows, and that table determines its value under every valuation.
- **Worked examples.** `exm-truth-table-modus-ponens` (kept); a table built column by column for \( (p \to q) \leftrightarrow (\neg q \to \neg p) \); a formula with three atoms (8 rows) where a shortcut (find the only row that can make it F) saves work — the "work backwards from F" technique, named.
- **Warning.** A truth table checks propositional form only; it cannot see inside an atom ("every \( \varepsilon \) has an \( N \)" is not a row) — kept from the pilot; leads to Chapter 4.
- **Quick checks.** The value of \( (p \vee q) \to \neg r \) under a given valuation, computed down the parse tree. Why does `def-truth-table` need `lem-coincidence`?
- **Exercises.**
  - **A1:** fill in tables (kept, pilot A1).
  - **B1:** four tables with the "backwards from F" shortcut. **B2 (faded, last step blank):** the count "atom occurrences = binary connectives + 1" for formulas built from atoms with binary connectives only; the \( \to \) case blank; "*Name the move, then complete it.*"

Since \( \models \) arrives only in §04, the item states truth-preservation in rows: for \( \to \)E, "whenever \( \varphi \to \psi \) and \( \varphi \) are T in a row, so is \( \psi \)"; for \( \to \)I, "if every row making the formulas \( \Gamma \) and \( \varphi \) true makes \( \psi \) true, then every row making \( \Gamma \) true makes \( \varphi \to \psi \) true". Intended answer: \( \to \)E forces (T, F) ↦ F; \( \to \)I with \( \Gamma \) empty and \( \varphi = \psi = p \) forces (T, T) ↦ T and (F, F) ↦ T; with \( \Gamma = q \), \( \varphi = p \), \( \psi = q \) it forces (F, T) ↦ T. **C2 (find the error, true conclusion):** the all-T valuation claim (see Scaffolding); the solution names the \( \to \) case, the wrong reason, and a formula (\( p \to q \)) on which the stated reason is false though the conclusion holds.

## 04-entailment-and-equivalence.md — Tautologies, Entailment and Equivalence

*Migrates:* pilot §06 "Tautologies and equivalence" (`def-tautology`, `prp-standard-equivalences`), "Using tables to refute" (`def-valid-argument`, `exm-affirming-the-consequent`); pilot exercises B1–B3, C1 (C1, "two connectives are enough", moves to §07).

- **Definitions.** `def-tautology` (kept: tautology, contradiction), `def-satisfiable` (new; for a formula **and** for a list \( \Gamma \): some valuation makes every member true, since "\( \Gamma \) unsatisfiable" is used below), `def-valid-argument` (kept label): \( \Gamma \models \psi \) iff every valuation making all of \( \Gamma \) true makes \( \psi \) true; \( \models \psi \) iff \( \psi \) is a tautology. `def-semantic-equivalence`: \( \varphi \equiv \psi \) iff \( \varphi \models \psi \) and \( \psi \models \varphi \).
- **Paraphrase and degenerate cases.** \( \Gamma \) unsatisfiable \( \Rightarrow \) \( \Gamma \models \psi \) for every \( \psi \) (the semantic ex falso; compare `prp-ex-falso`); \( \models \psi \) with \( \Gamma \) empty. - **Results.**

## 06-completeness.md — Completeness

- **Hook.** Soundness says the rules prove nothing false. Could they be too weak — a tautology with no derivation? Completeness says no. Frame: the pilot's remark ("the converse also holds, with RAA among the rules") becomes a theorem.
- **Proof route (our presentation; Step roadmap in the Idea of `thm-completeness`).**

## 07-normal-forms.md — Normal Forms and Adequate Connectives

*Migrates:* pilot §06 exercise C1 ("two connectives are enough"), generalized.

- **Hook.** The proof of completeness read a formula off its truth table line by line. Doing the same without derivations gives every formula a standard shape.
- **Definitions.** `def-literal` (an atom or its negation), clause and conjunct (minor), `def-dnf`, `def-cnf`. `def-truth-function` (a table with \( n \) inputs), `def-adequate` (a set of connectives that expresses every truth function).
- **Results.**
  - `thm-dnf`: every formula is equivalent to one in DNF; route: one conjunction of signed atoms per T-row (the \( p^{v} \) of §06), disjoined; a contradiction has no T-row, and its DNF is by convention \( p \wedge \neg p \) for an atom \( p \) of the formula (not \( \bot \): \( \bot \) is not a literal, and `thm-adequate-connectives` reuses this construction inside \( \{\neg, \wedge, \vee\} \); S2 recheck #26); a formula with no atoms (built from \( \bot \) alone) is equivalent to \( p \vee \neg p \) or to \( p \wedge \neg p \). `def-truth-function` takes \( n \ge 1 \) inputs.
