# Negation and Classical Logic

Is there a rational number whose square is \( 2 \)? Is \( x^2 + 1 \) ever \( 0 \) for a real number \( x \)? Is "if \( x^2 < 0 \), then \( x = 7 \)" true? Chapter 1 could prove that something is so. It could not prove that something is **not** so. It read the third sentence only informally, as a promise that is never broken, and no rule of Chapter 1 derives it.

This chapter gives the rules for "not". By its end you will be able to prove that something is not so, for instance that \( \sqrt 2 \) is not a fraction. You will also meet one step of a different kind from the others: "suppose the statement is false, and reach a contradiction". This step is all that **classical logic**, the logic of ordinary mathematics, adds to the other rules. Two questions stay open at the end. How do you find a derivation when none comes to mind? Chapter 3 answers it. And how could you ever show that something is **not** derivable? Chapter 4 answers that.

**What you need.**

- All of Chapter 1: the rules for "and", "implies" and "or".
- Above all, what a derivation is, with its boxes and the rule about which earlier lines a line may use (@def-derivation).
- School algebra about integers and real numbers, used in examples as known facts.
- Steps about "every" and "some": applying a fact about every integer to one integer, or naming the \( k \) in "\( n = 2k + 1 \)". Here they are used as ordinary reasoning, as in school algebra. Chapter 5 gives them their rules.

**Time.** About 30 hours if you read the main text and do every A exercise and one B and one C exercise per section; about 50 hours for everything.

## Roadmap

- **Contradiction: Using a Negation.** The rules for a contradiction: what a clash between a statement and its negation is, what it gives you, why an impossible case closes itself, and why an implication whose hypothesis cannot hold is true.
- **Proving a Negation.** The rule for proving "not", and with it the proof that \( \sqrt 2 \) is irrational, then that \( \sqrt p \) is irrational for every prime \( p \).
- **Derived Rules: Proved Sequents as New Rules.** How a result already proved can be used as one line, three such rules (modus tollens, the contrapositive and disjunctive syllogism), and why this is always safe.
- **Iff, Equivalence, and What a Definition Says.** The rules for "if and only if", statements that say the same thing, why the "if" in a definition means "if and only if", and why no statement says the same as its own negation.
- **Proof by Contradiction and the Classical Rules.** The rule "suppose not, reach a contradiction", the laws it gives (double negation, excluded middle, De Morgan's laws), where it is needed, and why its three forms stand or fall together.
